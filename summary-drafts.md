# Draft summaries for review

These 19 empire profiles have no summary of their own, so their empire pages show the short intro
line from EMPIRE_INFO in shared-data.js instead. The drafts below were written from facts in each
empire's own profile text and have NOT been published.

To publish one: check it, paste it as the summary: field of that empire in empire-profiles.js,
regenerate the empire pages with generator.html, then commit and push.
This file is not deployed to the live site.

## Jolof Empire

The Jolof Empire stands as West Africa's clearest example of a confederal empire, whose negotiated authority over Wolof states such as Waalo, Cayor, and Baol allowed rapid consolidation but left the bourba unable to hold those states together once Atlantic trade gave them wealth of their own.

## Kaabu Empire

The Kaabu Empire shows both the power and the cost of a state built on war and the Atlantic slave trade: its warrior aristocracy dominated Senegambia for centuries, yet the same militarised extraction left it divided and exposed when the Fula states rose against it, ending with the destruction of Kansala in 1867.

## Kanem-Bornu Empire

The Kanem–Bornu Empire stands as one of the longest-lasting states in African history, sustained for over a millennium by the Sayfawa dynasty's control of trans-Saharan trade, its cavalry power, and the Islamic institutions that bound the Lake Chad basin to the wider Muslim world.

## Khazar Khaganate

The Khazar Khaganate's significance lies in its role as the steppe power between Byzantium and the Islamic caliphates, whose control of the Volga trade route, dual kingship, and rare religious pluralism, including its ruling elite's conversion to Judaism, shaped early medieval Eurasia until the Kievan Rus brought it down in the tenth century.

## Khmer Empire

The Khmer Empire stands as one of mainland Southeast Asia's greatest civilisations, whose divine kingship and vast hydraulic network raised Angkor Wat and Angkor Thom, and whose dependence on that network left it vulnerable when climate, politics, and trade turned against it.

## Khwarazmian Empire

The Khwarazmian Empire is one of history's starkest examples of how quickly an empire can be undone: within a few years of reaching its zenith under Ala ad-Din Muhammad II, the execution of Mongol envoys in 1218 brought the invasion that destroyed it by 1221 and opened Central Asia to Mongol dominance.

## Kongo Empire

The Kingdom of Kongo stands among the first sub-Saharan African states to sustain diplomatic relations with a European kingdom, adopting Christianity and Portuguese literacy while its growing entanglement in the Atlantic slave trade fed the internal conflicts that broke it apart after the Battle of Mbwila in 1665.

## Kushite Empire

The Kushite Empire stands as the great Nubian civilisation of the Nile Valley, which ruled Egypt as its Twenty-Fifth Dynasty, developed the Meroitic script and Meroë's iron industry, and linked sub-Saharan Africa to the Mediterranean world for centuries.

## Luba Empire

The Luba Empire stands as one of Central Africa's most influential states, whose model of sacred kingship, held in check by the Bambudye society, was adopted by neighbours such as the Lunda and outlived the civil war and colonial conquest that ended its independence.

## Lunda Empire

The Lunda Empire's legacy lies in how it adapted the Luba model of sacred kingship into a far-reaching tributary state that linked the copper and ivory trade of the Central African interior to wider regional economies, leaving traditions still visible in Zambia, Angola, and the Democratic Republic of Congo.

## Majapahit Empire

The Majapahit Empire stands as the high point of Hindu-Buddhist Java, a maritime power that combined rice-growing wealth with command of the archipelago's sea lanes and left a model of statecraft for the sultanates that succeeded it.

## Mamluk Sultanate

The Mamluk Sultanate's enduring significance lies in its transformation of a slave-soldier corps into a durable ruling state, one that halted the Mongols at Ain Jalut, ended the Crusader states, and made Cairo the leading city of the Islamic world until the Ottoman conquest of 1517.

## Maratha Empire

The Maratha Empire stands as the power that broke Mughal supremacy in India, building from Shivaji's hill forts and guerrilla warfare a confederacy that dominated eighteenth-century India until the Third Battle of Panipat and the Anglo-Maratha Wars ended its independence.

## Maurya Empire

The Maurya Empire stands as one of the largest empires in Indian history, whose centralised administration, guided by the Arthashastra, and Ashoka's promotion of dharma and Buddhism left a model of governance and a religious legacy that long outlived the dynasty.

## Median Empire

The Median Empire stands as the bridge between Assyrian and Persian power: it united the tribes of the Zagros, helped bring down Assyria with the fall of Nineveh in 612 BCE, and passed its institutions and nobility intact to the Achaemenid Empire that absorbed it.

## Ming Empire

The Ming Empire stands as the restoration of Han Chinese rule after the Mongol Yuan, whose Confucian bureaucracy, northern defences, and the voyages of Zheng He shaped East Asia long after the dynasty fell to rebellion and the Manchu capture of Beijing in 1644.

## Mitanni Empire

The Mitanni Empire stands as the Late Bronze Age great power that joined Hurrian and Indo-Aryan traditions, spread chariot warfare and treaty diplomacy across the Near East, and left its institutions to the Hittites and Assyrians who absorbed it.

## Mongol Empire

The Mongol Empire stands as the steppe power that, from Genghis Khan's unification of the tribes in 1206, joined China, the Middle East, and Europe in a single network of trade and exchange under the Pax Mongolica, and whose break-up into rival khanates showed the limits of steppe-based rule over vast settled populations.

## Ottoman Empire

The Ottoman Empire stands as one of history's longest-lived empires, which for over six centuries governed a multi-ethnic, multi-religious realm across three continents, and whose dissolution after the First World War left the borders and tensions of the modern Middle East.
