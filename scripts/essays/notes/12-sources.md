## s12-first The First Marks
1. sumerian: Origins (temples and administrators of Uruk, largest city; goods, rations, animals, labour; clay tokens and
   numerical tablets, c. 3200 BCE first tablets combining numbers with signs; most accounts, others word lists to train
   scribes; signs for words and things; rebus ti arrow / ti life; extended to names, grammar, any sentence); Timeline
   c. 2600 BCE earliest literary texts.
2. ancient-egyptian: Origins (apparently independently; bone and ivory labels, tomb U-j at Abydos, c. 3250 BCE, place
   names or quantities; Peribsen seal c. 2700 BCE earliest complete sentence); Script (about two dozen one-consonant
   signs, word signs, determinatives; vowels not written; nfr written nefer by convention).
3. classical-chinese: Origins (oracle bones from c. 1250 BCE near Anyang; questions about harvests, weather, war,
   hunting, childbirth; cattle shoulder blades and turtle shells, heat, cracks; mature system, no earlier stage firmly
   identified).
4. ancient-greek: Origins (Linear B tablets Knossos c. 1400 BCE, Pylos, Mycenae; inventories of land, livestock,
   textiles, weapons, offerings; after fall of palaces c. 1200 BCE writing disappeared for some four centuries except
   Cyprus); DYK (unbaked clay meant to be reused, baked by the fires). The last sentence is the essay's reading.
5. sanskrit: Origins (Rigveda 1,028 hymns, c. 1500–1200 BCE, learned by heart, repeating words in different orders);
   Script (Sanskrit inscriptions from about the 1st century BCE). pali: Literature (Mahāvaṃsa: written down 1st century
   BCE because famine, war and the rival Abhayagiri Vihāra threatened the Sangha). quechua: Facts/Origins (khipus,
   knotted cords; census, tribute, calendar records); Facts earliest records (Domingo de Santo Tomás, Dominican, 1560).
   nahuatl: Script (pictorial codices, glyphs read by their sounds as in a rebus). hawaiian: Origins (no writing apart
   from petroglyphs; stories and hula); Facts (Cook's arrival 1778, words noted). guarani: Facts/Origins (Alonso de
   Aragona, Jesuit, teaching grammar completed 1629).

## s12-scripts Scripts That Travelled
1. ancient-egyptian: Legacy (Semitic speakers in Egypt and Sinai, early 2nd millennium BCE; ox's head; Proto-Sinaitic
   ancestor of Phoenician and through it Greek, Latin, Hebrew, Arabic; letter A from a hieroglyph). phoenician: Facts
   and Origins (Proto-Canaanite until mid-11th century BCE, first attested on inscribed bronze arrowheads); Script (left
   even long vowels unwritten). Entry phoenician-alphabet (22 consonant letters).
2. ancient-greek: Origins (8th century BCE; Phoenician letters for consonants Greek lacked used for vowels; aleph →
   alpha, yod → iota; first full alphabet); Legacy (through the Etruscans the Latin alphabet; Cyrillic around 900 largely
   from Greek; inspired Coptic, Gothic, Armenian). latin: Legacy (Latin alphabet now the most widely used writing system).
   ancient-egyptian: Script (Coptic in Greek alphabet with extra letters from demotic; for the first time the vowels were
   written); coptic: Facts (literary Coptic from the 3rd century).
3. aramaic: Origins (written quickly with ink on leather and papyrus, easier than cuneiform); Script (square script after
   the exile = Hebrew alphabet; Nabataeans of Petra → Arabic script; Edessa → Syriac; Middle Persian, Parthian, Sogdian;
   through Sogdian Old Uyghur, Mongolian, Manchu; Kharosthi). old-turkic: Script (many scholars believe its letters were
   developed from the Sogdian alphabet).
4. sanskrit: Script (Ashoka's edicts in Brahmi and Kharosthi; no script of its own; many scripts derived from Brahmi;
   Siddham carried Buddhist mantras to China and Japan; Devanagari usual since the 19th century). Scribe sanskrit.json
   Brahmi "about": "nearly every script of South and Southeast Asia comes from it". tamil: Facts/Origins (Tamil-Brahmi,
   c. 300 BCE). malay: Facts/Origins (Kedukan Bukit, near Palembang, 1 May 683, Pallava variety of the Grantha alphabet).
   pali: Facts and Script (no script of its own; Sinhala, Mon-Burmese, Khmer, Thai since 1893, Lao since 1930).
5. arabic: Script (28 letters); Origins (from Nabataean; dated inscriptions 512, 528, 568, in the century before Islam);
   Change (adapted for Persian, Urdu, Pashto, Kurdish, Uyghur, Ottoman Turkish, Malay, Swahili, Hausa). persian: Script
   (four extra letters p, ch, zh, g). malay: Script and Literature (Jawi; Terengganu Inscription Stone c. 1303 oldest
   evidence of Jawi). geez: Facts/Script (script developed for the language, abugida; 4th-century inscriptions); Legacy
   (script outlived the spoken language; Amharic, Tigrinya, most other languages of Ethiopia).
6. persian: Origins (Old Persian cuneiform, some three dozen signs, probably created at the order of Darius I). gothic:
   Facts/Script (alphabet most likely invented by Ulfilas for his Bible). old-church-slavonic: Origins (Cyril and
   Methodius, Glagolitic, 862/863, mission to Great Moravia). armenian: Facts/Timeline (Mesrop Mashtots, 405, 36 letters).
   classical-chinese: Timeline (Sejong promulgates Hangul 1446). hawaiian: Literature (first thing printed in 1822 was
   the alphabet).
7. akkadian: Script (LUGAL read šarrum). sumerian: Legacy (cuneiform used for more than a dozen languages).
   middle-persian: DYK and Script (about a thousand Aramaeograms in Book Pahlavi; LY read man). classical-chinese: Glance
   (each country's own pronunciation) and DYK ("brush talk"). mongolian: Glance (written in more scripts than almost any
   other language); Facts (Uyghur script adopted by Genghis Khan 1204; Cyrillic mandatory from 1941). Last sentence is
   the essay's reading.

## s12-carried Carried by Empires, Faiths and Trade
1. akkadian: Timeline (Sargon c. 2300 BCE, language of empire); Spread (international language of the Near East in the
   second half of the 2nd millennium; Amarna letters found 1887, pharaohs with kings of Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni, the
   Hittites, rulers of Canaan, almost all in Akkadian; rulers who spoke Egyptian, Hittite, Hurrian or Canaanite
   languages corresponded in Akkadian).
2. aramaic: Origins ("their language conquered the conquerors"; Assyrians deported hundreds of thousands; by the 8th
   century officials used Aramaic beside Akkadian; reliefs with pairs of scribes); Spread (Achaemenid Imperial Aramaic
   main language of administration from Egypt to Bactria; Ashoka at Kandahar); Timeline (Elephantine papyri of the Jewish
   garrison, 5th century BCE). persian: Origins (Old Persian almost only for royal monuments; Behistun trilingual).
   Profiles linked: /empire/assyrian-empire, /empire/achaemenid-empire (checked in shared-data.js).
3. ancient-greek: Spread (Alexander; government, trade and culture from Egypt to Afghanistan; Koine replaced the old
   dialects). latin: Origins (spread with colonies, roads, armies; replaced Oscan, Umbrian, Etruscan); Spread (in the east
   Greek remained the common language; Roman officials in the east often used Greek). persian: Glance (reborn after the
   Arab conquest in Arabic letters); Spread (from the 11th century Ghaznavids, Seljuks, Ilkhanids, Timurids, mostly Turkic
   and Mongol; Akbar 1582); DYK (official language of the courts of British India until 1837). classical-chinese: Spread
   (examinations 605–1905 on the classics; Korea, Japan and Vietnam used it for government for more than a thousand years;
   "also united a country" for "held its empire together in writing").
4. nahuatl: Origins (Triple Alliance 1428; government, tribute, trade); Spread (Philip II 1570, general language for
   instructing the Indigenous peoples of New Spain). quechua: Origins (Inca resettling communities, teaching the sons of
   conquered rulers in Cusco); Spread (Third Council of Lima 1582–1583 standard Quechua; 1584 trilingual catechism first
   book printed in South America). swahili: Spread (Germans, British, Belgians used it for administration). malay: Spread
   (Dutch and British used Malay as a tool of centralisation and modernisation). Profile linked: /empire/inca-empire.
5. arabic: Spread (Qur'an made Arabic the language of a new religion; Abd al-Malik around 700, language of government in
   place of Greek and Persian). pali: Spread (royal courts of Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia wrote to each other in
   Pali; in Cambodia Pali replaced Sanskrit as prestige language in the 13th century). sanskrit: Spread (inscriptions from
   Afghanistan to Java, Cambodia, Vietnam; "Historians have called this world a 'Sanskrit cosmopolis'"). aramaic: Spread
   (Church of the East missionaries; Xi'an stone 781 in Chinese and Syriac). gothic, coptic (Glance: Christian from its
   first literary texts), armenian (Facts: 5th-century Bible the oldest surviving text), georgian (Origins: written after
   the Christianisation of Iberia in the mid-4th century), geez (Glance/Literature: Bible of 81 books, mostly from Greek;
   only complete Book of Enoch), hawaiian (Literature: Bible), guarani (Literature: Jesuit books).
6. phoenician: Glance and Spread (lingua franca of the maritime Iron Age Mediterranean through Tyro-Sidonian trade and
   colonies; Punic spoken in Carthage). tamil: Spread (lingua franca of early maritime traders; Tamil inscriptions in
   Brahmi on trade goods in Thailand and Egypt). malay: Spread (Srivijaya spread Old Malay, lingua franca of traders);
   DYK/Literature (Sultan Abu Hayat of Ternate to the king of Portugal around 1521–1522; Ternate language unrelated).
   swahili: Origins (towns from around the 8th century); Spread (19th-century caravans from Zanzibar to the Great Lakes,
   Lake Tanganyika, the eastern Congo). Word origins: tamil DYK (rice from arici via Hebrew and Greek); nahuatl Glance
   (chocolate, tomato); arabic DYK (sugar, coffee); quechua DYK (llama, quinoa). Profile linked: /empire/srivijaya-empire.

## s12-afterlife Outliving the State
1. sumerian: Spread (gave way to Akkadian probably c. 2100–1800 BCE; tablet houses; for the next two thousand years
   ritual, incantation, learning; Ashurbanipal boasted he could read the difficult texts of Sumer). akkadian: Spread
   (after Alexander only among the temple scholars of Babylonia, astronomy, ritual, divination until the 1st century CE).
   ancient-egyptian: Change (Middle Egyptian classical language of religious and monumental inscriptions).
2. latin: Glance (outlived the Roman Empire by more than a thousand years); Spread (Germanic kingdoms continued to govern
   in Latin; no one's mother tongue in the Middle Ages; Church, universities, law, government; Magna Carta 1215; Hungary
   1844; Holy See); Legacy (plant descriptions in Latin until 2012).
3. hebrew: Spread (ceased to be anyone's mother tongue by the 3rd and 4th centuries; sixteen centuries of leshon
   ha-kodesh; prayed, studied, wrote, corresponded). sanskrit: Spread (language of priests, scholars, poets, courts; drama:
   kings and Brahmins Sanskrit, women and most others Prakrits); Legacy (Hindu rituals performed in Sanskrit). coptic:
   Facts/Legacy (no native speakers; liturgical language). geez: Spread (no longer living by the 14th century; official
   written language until the 19th; priests and monks ran traditional education; Pankhurst quotation).
4. old-church-slavonic: Glance/Legacy (place like Latin among the eastern Slavs, closer to everyday speech; diglossia).
   armenian: Glance/Literature (Grabar literary standard 5th–19th centuries). middle-persian: Spread (Zoroastrian priests
   rejected the changes as un-Iranian; by the 9th–11th centuries long ceased to be spoken); Glance (literature mostly
   9th–11th centuries in Book Pahlavi; script's name came to mean the language).
5. sanskrit: Script (Panini, some four thousand rules; fixed Classical Sanskrit, changed little). arabic: Change
   (Classical Arabic changed little in written form over thirteen centuries); Script (Modern Standard Arabic and regional
   varieties, diglossia). classical-chinese: Spread (read aloud, not understood by a listener who had not studied it).
   ancient-greek: Change (Katharevousa until Demotic made official in 1976). ottoman-turkish: Glance/Script (up to 88%
   Arabic and Persian vocabulary; unintelligible to rural Turks); DYK (asel / bal). "A learned language often lasted
   because it did not change" is the essay's reading of these.

## s12-ending How Languages End and Were Read Again
1. akkadian: Change (displaced by Aramaic; by Alexander probably spoken by few if any); Spread (the empires' own
   deportations spread Aramaic). aramaic: Spread (Arabic replaced it after the 7th-century conquests; survived in villages
   of the mountains of northern Iraq, south-eastern Turkey, north-western Iran, a few villages of Syria). phoenician:
   Spread/Change (east died out by the 1st century BCE; Punic until the 6th century CE, perhaps the 9th; kinship with
   Arabic probably eased Arabisation). gothic: Spread/Timeline (defeat by the Franks, elimination in Italy; 589; Walafrid
   Strabo 840–842, Tomis; Mangup graffiti mid-9th century).
2. coptic: Timeline/Spread (Abd al-Malik, Arabic replaces Greek as sole language of administration at the turn of the
   8th century; spoken outside the capital until the 10th century; al-Hakim, as Emile Maher Ishaq writes, forbade it on
   pain of having the tongue removed; majority language until the 10th–12th centuries; Zainiya passive speakers over 50
   in the 1930s); Literature (last literary work late 14th century).
3. latin: Glance/Change (Romance languages, nearly 900 million). old-english: Change (Danelaw, endings lost; Middle
   English). old-norse: Facts/Change (Icelandic, Faroese, Norwegian, Danish, Swedish). middle-persian: Change (evolved into
   New Persian). old-church-slavonic: Change (mid-11th century, recensions of Church Slavonic). Last sentence the essay's.
4. ancient-egyptian: Timeline (last hieroglyphic inscription Philae 394, last demotic 452); Change (Horapollo; error that
   misled scholars for more than a thousand years). akkadian: Change (last datable tablet, almanac from Uruk, 79–80 CE;
   knowledge lost for some seventeen hundred years).
5. phoenician: Legacy (Barthélemy 1758, Cippi of Melqart, Greek–Punic bilingual found in Malta). ancient-egyptian:
   Change (Rosetta Stone found 1799 by French soldiers; Champollion 1822, knew Coptic well; signs for sounds and meanings).
   akkadian: Change (Behistun, Rawlinson's copy, cliff face in western Iran); DYK (1857, four scholars, independent
   translations, accepted as proved). sumerian: Change (Oppert named it 1869). old-turkic: Change (Thomsen, 15 December
   1893, words for "heaven" and "Turk"). ancient-greek: Origins (Ventris 1952).

## s12-revival Silenced and Revived
1. hawaiian: Legacy (Act 57 of 1896, quotation; did not make Hawaiian illegal; children disciplined even on the
   playground; Winona Beamer expelled 1937). guarani: Legacy (prohibited in state schools for over 100 years; populists
   used pride in it). tamil: Spread (in Réunion France forbade it in public). mongolian: Spread (2020 requirement,
   protests quickly suppressed; from September 2023 Mandarin the only language of instruction).
2. hebrew: Legacy (revival unique: no other language that had ceased to be anyone's mother tongue has become the everyday
   language of a nation); Spread (Ben-Yehuda 1881, only Hebrew at home, dictionary; teachers in the settlements, schools;
   Technion 1913; Mandate 1922; some 9 million); DYK (Ben-Zion born 1882, often called the first native speaker); Change
   (milon from mila; Hebrew Language Committee 1890; Academy 1953; maḥshev).
3. hawaiian: Timeline/Legacy (ʻAha Pūnana Leo 1984, elders speaking Hawaiian to children; 1987 after 91 years; 2011
   census 2,000 native among 24,000 fluent; UNESCO critically endangered). guarani: Timeline/Spread/Legacy (1992
   constitution; 28% in 2002, 12% in 2022; Spanish monolingualism did not rise). coptic: Legacy (Cyril VI, second half of
   the 20th century). swahili: Timeline (1967). quechua: Timeline (1975). nahuatl: Timeline (2003). ottoman-turkish:
   Change (1928 Latin alphabet). mongolian: DYK (literacy 17.3% to 73.5%, 1941–1950; Cyrillic closer to the spoken
   language).
4. The essay's conclusion, drawn from: sumerian (tablet houses), latin (universities, Church), hebrew (taught to
   children, prayer), geez (Church and traditional education), classical-chinese (examinations), pali (centres of
   learning, monasteries: the Mahavihara), ancient-greek (Linear B lost with the palaces), hebrew and others (dictionaries,
   academies); hawaiian (1896) and swahili (1967).

   Rigveda kept by memory (sanskrit Facts); Sumerian's and Akkadian's ends are the last cuneiform tablet (their Facts and
   Change). Guarani's span begins with Aragona's grammar of 1629 (guarani Facts); Old Church Slavonic's ends c. 1100 as it
   grew into Church Slavonic (old-church-slavonic Facts/Change), still used in services and chants (Legacy).
3. numbers-12.txt line 4 ("ended: …"), reordered by date. Coptic as Egyptian's last stage: coptic Facts. No daughter
   language: sumerian (isolate, Change), akkadian (no direct descendants), phoenician (Punic Arabised), gothic (no
   descendants), coptic (no daughter language). Later stage takes over: ancient-greek (Medieval and Modern Greek),
   middle-persian (New Persian), old-church-slavonic (Church Slavonic), old-english (Middle English), old-norse
   (Scandinavian languages), ottoman-turkish (modern Turkish, its successor), classical-chinese (Family: ancestor of the
   modern Chinese languages; vernacular replaced it); old-turkic (close to the common ancestor). No count was computed:
   the essay says "many", not a number.
