## s13-solstice The Shortest Day
1. Entry winter-solstice (tilt, shortest day, 21 or 22 December / 20 or 21 June, festivals since prehistory, Newgrange,
   Stonehenge). Profile culture/ainu, beliefs section (Tohohata burial site: all but one of 75 graves face southeast,
   near the point of the midwinter sunrise). "The same attention is recorded far away" is the essay's link.
2. Entry great-frost-of-1709 (coldest European winter of the past 500 years; famine in France estimated 600,000 deaths
   by the end of 1710). Entry little-ice-age (14th to mid-19th centuries; shorter growing seasons, harvest failures,
   famine, epidemics, unrest). Entry river-thames-frost-fairs (Great Frost of 1683–84: Thames completely frozen for two
   months; fairs in some winters; last fair 1814). "The season these rites answered could kill" and "It came within
   the Little Ice Age" (1709 lies inside the entry's c. 1300–1850) are the essay's reading.
3. Entry dongzhi (all of it: 22nd solar term, 21 to 23 December, yin and yang, Zhou, Han official celebration, break
   from work, heaven, ancestors, tangyuan, Korea's Dongji and red beans). Profile religion/chinese-folk-religion,
   beliefs ("shares four core concepts: Tian …; qi …; the veneration of ancestors; and bao ying").
4. Entry yalda-night (countries, the night's customs, Hafez, pomegranates and watermelons, inauspicious in Zoroastrian
   tradition, customs to protect from evil, name borrowed from Syriac-speaking Christians, "birth").
5. Entry lohri (Punjab, passing of the winter solstice and end of winter, bonfires, foods, singing, dancing, Dulla
   Bhatti in Akbar's reign, the night before Makar Sankranti, commonly 13 January). Entry makar-sankranti (Hindu
   observance, typically 14 January, Sagittarius to Capricorn, south to north, Surya, many regional names, fairs, kite
   flying, bonfires, feasts, bathing in sacred rivers in thanks to the Sun). "in the middle of January" summarises the
   two dates.
6. Entry inti-raymi (main religious festival of the Inca Empire, Cusco, June solstice, banned by the Spanish
   authorities in the 16th century, revived 1944, staged each June at Sacsayhuamán). Entry inti (sun god, divine
   ancestor claimed by the Sapa Inca; "held at the winter solstice in June", hence "the Andean midwinter"). Entry
   hanukkah (eight days from 25 Kislev, usually in December; rededication of the Temple in 164 BCE by Judas Maccabeus
   after Antiochus IV Epiphanes desecrated it; one more candle or lamp each night). Profile religion/judaism names
   Hanukkah among the festivals that "remember later deliverances". "Not every feast of the season is a feast of the
   Sun" is the essay's sentence: the Hanukkah entry names no solstice.

## s13-rome Rome's December
1. Entry saturnalia (17 December, extended by the 1st century BCE to 23 December, seven days; sacrifice at the Temple
   of Saturn, public banquet, gift-giving, gambling, masters serving slaves, "King of the Saturnalia"). Entry
   saturn-mythology (god of agriculture and wealth; temple in the Forum housed the state treasury). "an old one and a
   new one" is the essay's framing of Saturnalia and Sol Invictus.
2. Profile religion/roman-religion, community section ("the calendars show roughly forty annual festivals"). Entry
   saturnalia (217 BCE reform after Lake Trasimene, sacrifices in the Greek manner; popular into the 3rd and 4th
   centuries CE; "may have influenced later midwinter customs of western Europe"). Entry battle-of-lake-trasimene
   (Hannibal ambushed a Roman army, 217 BCE).
3. Entry sol-invictus (official sun god of the late Roman Empire; Aurelian revived the cult in 274, chief god, temple
   dedicated 25 December 274; "according to some scholars" the festival Dies Natalis Solis Invicti on that day, the
   winter solstice of the Roman calendar; scholars divided on Syrian god or continuous Roman cult). Day 12-25, 274
   (temple in Rome, "fulfilling a vow made after his campaign against Palmyra and paid for with its spoils").
4. Profile religion/mithraism: beliefs (Mithras looking back towards the sun god Sol), community ("Prayers were
   addressed to the Sun three times a day … whether Mithras's birthday fell on 25 December is disputed"), spread
   (altar on the Esquiline to Sol Invictus Mithras, probably 80–100 CE). Entry sol-invictus (coinage of Constantine I
   until 325/326; last known inscription 387). Entry constantine-i (first emperor to convert to Christianity). Profile
   religion/roman-religion: timeline (380 Nicene Christianity the official state religion under Theodosius I; 391
   Theodosius I ends official support for all other cults) and legacy ("Christian festivals were fitted into the old
   calendar").

## s13-north The Northern Midwinter
1. Entry yule (heathen Germanic peoples; Gothic calendar of c. 350 CE; Bede c. 726, Giuli; Old Norse sources: feasting,
   ceremonial drinking, oath swearing). Profile religion/norse-religion, community (the blót: animals sacrificed, meat
   eaten, toasts drunk to the gods; "Great sacrifices were held at the turn of winter, at midwinter, the feast of Yule,
   and in summer").
2. Entry yule (Hákonar saga góða: Hákon the Good's law setting Yule at Christmas; merged with Christmas during
   Christianisation; Yule and cognates still name Christmas). Entry haakon-the-good (king of Norway from about 934,
   raised as a Christian at the court of Æthelstan of England). Profile religion/norse-religion, summary ("Displaced
   by Christianity between the 10th and 12th centuries"). "Yule was not abolished but moved" is the essay's summary.
3. Entry koliada (all its statements: period from Christmas to Epiphany, rituals some pre-Christian, festival at the
   end of December to honour the sun at the winter solstice, calendae theory, house-to-house custom, koledari).
   Profile religion/slavic-paganism: community ("Rites followed the farming year, from Koliada at Christmastide to …
   Kupala"), spread ("The church co-opted the old faith … Christian feasts fell on pagan dates"), legacy ("Much lived
   on in folk religion: the rites of Koliada …").
4. Song shchedryk (shchedrivka; girls house to house given treats; four-note chant "thought to be of prehistoric
   origin, older than Christianity in Ukraine", a New Year then celebrated in March; with Christianity the New Year
   moved to January). Entry wassail (Old Norse ves heill; drinking formula c. 1140 in Geoffrey of Monmouth;
   house-visiting wassail; "largely displaced by carol singing"). Song here-we-come-a-wassailing (sung while
   wassailing from door to door).
5. Entry saint-lucys-day (13 December; Lucia of Syracuse, martyr under the Diocletianic Persecution; the legend of the
   catacombs and the wreath; date once coincided with the shortest day; festival of light in Scandinavia, Italy and
   Saint Lucia; first modern-day Scandinavian celebration 1928, Stockholm). "by an accident of the calendar" is the
   essay's phrase for "its date at one time coincided with the shortest day".

## s13-christmas How Christmas Took Its Date
1. Entry nativity-of-jesus (Matthew and Luke; Bethlehem, reign of Herod, Mary, conception divinely effected; "The
   gospels state no date"; 6 to 4 BCE from the death of Herod).
2. Entry christmas (Chronograph of 354, the part written in Rome in 336; 25 December the traditional date of the
   winter solstice in the Roman Empire; Eastern churches on 25 December Julian = 7 January). Day 12-25, 336 ("Why it
   was chosen remains debated"). Entry sol-invictus (temple 25 December 274; "according to some scholars"). Entry
   yalda-night (the name borrowed from Syriac-speaking Christians, "birth"). The entries draw no causal line from Sol
   Invictus to Christmas and the essay draws none.
3. Profile religion/christianity (the Christian year marked by Christmas). Entry epiphany-holiday (6 January; "may have
   originated" in the Greek-speaking east as a feast of the baptism; Ammianus Marcellinus, 361; West: the Magi; East:
   baptism in the Jordan). Entry candlemas (2 February, the 40th day; Jerusalem in the 380s; Rome from the mid-7th
   century). Entry advent (existed from about 480, beginning not known; Council of Tours 567, monks to fast every day
   in December until Christmas). Entry twelfth-night-holiday (567, Council of Tours proclaimed the twelve days a
   sacred and festive season).
4. Entry christmas ("Its customs mix pre-Christian, Christian and secular themes"). Entries yule and koliada (as
   above). Entry twelfth-night-holiday (bean and pea in the cake, King and Queen for the night). Song personent-hodie
   (Feast of the Holy Innocents, 28 December; choristers and their "boy bishop" took the places of the senior clergy).
   Song the-holly-and-the-ivy (churchwardens' accounts of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries; holly for Jesus, ivy
   for his mother). The comparison with the Saturnalia's king is the essay's own and is marked as resemblance only.
5. Entry nativity-scene (Francis of Assisi "is said to have staged it at Christmas 1223 in a cave near Greccio";
   "seems to have been enacted rather than sculpted"). Entry christmas-tree (Central Europe, Germany and Livonia; first
   recorded among German Lutherans in the 16th century; Queen Charlotte 1800; spread after Victoria's marriage to
   Albert). Song o-tannenbaum (folk song from the 16th century, the fir green in every season standing for
   faithfulness; Anschütz's words of 1824; Christmas named only in the second verse).
6. Entry christmas (Puritans prohibited the celebration in England 1647–1660). Entry a-christmas-carol (published 19
   December 1843, sold out by Christmas Eve, never out of print). Entry christmas-truce (roughly 100,000 British and
   German troops; unofficial ceasefires along the Western Front; joint burials, carols, football; hostilities continued
   in other sectors; Benedict XV begged for an official truce on 7 December 1914, which both sides refused).

## s13-gifts Gift-Bringers and New Years
1. Entry saturnalia (gift-giving). Entry saint-nicholas (4th-century bishop of Myra in Lycia; wonder-worker, patron of
   sailors and children; relics to Bari in 1087). Song personent-hodie (feast 6 December; the 12th-century song
   "Intonent hodie", the three boys saved from drowning and the three girls from prostitution).
2. Entry sinterklaas (based on Saint Nicholas; steamboat from Spain in mid-November; 5 December in the Netherlands, the
   morning of 6 December in Belgium, Luxembourg, western Germany, northern France). Entry santa-claus (folklore of Saint
   Nicholas, Sinterklaas brought to 17th-century New Amsterdam, merged with Father Christmas; gifts on the night of
   Christmas Eve; name in the US press 1773; the 1823 poem; Thomas Nast from 1863).
3. Entry krampus (Central and Eastern Alpine folklore; horned, hairy, chains and birch rods; night of 5 December;
   pre-Christian roots proposed by some folklorists, considered unlikely by historians, not attested until the 16th
   century). Entry sinterklaas (Zwarte Piet first in print 1850; debate and protest since the 2010s over his blackface
   appearance).
4. Entry ded-moroz (East Slavic; origins predate Christianity as a personification of winter; shaped by literature
   since the 19th century; 1928 ally of the priest and kulak; letter in Pravda 1935; main symbol of the Soviet New
   Year's holiday; presents at New Year with Snegurochka). The first and last sentences are the essay's reading of
   that entry.
5. Entry hogmanay (Scots word for the last day of the old year; etymology obscure; roots "speculated" to reach back to
   the Norse winter solstice and Samhain; first-footing and its gifts). Song auld-lang-syne ("Singing it at Hogmanay …
   quickly became a custom in Scotland"). Entry omisoka (last day of the 12th lunar month until 1873, then 31 December;
   cleaning, debts, noodles, the bell struck for the 108 earthly temptations). Profile religion/shinto, community
   ("Millions of people visit shrines in the first days of the New Year").
6. Entry chinese-new-year (traditional lunisolar calendar; new moon between 21 January and 20 February; records from
   the Warring States period). Entry candlemas (hedgehog's shadow, Groundhog Day). Entry maslenitsa (last week before
   Great Lent; straw effigy burned in a bonfire). Entry kwanzaa (26 December to 1 January; Karenga 1966; African
   harvest festival traditions; meant as an alternative to Christmas, but many who celebrate it also observe
   Christmas). "the season closes by stages" and "has also gone on acquiring feasts" are the essay's.

## s13-song Midwinter in Song
1. Entry christmas-carol (4th-century Rome "may be traced"; 13th-century vernacular songs in France, Germany, Italy;
   manuscript of 1426; Reformation, Luther, congregational singing; printed collections of the 19th century).
2. Song maoz-tzur (sung at Hanukkah after the lights are lit; 13th century, recent research the 12th; tune an
   adaptation of "So weiss ich eins, dass mich erfreut", widespread among German Jews as early as 1450; the first
   melody Luther used for his chorales). Song in-dulci-jubilo (c. 1328, Heinrich Seuse, "thought to"; German and Latin;
   Codex 1305 of about 1400; Klug's Lutheran hymnal 1533). numbers-13.txt 35: the oldest December songs.
3. Song personent-hodie ("probably a parody, in the musical sense" of the Saint Nicholas song; "probably rewritten for
   the Feast of the Holy Innocents"). Song good-king-wenceslas (1853 words; tune of the 13th-century spring carol
   "Tempus adest floridum"; the Oxford Book of Carols editors hoped the tune would be given back to spring). Song
   deck-the-halls (Welsh air "Nos Galan", New Year's Eve; English words 1862). Song coventry-carol ("Although the plays
   were performed in summer"; the Coventry guilds' pageant). Song shchedryk (Wilhousky's English words about bells,
   1936; "closely associated with Christmas in the English-speaking world"; in Ukraine it kept its New Year meaning).
4. Song huron-carol (probably 1642, Jean de Brébeuf, Sainte-Marie among the Hurons, Wendat language, tune "Une Jeune
   Pucelle"). Song silent-night (Christmas Eve 1818, Oberndorf's parish church; the Strassers and Rainers; about 300
   languages). Song es-ist-ein-ros-entsprungen (sung by Catholics and Protestants; Nazi-era rewriting of carols to
   promote ideology and remove references to the Jewish origins of Jesus; "Uns ist ein Licht erstanden", sunlight on
   the Fatherland).
5. numbers-13.txt 36 (White Christmas last of the newest). Song white-christmas (first performed Christmas Day 1941, a
   few weeks after the attack on Pearl Harbor; "written by a Jewish immigrant to the United States, showed that secular
   Christmas songs could succeed commercially"; themes of home and nostalgia). Last sentence: the essay's.

## Doubts for the editor
- Section count: eight h2 including data and reading (the guide's maximum), so the suggested "gift-bringers" and "the
  year's turning" sections are one section, and Asia, Iran and the Andes are in the first.
- Left out for length, though read: The Nutcracker, Boxing Day, Joy to the World, The Twelve Days of Christmas, Tu
  scendi dalle stelle, Gaudete, the Dubrovnik kolenda, the Lahore court, Dollfuss's ban on the Krampus, the
  Ancestral Puebloans' Sun Dagger, the orchard wassail.
- The winter-solstice entry says flatly that Aurelian "made" 25 December the festival of the birthday of Sol Invictus
  in 274; the sol-invictus entry says "according to some scholars". The essay keeps the hedge. The two entries could
  be brought into line.
- The roman-religion profile calls Aurelian's Sol Invictus "Palmyrene"; the entry says scholars are divided on whether
  he was a Syrian god. The essay follows the entry and takes only the Palmyra vow from the day page.
- No entry exists for Aurelian, Francis of Assisi, Stonehenge, Newgrange, Thomas Nast or Benedict XV; they are named
  without links. Theodosius I and Palmyra have entries (theodosius-i, palmyra) and are linked.
- "Haakon the Good … raised as a Christian at an English court" rests on the first lines of his entry (court of
  Æthelstan of England); the yule entry spells him Hákon.
- Essay's own readings, to be judged: "an old one and a new one" (Rome); "Yule was not abolished but moved"; the
  Twelfth-night king and boy bishop "recall" the Saturnalia (said to be resemblance only); "by an accident of the
  calendar" (Lucy); "The gift-bringer of the Soviet winter belonged to the New Year, not to Christmas".
- The table repeats entry text in short form; if the editor prefers no table, the nine names can go into the sentence.
- Body dates the truce paragraph without the 1915 sequel (cut for length).
