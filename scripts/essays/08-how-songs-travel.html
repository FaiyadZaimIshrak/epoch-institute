The Oldest Voices

The oldest song in the Institute's collection is a funeral for a city. The Lament for Ur was composed around 2000 BCE, about the time Ur fell to the Elamites and its Third Dynasty came to an end. In 438 lines the goddess Ningal weeps for her city and recalls how she pleaded with the great gods not to let it be destroyed. It is one of five known Mesopotamian city laments, dirges sung in the voice of the city's goddess by male lamentation-priests, who took on the part of a traditional female mourner and asked that the gods be appeased so that the temples could be restored. The text survives on clay tablets scattered among many collections; Samuel Noah Kramer joined twenty-two fragments into the first complete edition in 1940.

Most of the oldest songs were made for gods. The Hymn to Ninkasi, known from Old Babylonian copies of about 1800 BCE, praises the goddess of beer and brewing. The Hurrian Hymn to Nikkal, from a fourteenth-century BCE stratum of the royal palace at Ugarit, is the oldest surviving substantially complete work of notated music in the world: Hurrian words written in a spiral across the tablet, then musical instructions in Akkadian for a singer with a nine-stringed instrument, most likely a lyre. The script is well understood, but there are at least five rival readings of the notation, each giving entirely different music. The Great Hymn to the Aten, composed in the middle of the fourteenth century BCE and attributed to Akhenaten or his courtiers, was found in the tomb of the courtier Ay.

Egypt's tombs also kept songs about death itself. The Harper's Songs first appear in tomb inscriptions of the Middle Kingdom and were still copied on papyrus in the New Kingdom; most praise the tomb and the life to come, but one doubts that there is an afterlife at all and advises the listener to enjoy life while it lasts, and later songs answered it. The Epic of Gilgamesh, the world's oldest surviving narrative literature, grew from five Sumerian poems about the king of Uruk into an Akkadian epic whose first surviving version dates to the eighteenth century BCE; some of the best copies of its later version come from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. No music for it survives.

The Greeks wrote their music on stone. The Delphic Hymns, two hymns to Apollo carved on the Athenian Treasury at Delphi and found in 1893, were probably written for the Athenian procession to Delphi of 128 BCE, and their composers are known by name. The Seikilos epitaph, a marble pillar from Tralles in western Anatolia of the first or second century CE, preserves the oldest surviving complete musical composition, a short song with signs for the melody above the words; found around 1883 while a railway was being built, it had its base sawn flat by its finder so that his wife could stand flowerpots on it, which destroyed the last line. China's oldest songs reach us through anthologies: Guan ju, the first poem of the Classic of Poetry, dated to the seventh century BCE, which Confucius praised in the Analects, and the Song of the Yue Boatman, said to have been recorded around 528 BCE and preserved by Liu Xiang five centuries later, its original sounds transcribed in 32 Chinese characters. Of the 20 ancient songs in the collection, 16 are sacred. The oldest voices sang for gods, for cities and for the dead, and they reach us by tablet, tomb wall, papyrus, stone and anthology, often with their music lost and sometimes with the language itself only half understood.

From Hymn to Anthem

Across the collection the sacred song gives way to the anthem. Of the songs to 1750, 74 per cent are sacred and 8 per cent anthems, and none is a protest song; from 1750, 9 per cent are sacred, 40 per cent anthems and 17 per cent protest songs. Of the 300 songs, 98 are anthems, 70 sacred, 57 war songs and 41 protest songs. By era the anthems run 0 ancient, 1 medieval, 4 early modern, 10 in the age of revolutions, 30 in the nineteenth century, 35 in the twentieth to 1945 and 18 after the war.

The oldest anthems were songs of a cause before they were songs of a state. The Wilhelmus was written at the start of the Dutch Revolt, between 1568 and 1572, to the tune of a Catholic French song mocking a Huguenot prince, so that a satire of one Protestant prince became a tribute to another; it became the official anthem only in 1932. God Save the King appeared close to its present form in 1744 and spread among supporters of George II during the Jacobite rising of 1745; its status as the British anthem rests on custom, not on any proclamation or act of Parliament. Spain's Marcha Real, first printed in 1761 in a book of fife and drum calls, has never had official words.

The revolutions gave the anthem its modern form, a song of the nation in arms. La Marseillaise was written on the evening of 25 April 1792 in Strasbourg to rally soldiers against the invading armies of the First Coalition, and the National Convention made it France's first national anthem on 14 July 1795; it was banned by Louis XVIII and Charles X and restored for good in 1879. In 1797, with Austria gravely threatened by Revolutionary France, Joseph Haydn wrote Gott erhalte Franz den Kaiser as the Habsburg monarchy's answer to the British anthem. The same summer Józef Wybicki wrote Poland Is Not Yet Lost for the Polish Legions in the French army, two years after the third partition had erased the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth from the map; its idea, new in the age of early nationalism, was that a nation could exist without a state as long as its people lived and fought for it. The Star-Spangled Banner began as a poem written on 14 September 1814, after a night watching the bombardment of Fort McHenry in the War of 1812, fitted to the tune of a London club song.

The nineteenth century holds 30 of the collection's anthems, most written for nations that did not yet have states. The poem of Himnusz was completed in 1823 with a subtitle, it is often argued, meant to pass the Habsburg censors. The Deutschlandlied was written in 1841 as an appeal to the German princes to put a united Germany before their small states, and cost its author his post. The Canto degli Italiani was first performed in December 1847 before some 30,000 patriots in Genoa; the Piedmontese police banned it until March 1848, and when the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in 1861 its republican content did not suit the monarchy. The poem that became the State Anthem of Ukraine was written in Kyiv in 1862, and the same year the head of the gendarmes exiled its author. Hatikvah was written in 1877 and set a decade later, by a settler aged about seventeen or eighteen, to a melody he knew from Romania. The monarchies took the form up through foreign bandmasters: King Kalākaua wrote Hawaiʻi Ponoʻī in 1874 to music by his bandmaster Henri Berger; Japan's Kimigayo, whose words are a poem of about 920, the oldest of any national anthem, received Western harmony from the German Franz Eckert; and Siam's Sansoen Phra Barami was printed in 1888 in an arrangement by the Russian Pyotr Schurovsky.

Beside the anthem grew the protest song and the work song. The protest songs number 41: 4 from the age of revolutions, 9 from the nineteenth century, 17 from the twentieth to 1945 and 11 after the war. Eugène Pottier, a member of the Paris Commune, wrote The Internationale in June 1871, after the French army crushed the Commune; a woodcarver in Lille composed its melody in 1888, and by 1910 it had been sung in 18 languages. The Polish Warszawianka, written between 1879 and 1883, became the anthem of the workers' protests of 1905; Bread and Roses began as a suffrage slogan in 1911 and by the 1930s was sung on picket lines. The work songs are fewest, 14 in all: The Song of the Volga Boatmen, written down at Nizhny Novgorod in 1860 or 1861, whose slow beat let a team of men pull together on the towline; the hammer songs of John Henry, which told what happened to men who worked too fast; and Shosholoza, first sung by Ndebele men travelling by steam train to the mines of South Africa. The protest song did not leave the hymn behind: We Shall Overcome was long thought to descend from a hymn of 1901, Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika was composed in 1897 as a hymn of a single stanza, and John Brown's Body took its tune from a camp-meeting hymn traceable in print to 1806–1808.

Tunes That Branch

Songs seldom travel alone. The collection records 32 links between its songs, from 20 parents, and of its 98 anthems 22 have a named source song. The commonest form of borrowing is the one the Japanese song Hotaru no Hikari illustrates: new words to an existing tune, known as contrafactum.

No tune has branched further than God Save the King. About 140 composers have used it, and the melody served Prussia from 1795 to 1918, the Russian Empire from 1816 to 1833 and Switzerland until 1961. The Prussian Heil dir im Siegerkranz began as a song of 1790 for the king of Denmark and became the royal anthem of the Kingdom of Prussia in 1795; because it praised a Prussian monarch and borrowed a British tune it never became popular across Germany, and after the First World War began a composer set its text to new music to remove the likeness. Haydn heard the British anthem in London in 1794 and was moved to write one for the Habsburg emperor: an offspring by example rather than by tune. The melody of Hawaiʻi Ponoʻī recalls both the British and the Prussian anthems; adopted by the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1876, it became the official anthem of the state of Hawaii in 1967. In Japan the bandmaster John William Fenton noticed in 1869 that the country had no anthem, and the officer who chose the old poem for Kimigayo may have done so for its likeness to the British one. In Siam two former British officers adopted God Save the King as the king's honour music in 1851; when King Chulalongkorn visited Singapore in 1871 and found Siam using the same anthem as Great Britain, which ruled the island, he had a tune of its own chosen. The first five notes of Land of Hope and Glory resemble the anthem's opening in a version of 1745, and the pseudonymous composer of Glory to Hong Kong named it, with The Star-Spangled Banner, the anthem of Russia, the Battle Hymn of the Republic and Vivaldi's Gloria, as a model in 2019.

The anthems of the revolutions bred anthems in turn. Pottier meant The Internationale to be sung to La Marseillaise, and it reputedly was at his burial in 1887; the Canto degli Italiani drew on the French text; and Julián Felipe, commissioned in June 1898 to compose a march for the proclamation of Philippine independence, said he had based Lupang Hinirang on the Marcha Real, the Grand March from Verdi's Aida and La Marseillaise. The Polish mazurka travelled east and south. On 2 November 1834 a Slovak Lutheran minister, dismayed to hear more German than Czech in the streets of Prague, wrote three verses by candlelight to be sung to Dąbrowski's Mazurka; Hey, Slavs became the Pan-Slavic anthem and served Yugoslavia for 48 years, from 1943 to 1992. Scholars think the words of the Ukrainian anthem were influenced by the Polish song, and Hatikvah too is named as inspired by it, though its melody belongs to a family that goes back to an Italian song of about 1600. Haydn's tune passed from one empire to another when the Deutschlandlied was written to it in 1841.

A Scottish song of parting became the sound of East Asian ceremonies. Auld Lang Syne, which Robert Burns wrote in 1788 from an older folk song, has a pentatonic tune that suited the musical traditions of Asia. Hotaru no Hikari set entirely different words to it and appeared in 1881 in a collection for elementary school pupils; it is heard at graduations, in shops that are closing and as the last song of NHK's New Year's Eve programme. In Korea the words of Aegukga were sung to the Scottish tune by dissidents under Japanese rule; the composer Ahn Eak-tai, regretting the foreign tune, wrote a Korean melody in 1935, which South Korea adopted in 1948, while North Korea kept the Scottish tune briefly before adopting a new Aegukka in 1947. In the United States a camp-meeting hymn became John Brown's Body in the first months of the Civil War, its words worked out by Massachusetts militiamen teasing a comrade of that name; in November 1861 Julia Ward Howe, who had heard troops singing it, woke before dawn and wrote the Battle Hymn of the Republic to the same tune, selling it to The Atlantic Monthly for four dollars. The Warszawianka crossed to Spain through a German translation: A las Barricadas was by 1936 the anthem of the anarcho-syndicalist CNT, sung by the Durruti Column as it left Barcelona for the front.

Not every branching was consented to. In 1939 Solomon Linda and the Evening Birds recorded Mbube in Johannesburg in three takes; it sold over 100,000 copies in Africa and earned its author almost nothing, because just after the session he sold his rights for ten shillings. In the United States Pete Seeger misheard the chorus, and the Weavers' "Wimoweh" of 1951 was credited by its publisher to a fictitious name; in 1961 George David Weiss turned Linda's closing improvisation into the main tune of The Lion Sleeps Tonight, which reached No. 1 with no credit to Linda. He died in 1962 aged 53 with about forty dollars in today's money in his bank account. In 2004, backed by the South African government, his family sued Disney over the song's use in The Lion King, and a settlement in 2006 paid royalties from 1987 and named Linda as co-composer. Other families are gentler. Psalm 137, the lament of the Judaean exiles by the rivers of Babylon, inspired the chorus of Hebrew slaves in Verdi's Nabucco of 1842, Va, pensiero, and gives a line to Jerusalem of Gold, first performed on 15 May 1967, three weeks before the Six-Day War made it a morale song. Two Australian songs stand in the data as parent and child, but their pages tell no such story: Botany Bay comes from a London burlesque of 1885, while Waltzing Matilda descends from a Scottish poem, a setting of 1818 and a march that Christina Macpherson heard at the Warrnambool races in 1894 and picked out by ear for Banjo Paterson the next year. The two are joined only by the title of a recording of 2008.

Songs Put to Work

A song becomes an anthem by use before it becomes one by law, and the laws often came late. The Star-Spangled Banner became the national anthem on 3 March 1931, after six unsuccessful bills and a petition with five million signatures. Poland Is Not Yet Lost was adopted in 1927, the Wilhelmus in 1932, Himnusz by the constitution of 1989, the Canto degli Italiani provisionally in 1946 and by law in 2017. Ukraine's parliament adopted its anthem in 1992 and its words only in 2003, by 334 votes of 450. Japan's Act on National Flag and Anthem of 1999 passed, by 403 votes to 86, after a Hiroshima school principal killed himself over a dispute between his school board and his teachers about the flag and anthem. China's March of the Volunteers, written for a film of 1935, became the provisional anthem of the People's Republic in 1949, the official anthem in 1982 and the subject of a National Anthem Law in 2017.

Once adopted, an anthem was taught. Kimigayo was sung at school ceremonies by 1893, and during the Second World War schoolchildren in Japan and its colonies were ordered to sing it and salute the emperor every morning; under a Tokyo directive of 2003 requiring teachers to stand and sing at graduations, 410 teachers and school workers were punished by 2008, and in 2011 the Supreme Court of Japan upheld the requirement. Siam adopted Sansoen Phra Barami for schools in 1914, and it is still played before films in cinemas and at the start and close of broadcasting. The Philippines' Flag and Heraldic Code of 1998 requires that schools see every student learns Lupang Hinirang by heart. The Ode to the Motherland, drafted on a train in 1950, was ordered sung throughout China by Zhou Enlai in 1951 and has opened China National Radio's news ever since. Dictatorships were the most thorough. Under Italian fascism the school day had to open with Giovinezza or the song of the regime's youth organisation, radio closed each day with it, and those who did not join in could be roughed up by the Blackshirts; when Arturo Toscanini refused to conduct it in Bologna in 1931, Blackshirts assaulted him and he left Italy. In Germany a law of 19 May 1933 made the Horst-Wessel-Lied a national symbol, the raised-arm salute was required while its first stanza was sung, and Hitler fixed the tempo at which it had to be played.

The cult of a person could be built in song. The East Is Red began as a peasant love song of the Loess Plateau; its modern words of 1942 idealise Mao Zedong, and when a political commissar proposed teaching it to the army in 1956 the defence minister Peng Dehuai refused and called it a personality cult, an opposition that contributed to his purge in 1959. During the Cultural Revolution, when the author of the official anthem was purged and died in prison, it served as China's unofficial anthem: loudspeakers played it at dawn and dusk, students sang it at the start of the first class each morning, and in 1970 China's first satellite sent its melody back to Earth as its first signal. The Song of General Kim Il Sung, composed in 1946, is the earliest known work of art to mention Kim Il Sung and so marks the beginning of the cult around him; promoted by his son from the early 1980s, it displaced the national anthem at public gatherings, and its words are carved on the Arch of Triumph in Pyongyang. East Germany's Lied der Partei, whose title line held that the party is always right, named Stalin in its chorus until his name was removed in the 1960s; its author told his wife that the song would one day do him great harm. The Partisans' With Marshal Tito of 1943 originally named Tito together with Stalin, and after the split of 1948 that version was no longer performed or printed. The State Anthem of the Soviet Union, whose music Stalin chose himself from more than 200 entries, named him in its verses; after his death it was played without words from 1956 to 1977.

What states adopted, they also banned. The Horst-Wessel-Lied, Germany's second national anthem from 1933 to 1945, has been illegal in Germany and Austria since the regime fell, except for artistic or educational purposes. That regime's ally had itself censored a song: Faccetta nera, written in April 1935 as the Italian army prepared to invade Ethiopia, presented the conquest as the liberation of an enslaved Ethiopian girl and alluded to the concubinage of Italian men with Ethiopian women; from May 1936 the government forbade cohabitation and marriage between Italians and the colonised, and Mussolini, said to have hated the song because it seemed to favour the mixing of races, tried to ban it and, since it was too popular, settled for cutting a verse. The Deutschlandlied's stanzas were rationed by regime: the whole song under the Weimar Republic from 1922, the first stanza followed by the Horst-Wessel-Lied under Nazi Germany, the third alone in reunified Germany from 1991; the first is not illegal, but singing it is taboo. Winston Churchill forbade the BBC to play The Internationale after Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, until the ban was lifted in January 1942. South Africa banned Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika for its link to the African National Congress, and Asimbonanga and Free Nelson Mandela too. In Hong Kong, after the national security law of 2020, the government told schools not to allow Glory to Hong Kong; a court refused an injunction in 2023, citing the chilling effect on freedom of expression, and on 8 May 2024 the Court of Appeal granted one, banning the broadcasting, performing, publishing or distribution of the song, with exceptions for academic and journalistic purposes. Bella ciao was never banned: its partisan words have no documentary trace before 1953, and a former partisan said he never heard it in twenty months of war, yet since the 1960s it has been sung in dozens of languages against fascism and oppression, from Myanmar after the coup of 2021 to Italian trade unionists in 2023.

What a Song Can Do

The clearest record of what a song can do for people without power is the spiritual. Go Down Moses draws on the Book of Exodus, where God sends Moses to demand the release of the Israelites; enslaved people in the United States saw their own enslavement in that story, and because of the song's double meaning many enslavers outlawed it. Its earliest written record comes from 1861, when people who had escaped to the Union lines sang it at Fort Monroe in Virginia, and the authorised biography of Harriet Tubman quotes her as saying she used it as a code song for people escaping slavery. Swing Low, Sweet Chariot was probably composed after 1865 by Wallace Willis, a Choctaw freedman, and made known by the Fisk Jubilee Singers; readings of its Jordan as the first step to freedom cannot be proved, and in 1939 Nazi Germany's Reich Music Examination Office listed it among undesired and harmful works. Wade in the Water is believed, though it has not been proven, to have warned escaping people to go into the water so that dogs could not follow their scent; in the 1960s the Staple Singers' version became part of the civil rights movement.

That movement's own anthem took its modern shape in a strike. Through the winter of 1945–1946, tobacco workers in Charleston, South Carolina, mostly African American women, ended each day's picketing with a slow hymn led by the striker Lucille Simmons; Zilphia Horton of the Highlander Folk School learned it from her, Pete Seeger changed "will" to "shall", and in 1959–1960 the school's young student activists gave We Shall Overcome the words and rhythm known today as they sang it through police raids and spells in jail. Joan Baez led a crowd in it at the March on Washington in 1963, President Johnson used its words before Congress after the attacks on marchers at Selma in 1965, and more than 50,000 people sang it at Martin Luther King Jr.'s funeral. Marchers in Northern Ireland sang it before the shootings of 1972, hundreds of thousands sang it in Wenceslas Square during the Velvet Revolution, and Bengali versions were sung in the Bangladesh war of 1971.

In South Africa the songs of the struggle against apartheid were of several kinds. Senzeni Na?, sung at funerals, demonstrations and in churches at least since the 1950s, asks over and over what wrong its singers have done to be treated so. Shosholoza was sung by prisoners too: Nelson Mandela described singing it while he worked on Robben Island, comparing the struggle to the motion of an oncoming train, and after the Rugby World Cup of 1995 it became a favourite at sporting events, often called the country's second national anthem. Asimbonanga, written in 1986 while Mandela was imprisoned, mourned his absence and named Steve Biko and other activists, which brought its mixed band raids and arrests; in 1999 Mandela joined the band on stage and danced to it. Free Nelson Mandela, recorded in Britain in 1984 when few people outside South Africa knew who Mandela was, was banned in South Africa and got back into the country to be played at football matches and ANC rallies. The oldest of these songs went furthest. Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika, a teacher's hymn of 1897, was the closing anthem of the African National Congress by 1925 and was sung at the meetings of the worldwide anti-apartheid movement; it became the anthem of Tanganyika in a Swahili translation first performed on 8 December 1961, as the Union Jack was lowered for the last time, and of Zambia, Zimbabwe and Namibia at their independence. In 1994 Mandela declared it and the former anthem, Die Stem van Suid-Afrika, national anthems together, and since 1997 a shortened combination of the two has been South Africa's anthem.

Elsewhere a song stood in for a state that did not exist. Ey Reqîb was written in 1938 by the Kurdish poet Dildar in jail, addressing the guards of the prison where he was tortured; in 1946 it became the anthem of the Kurdistan Republic of Mahabad, which lasted a year, and it is today the official anthem of the Kurdistan Region of Iraq. My Homeland, a poem by the Palestinian Ibrahim Tuqan of about 1934, served for much of the century as an unofficial anthem of the Palestinians, and in 2004 became the anthem of Iraq on the order of the head of the Coalition Provisional Authority, after the anthem associated with Saddam Hussein's government had been set aside.

Songs also outlive the states that made them. The music of the Soviet anthem outlasted the Soviet Union: in December 2000 Russia's legislature approved it, with a third set of words by the same poet, as the anthem of Russia, while in Ukraine since 2015 a public performance of the Soviet anthem can carry up to five years in prison. Rise, O Voices of Rhodesia, set in 1974 to Beethoven's Ode to Joy, which the Council of Europe had adopted as the Anthem of Europe in 1972, lost its legal status in December 1979, and because of it the melody remains controversial in Zimbabwe, where a conductor needed long negotiations in the 1980s before he could give the country's first performance of the Ninth Symphony. Democratic Kampuchea's anthem, The Glorious Seventeenth of April, celebrating the day the Khmer Rouge came to power, outlived a regime whose policies killed 1.5 to 2 million people: kept by the coalition government in exile that many Western nations and the United Nations recognised, it went on being presented in the West as Cambodia's anthem until the monarchy was restored in 1993. East Germany's Auferstanden aus Ruinen spoke of a single German fatherland, which sat badly with the state after it built the Berlin Wall in 1961, so that on official occasions only its tune was played. Hey, Slavs served Yugoslavia and then, because Serbia and Montenegro could not agree on new symbols, their state union until 2006. God Save the Tsar!, anthem of the Russian Empire until 1917, lives on in English-language hymnals as the Russian Hymn.

The Lesson for Institutions

The first lesson of these three hundred songs is that a song's meaning belongs to its singers, not to its makers. God Save the King spread among supporters of George II and, by some claims, was first sung for the Jacobite kings; its tune served Prussia, Russia and Siam, and two of them later took pains to be rid of it. A camp-meeting hymn became a soldiers' joke and then a hymn of judgment; a Shaanxi love song became the sound of a personality cult; a Zulu song of a lion hunt became an American hit from which its author earned ten shillings. The institutions that adopted songs seldom controlled what they came to mean: the Deutschlandlied's author meant its first stanza as an appeal to the princes for a united Germany, and critics abroad read it as a claim of supremacy over other peoples; Faccetta nera was written as propaganda for a colonial war and censored by the regime it served.

The second lesson is that adoption and prohibition are weaker than custom. Anthems were sung for generations before they were legislated, and laws about them, from the Philippine code of 1998 to Japan's Act of 1999 and Hong Kong's injunction of 2024, have more often marked a dispute than ended one. Bans failed in a pattern: the Piedmontese police could not stop the Canto degli Italiani, twenty German court sentences between 1873 and 1911 did not silence the Polish mazurka, apartheid South Africa could not keep Free Nelson Mandela off its football grounds, and the Soviet ban on the Ukrainian anthem ended with its singing at a presidential inauguration in 1991. The songs most thoroughly imposed, Giovinezza, the Horst-Wessel-Lied, The East Is Red, are the ones least willingly heard now.

The third lesson concerns what lasts. The songs that crossed the most borders named no party and fixed no enemy: a hymn asking God to bless Africa served four states at their independence and still carries two anthems; a slow hymn of a tobacco workers' strike was sung at the March on Washington, in Northern Ireland and in Prague; a partisan song whose link to the partisans is doubtful could unite the Catholic, Communist, Socialist and Liberal strands of the Italian Resistance because it named none of them. A state that wants its anthem to outlive it should write it as a prayer or a promise rather than a roll of honour, since a name in a chorus is the first thing the next turn of politics removes: Stalin's from the Soviet anthem and the Lied der Partei, Stalin's beside Tito's from the Partisan song, and the verse of the Ode to the Motherland that names Mao is often left out.

What the Data Shows

The Institute's Songs in History holds 300 songs in 85 languages. For 187 the words are printed; 88 are in copyright and are described without them; 265 have a checked performance. By type, 98 are anthems, 70 sacred, 57 war songs, 41 protest songs, 39 love songs, 36 celebrations, 35 laments, 22 ballads, 18 dances, 14 work songs, 6 lullabies and 1 an epic; a song may carry more than one type. By era, 20 are ancient, 26 medieval, 19 early modern, 25 of the age of revolutions, 73 of the nineteenth century, 87 of the twentieth to 1945 and 50 postwar. By region, 120 are European, 47 from the Americas, 35 from Central and East Asia, 25 from South and Southeast Asia, 20 from Sub-Saharan Africa, 19 from Oceania, 17 from the Middle East, 10 from the Ancient Near East and 7 from Egypt and North Africa. The collection is a selection, not a census: its songs were chosen one by one, it counts the songs that states, churches and movements kept rather than the songs people sang most, and its weight towards Europe and the last two centuries reflects what was written down as much as what was sung.

Read against time, the types tell the story of the second section. Sacred songs are 16 of the 20 ancient songs, 21 of 26 medieval and 11 of 19 early modern, then 2 of 25 in the age of revolutions, 11 of 73 in the nineteenth century, 7 of 87 in the twentieth and 2 of 50 postwar: 74 per cent of the songs to 1750, 9 per cent after. Anthems follow the opposite curve, from 1 medieval and 4 early modern to 10, 30, 35 and 18, so that from 1750 they are 40 per cent of the whole. Protest songs appear only with the revolutions, 4, then 9, 17 and 11; war songs peak at 27 in the twentieth century to 1945; work songs cluster in the nineteenth century, 7 of 14. Of the 98 anthems, 22 have a named source song, and the collection records 32 links between its songs from 20 parents, listed below. The links are of several kinds, which the table names: a tune taken whole, a text drawn on, a model named by the composer. Two rows rest on titles alone rather than on any borrowing, and two record a song's inclusion in a stage work and film named after another.
