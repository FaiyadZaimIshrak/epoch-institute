What Does an Empire Live On?

If the first principle of empire is that it must make distance governable, the second is that it must make distance profitable. Governing is expensive: armies must be paid, officials salaried, roads maintained, garrisons supplied, and capitals fed. No empire has ever sustained itself on conquest alone, for conquest is a cost before it is a reward. The territory an empire seizes must be made to yield more than it consumes, and the surplus must be moved from field to granary, from province to capital, and from frontier to treasury reliably enough that the whole apparatus of rule can be financed year after year.

For this reason the economic question bears directly on imperial survival. As well as a system for making distance governable, an empire is a system for converting territory into revenue, and revenue into power, and the two functions cannot be separated. The roads that carried armies also carried grain and taxes, the administrators who enforced the law also assessed the tribute, and the coinage that paid the soldiers also priced the goods that crossed the frontier. Without its economic foundation the political structure is weakened and, beyond that, loses the means by which it sustains itself.

Where the architecture of power describes how empires translate authority across distance, the economy of power describes what that authority feeds upon. Trade routes, currency systems, and agricultural surplus are among the decisive forces of imperial history and should not be treated as its background. Empires rose by controlling them and fell when they lost control of them.

The Logic of the Route

Routes existed before empires did. Rivers, mountain passes, coastlines, and caravan tracks determined where goods could move and at what cost, and the empires that endured were very often those that understood that controlling a route was more valuable than controlling a territory.

The reason is structural. A trade route gives leverage out of all proportion to its size, since a single mountain pass, a strait, a river mouth, or an oasis chain can govern the movement of goods across thousands of miles. Whoever controls such a point can tax everything that passes, and taxation of movement is among the most efficient revenue an empire can collect. It requires neither the occupation of distant lands nor the costly administration of subject peoples, only the ability to hold a chokepoint and levy a toll. The wealth flows to the holder regardless of who produced the goods or who will consume them.

The Achaemenid Persians grasped this early. The Royal Road that ran from Sardis to Susa is often remembered as a marvel of communication, a means of moving messengers and armies faster than had been possible before. It was also an artery of commerce, and the standardized coinage introduced under Darius made the wealth that moved along it legible and taxable. Persia thus monetized the connection between its provinces as well as creating it. The empire's prosperity rested on its position astride the routes that linked the Mediterranean world to the resources of the Iranian plateau and beyond.

Rome built its entire economic life upon the same principle, on the scale of an inland sea. The Mediterranean was the great trade route of the ancient world, and Rome, by ringing it entirely, transformed it into something approaching an internal market. Grain from Egypt and North Africa, olive oil from Iberia, wine, metals, marble, and slaves moved across a sea made safe by Roman fleets and connected by Roman ports. The provisioning of the city of Rome itself, the famous grain supply that fed a population approaching a million, depended on this maritime network operating without interruption. The taxes the commerce generated paid for the security that made the commerce possible, so that the route financed the force that protected it.

The medieval world shows the same thing over a wider area. The empires and states that controlled the overland routes of Central Asia, the maritime passages of the Indian Ocean, and the river systems of the Eurasian interior grew wealthy in proportion to their command of movement. The Mongol Empire, for all its reputation as a machine of destruction, presided over a period in which the overland routes across Asia became more secure and more active than they had been in centuries, and the wealth that flowed from this commerce was a foundation of Mongol power. Control of the route gave control of the wealth, and with the wealth came the means of empire.

The Discipline of the Coin

If routes were the arteries of the imperial economy, currency was its blood. A coinage system is one of the most revealing instruments an empire possesses, because it shows the true condition of the state more honestly than any monument or chronicle. A stable, trusted coin is the sign of a confident and solvent power, and a debased coin is the sign of a state borrowing against its own future.

Currency performs functions that are easy to overlook because they are so basic. It allows taxes to be assessed and collected across distance, soldiers in distant provinces to be paid in a form they trust, and goods to be priced and exchanged among people who share no language and no personal acquaintance. A reliable currency is in this sense a form of administrative reach, extending the authority of the center into every transaction in which the coin is used. The emperor's face on the coin is therefore more than vanity: it declares that the holder of the coin stands within the emperor's economic order.

This is why the debasement of currency recurs so persistently in the histories of declining empires. When an empire's costs outrun its revenues, as wars grow longer, frontiers grow harder to defend, and the administrative class grows more expensive to maintain, the temptation to reduce the precious-metal content of the coinage becomes nearly irresistible. By minting more coins from the same quantity of silver or gold, the state can pay its immediate obligations, but the relief is temporary and the damage is structural. The coin, once trusted, becomes suspect, and prices rise as the market recognizes the dilution. Tax revenues, collected in a depreciating currency, lose value before they can be spent. The instrument on which the empire relied to project its authority into the economy then spreads distrust.

The later Roman Empire is the standard case. The progressive debasement of the silver coinage over the third century, as emperors struggled to fund armies during a period of relentless internal and external crisis, helped produce a spiral of rising prices and collapsing monetary confidence. Attempts to halt the spiral by decree could not restore the simple belief that a Roman coin was worth what it claimed to be. The monetary disorder did not by itself destroy the empire, but it dissolved one of the bonds that had held the imperial economy together, and it signaled to everyone who handled money that the center was no longer fully in command of its own affairs.

The Foundation of the Field

Beneath the routes and the coinage lay the deepest economic foundation, the land and its surplus. Every pre-modern empire was, at bottom, an agrarian state. The great majority of its people worked the soil, and the surplus they produced beyond their own subsistence was the source of everything else: the taxes, the armies, the cities, the administration, and the monuments. Trade and coinage moved wealth and made it usable, but agriculture created it.

The management of agricultural surplus was therefore among the most consequential tasks of imperial government, and the empires that performed it well achieved a durability that those reliant on plunder or trade alone could not match. The great hydraulic civilizations organized their whole administration around the productivity of the land, which meant irrigation, flood control, the timing of the agricultural year, and the systematic assessment and collection of the harvest. The wealth of Egypt was the wealth of the Nile, and the Egyptian state was, in large part, an apparatus for capturing the river's annual gift and converting it into the granaries that fed officials, soldiers, and the institutions of rule.

The Mughal Empire built one of the most developed systems of land-revenue administration in the pre-modern world. Its prosperity rested on the careful assessment of agricultural production across a large and fertile territory, and on a fiscal system designed to channel a share of that production to the imperial center while leaving enough in the countryside to sustain the cultivators who generated it. The fertility of the Gangetic plain and the administrative machinery built to tax it were together the foundation of Mughal power. When that machinery functioned, the empire was among the wealthiest states on earth. When the cost of holding the empire began to outrun what the land could be made to yield, as prolonged warfare in the Deccan strained the treasury, that foundation began to give way.

This points to a vulnerability that agrarian empires could never fully escape. Their wealth was real, but it was inelastic in the short term, since a harvest could not be increased by decree. When the demands of the state, driven by war, by an expanding administration, and by the cost of defending overextended frontiers, grew faster than the surplus the land could provide, the empire faced a choice between two dangerous options. It could tax the cultivators beyond what they could bear and risk rural collapse, or fail to meet its obligations and risk the loyalty of the soldiers and officials on whom its power depended. Many empires, confronted with this choice, did both, which hastened their decline.

When the Economy Fails First

Viewed in this way, a great many imperial collapses have an economic cause beneath their political and military surface. The visible event, whether a lost battle, a deposed ruler, or a sacked capital, is frequently the final consequence of an economic disorder that had been advancing, often unnoticed, for a long time before.

The pattern follows a recognizable sequence. First, the costs of empire rise through a long war, a hardened frontier, or a swollen administrative or military establishment. Then the revenue base, unable to expand as quickly, comes under strain. The state responds with the instruments available to it: heavier taxation, debasement of the currency, the sale of offices, and the alienation of revenues to local powers in exchange for immediate relief. Each of these measures buys short-term solvency at the price of long-term capacity. Heavier taxation exhausts the productive base, debasement corrodes monetary confidence, and the alienation of revenues hands the economic foundations of central power to the regional figures who will later use that wealth to act independently of the center. By the time the visible crisis arrives, the empire has already lost the economic means with which it might have responded.

This is why disruption to an empire's economic networks so often proved fatal out of proportion to its apparent scale. The severing of a trade route, the loss of a grain-producing province, the collapse of a monetary system, or the breakdown of the administrative machinery that assessed and collected the agricultural surplus could each remove a foundation on which the entire structure of rule depended. An empire can survive the loss of a battle if its economy remains intact, for an intact economy can fund the recruitment of new armies and the rebuilding of lost positions. An empire whose economy has already failed may collapse even without a decisive military defeat, because it has lost the means to sustain itself whatever happens on the battlefield.

The economic foundation is thus what gives an empire its resilience, meaning its capacity to absorb shocks and recover from setbacks. An empire whose foundation is sound can withstand a crisis, while one whose foundation has been hollowed out may be ended by any crisis, because it has nothing left in reserve.

The Lesson for Institutions

The lesson for the historian, and for the student of any enduring institution, is that economic foundations are easy to take for granted because they operate quietly when they operate well. A functioning fiscal system, a trusted currency, secure routes of supply, and a productive base capable of generating surplus do not announce themselves. They are noticed chiefly when they fail and disorder follows.

The study of empires tends to be drawn to the dramatic surface of conquests, rulers, and decisive battles. The question that most reliably predicts whether a large-scale order will endure, however, concerns its economy and rarely its conquests. It asks whether the order can pay for what it has built, move resources from where they are produced to where they are needed, and maintain the trust on which its currency and its commerce depend. It asks further whether the order can extract enough surplus to fund itself without exhausting the base that produces the surplus, and whether it can absorb the loss of a route, a province, or a revenue stream without losing the capacity to recover.

Empires that met these tests endured, and those that did not, however dazzling their conquests or charismatic their rulers, failed. The economic architecture of power is no more glamorous than the administrative architecture examined in the first essay of this series. It is hidden in tax registers, mint records, granary accounts, toll schedules, and the ledgers of distant provinces, yet in the long view it is decisive. An empire is sustained less by the wealth it seizes in a season of conquest than by the systems through which it produces, moves, and renews wealth across generations.

The study of these patterns serves a larger purpose than antiquarian curiosity. To understand how empires fed themselves, financed themselves, and eventually failed to do so is to see more clearly the foundations beneath every large and complex order, namely the quiet systems of production, exchange, and revenue without which no structure of power, ancient or modern, has long survived.
