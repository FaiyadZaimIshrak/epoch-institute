The Shortest Day

The winter solstice is the moment when one of Earth's poles reaches its greatest tilt away from the Sun, giving that hemisphere its shortest day and longest night of the year. In the Northern Hemisphere it falls on 21 or 22 December, in the Southern on 20 or 21 June. Since prehistory it has been marked by festivals and rites in many cultures, as the point when daylight begins to increase again, and monuments such as Newgrange in Ireland and Stonehenge in England are aligned with its sunrise or sunset. The same attention is recorded far away: at the Tohohata burial site of the Ainu, all but one of 75 graves face southeast, near the point of the midwinter sunrise.

The season these rites answered could kill. The Great Frost of 1709 was the coldest European winter of the past 500 years, and the famine that followed in France is estimated to have caused 600,000 deaths by the end of 1710. It came within the Little Ice Age, a period of cooler climate between roughly the 14th and mid-19th centuries in which shorter growing seasons and harvest failures contributed to famine, epidemics and unrest. In the Great Frost of 1683–84 the Thames at London was completely frozen for two months, and frost fairs were held on its ice in some winters until 1814.

In China the solstice is a festival in its own right. Dongzhi is the 22nd solar term of the Chinese calendar and the festival observed at that time, around 21 to 23 December. Its origins are traced to the philosophy of yin and yang, the lengthening days representing positive energy. It was first celebrated during the Zhou dynasty and declared an official celebration during the Han dynasty, when people took a break from work, worshipped heaven and honoured their ancestors; the veneration of ancestors is one of the four core concepts that the profile of Chinese folk religion names. Families still gather to eat tangyuan, balls of glutinous rice that symbolise reunion, and in Korea, where the day is called Dongji, porridge is made with red beans.

In Iran and its neighbours the solstice is a night rather than a day. Yalda Night, also called Chilla Night, is celebrated in Iran, Kurdistan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Afghanistan, Azerbaijan and Turkmenistan. On the longest night of the year friends and family gather to eat, drink and read poetry, especially Hafez, late into the night; pomegranates and watermelons are particularly significant. In the tradition of Zoroastrianism the night was inauspicious, and its customs were originally intended to protect people from evil. The name is a loan: Yalda was borrowed from Syriac-speaking Christians, in whose language it means "birth".

In South Asia the turning is marked in the middle of January. Lohri, a midwinter folk and harvest festival of the Punjab, marks the passing of the winter solstice and the end of winter with bonfires, festive foods, singing and dancing; many of its songs tell the legend of Dulla Bhatti, set in the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar. It falls on the night before Makar Sankranti, an observance of Hinduism typically kept on 14 January. That day marks the passage of the Sun from Sagittarius to Capricorn, which coincides with its movement from south to north, and is dedicated to the solar deity Surya; it is observed under many regional names with fairs, kite flying, bonfires, feasts and bathing in sacred rivers in thanks to the Sun.

South of the equator the calendar turns over. Inti Raymi, the Festival of the Sun, was the main religious festival of the Inca Empire, held in Cusco at the June solstice, the Andean midwinter, in honour of Inti, the sun god and the divine ancestor claimed by the Sapa Inca. The Spanish authorities banned it in the 16th century; it was revived as a public spectacle in 1944 and is now staged each June at Sacsayhuamán. Not every feast of the season is a feast of the Sun, however. Hanukkah, the eight-day festival of Judaism that begins on 25 Kislev, usually in December, commemorates an event: the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem in 164 BCE by Judas Maccabeus and his followers, after the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes had desecrated it. It is kept by lighting one more candle or lamp each night.

Rome's December

The Roman midwinter had two feasts, an old one and a new one. The old one was Saturnalia, held in honour of Saturn, a god of agriculture and wealth whose temple in the Roman Forum housed the state treasury. It fell on 17 December and by the 1st century BCE had been extended to 23 December, seven days in all. It opened with a sacrifice at the temple and a public banquet, followed by gift-giving, gambling and a licence in which masters served their slaves at table and a "King of the Saturnalia" presided over the merrymaking.

The festival was one of roughly forty that the calendars of Roman religion show in a year, and it changed with the state's fortunes. In 217 BCE, after Hannibal's victory at the Battle of Lake Trasimene, it was reformed with sacrifices in the Greek manner. It remained popular into the 3rd and 4th centuries CE, and its entry goes no further than to say that it may have influenced later midwinter customs of western Europe.

The new feast belonged to the Sun. Sol Invictus, the "Unconquered Sun", was the official sun god of the late Roman Empire. The emperor Aurelian revived his cult in 274 CE, promoted him as the chief god of the empire and on 25 December of that year dedicated a new temple to him in Rome, fulfilling a vow made after his campaign against Palmyra and paid for with its spoils. In the Roman calendar 25 December was the date of the winter solstice, and according to some scholars Aurelian also instituted on that day the festival of the birthday of the Unconquered Sun, Dies Natalis Solis Invicti. Scholars are likewise divided on whether Sol Invictus was a Syrian sun god distinct from the old Latin Sol or belonged to a single, continuous Roman cult.

The Sun had other worshippers in Rome. In the temples of Mithraism, Mithras was shown looking back towards the sun god Sol as he killed the bull; prayers were addressed to the Sun three times a day, and an altar on the Esquiline to "Sol Invictus Mithras" probably dates from 80–100 CE. Whether Mithras's birthday fell on 25 December is disputed. Sol Invictus appeared on the coinage of Constantine I, the first emperor to convert to Christianity, until 325/326, and the last known inscription referring to him dates to 387. In 380, under Theodosius I, Nicene Christianity became the official religion of the empire, and in 391 he ended official support for all other cults. The old calendar outlasted the old gods: the profile of Roman religion records that Christian festivals were fitted into it.

The Northern Midwinter

North of the empire the season had its own name. Yule was a winter festival and time of the year observed by heathen Germanic peoples. Its earliest record is a Gothic calendar of c. 350 CE, and Bede, writing c. 726, recorded that the heathen Anglo-Saxons called both December and January Giuli. Old Norse sources describe a festival in which feasting, ceremonial drinking and oath swearing were central, and the profile of Norse religion places it among the great sacrifices of the year, held at the turn of winter, at midwinter and in summer, at which animals were killed, the meat eaten and toasts drunk to the gods.

Yule was not abolished but moved. According to Hákonar saga góða, Haakon the Good, a king of Norway raised as a Christian at an English court, passed a law that set Yule at the time when Christians celebrated Christmas. Norse religion was displaced by Christianity between the 10th and 12th centuries; the festival merged with Christmas during Christianisation, and Yule and its cognates still name Christmas in English and other Northern European languages.

Among the Slavs the name is Koliada, the traditional word for the period from Christmas to Epiphany or, more generally, for Christmas-related rituals, some of which date to pre-Christian times. It denotes a festival held at the end of December to honour the sun at the winter solstice, and one theory derives the word from the ancient calendae. The profile of Slavic paganism records that the old rites followed the farming year from Koliada at Christmastide onwards, that the church co-opted the old faith so that Christian feasts fell on pagan dates, and that the rites of Koliada lived on in folk religion. In several Slavic languages the word now means the custom of going from house to house on Christmas Eve, when groups of singers called koledari sing carols and receive gifts in return.

The same visit is made in Ukraine and in England. Shchedryk is a shchedrivka, a song sung to wish a household well at the New Year, often by girls who went from house to house and were given treats. The four-note chant at its heart is thought to be older than Christianity in Ukraine and to have belonged to a New Year then celebrated in March; with Christianity the New Year moved to January, and the song moved with it. The English custom is the wassail, whose name was borrowed from the Old Norse salutation ves heill, "be in good health", and is first recorded as a drinking formula around 1140, in Geoffrey of Monmouth. In the house-visiting wassail people go from door to door singing and offering a drink from the wassail bowl in exchange for gifts, as in Here We Come A-wassailing. The house-visiting custom has been largely displaced by carol singing.

A Christian feast came to stand for the solstice in the north by an accident of the calendar. Saint Lucy's Day, 13 December, commemorates Lucia of Syracuse, an early-fourth-century martyr of the persecution under Diocletian, who according to legend brought food to Christians hiding in the Roman catacombs wearing a candle-lit wreath on her head to light her way. Because its date at one time coincided with the shortest day of the year, the feast is now widely celebrated as a festival of light, most widely in Scandinavia, Italy and Saint Lucia. The first modern-day Scandinavian celebration was held in Stockholm in 1928.

How Christmas Took Its Date

The gospels give the birth of Jesus no date. The Nativity is told by Matthew and Luke, who agree that he was born in Bethlehem in the reign of King Herod, to Mary, and that the conception was divinely effected. Reckoning from the death of Herod would place the birth between 6 and 4 BCE.

The earliest document to place the birth on 25 December is the Chronograph of 354, in a part generally agreed to have been written in Rome in 336. The day was the traditional date of the winter solstice in the Roman Empire, and it was the day on which Aurelian had dedicated his temple to the Unconquered Sun in 274. Why it was chosen remains debated, and the entries for Christmas and Sol Invictus do not settle it: the first records the coincidence with the solstice, the second attributes a solar festival on that day only to "some scholars". What the record does show is how the date travelled. Part of the Eastern churches keep the feast on 25 December of the Julian calendar, which currently corresponds to 7 January, and the Iranian solstice night bears a name borrowed from Syriac-speaking Christians, whose word for birth it is.

Around the day the churches of Christianity built a season. Epiphany, on 6 January, may have originated in the Greek-speaking east of the empire as a feast of the baptism of Jesus; the earliest reference to it as a Christian feast, by Ammianus Marcellinus, dates from 361, and Western Christianity principally commemorates the visit of the Magi, Eastern Christians the baptism in the River Jordan. Candlemas, on 2 February, the fortieth day, was recorded in Jerusalem in the 380s and introduced to Rome from the mid-7th century. Advent, the season of waiting, existed from about 480, though when it began is not known. In 567 the Council of Tours ordered monks to fast every day in December until Christmas and proclaimed the twelve days from Christmas to Epiphany a sacred and festive season.

The season then filled with customs, and the entry for Christmas says of them only that they mix pre-Christian, Christian and secular themes. Some entries record a meeting of old and new directly: Yule merged with Christmas, and Koliada kept rituals older than the church. Others record resemblance without descent. On Twelfth Night a bean and a pea were hidden in a cake, and the man and the lady who found them became King and Queen for the night; on the Feast of the Holy Innocents, 28 December, the choristers and their "boy bishop" traditionally took the places of the senior clergy in the choir stalls. Both recall the licence of the Saturnalia and its festival king, but the pages claim no line of descent between them. Holly and ivy are recorded regularly as church decorations for Advent and Christmas in churchwardens' accounts of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and in the carol The Holly and the Ivy the holly stands for Jesus and the ivy for his mother.

The furniture of the modern feast is later still, and most of it can be dated. The first nativity scene, which seems to have been enacted rather than sculpted, is attributed to Francis of Assisi, who is said to have staged it at Christmas 1223 in a cave near Greccio. The Christmas tree developed in Central Europe, particularly Germany and Livonia, and was first recorded among German Lutherans in the 16th century; in Britain the German-born Queen Charlotte introduced a tree in 1800, and the custom spread after the marriage of Queen Victoria to Prince Albert. The song that now belongs to the tree shows how such things were assembled: O Tannenbaum rests on a folk song known from the 16th century in which the fir, green in every season, stood for faithfulness; its words of 1824 name Christmas only in the second verse.

The feast was also resisted and remade. The Puritans prohibited its celebration in England from 1647 to 1660. Charles Dickens published A Christmas Carol on 19 December 1843; the first edition sold out by Christmas Eve, and the book has never been out of print. The day's hold was shown most plainly in the First World War. In the Christmas truce of 1914, roughly 100,000 British and German troops took part in unofficial ceasefires along the Western Front, with joint burial ceremonies, carols and games of football in some sectors while hostilities continued in others. Both sides had refused the official truce for which Pope Benedict XV had begged on 7 December.

Gift-Bringers and New Years

Gifts were given at the Saturnalia, as they are at many of the feasts described here, but the figure who brings them has a traceable history of his own. Saint Nicholas was a 4th-century bishop of Myra in Lycia, venerated across Eastern and Western Christianity as a wonder-worker and patron of sailors and children. In 1087 merchants from Bari carried his relics to their city. His feast is 6 December, and a 12th-century Latin song in his honour told of the three boys and the three girls he saved, the boys from drowning and the girls from prostitution.

From the saint came a line of figures, each made from the last. Sinterklaas, the Dutch figure based on him, traditionally arrives by steamboat from Spain in mid-November; gifts are given on 5 December in the Netherlands and on the morning of 6 December in Belgium, Luxembourg, western Germany and northern France. Settlers brought his legend to 17th-century New Amsterdam, and from it, from the folklore of Saint Nicholas and from the English Father Christmas came Santa Claus, who is said to bring gifts on the night of Christmas Eve. The name was first used in the United States press in 1773; the 1823 poem "A Visit from St. Nicholas", with its sleigh pulled by flying reindeer, laid the foundation for the modern image, which the cartoonist Thomas Nast helped to define from 1863.

The saint's companions have histories less settled. The Krampus of Central and Eastern Alpine folklore, hairy, horned and carrying chains and birch rods, is said to accompany Saint Nicholas on the night of 5 December and punish badly behaved children. Some folklorists have proposed pre-Christian roots for him, but historians generally consider this unlikely, as the figure is not attested until the 16th century. Sinterklaas's helper Zwarte Piet, who first appeared in print in 1850, has been the subject of debate and protest since the 2010s over his blackface appearance.

In the east the gift-bringer was detached from the church altogether. Ded Moroz, Father Frost, is a figure of East Slavic tradition whose origins predate Christianity as a Slavic personification of winter, and whose legend has been shaped by literature since the 19th century. In 1928, in the Soviet Union, he was declared an ally of the priest and the kulak; the traditions were revived after a letter published in Pravda in 1935, and he became the main symbol of the Soviet New Year's holiday, bringing presents at New Year with his granddaughter Snegurochka. The gift-bringer of the Soviet winter belonged to the New Year, not to Christmas.

The turn of the year is the other great midwinter observance, and it need not fall in midwinter. Hogmanay is the Scots word for the last day of the old year; its etymology is obscure, and its roots are speculated to reach back to the Norse celebration of the winter solstice and the Gaelic Samhain. Its most widespread custom is first-footing, in which the first person to cross a threshold after midnight brings symbolic gifts such as coal, shortbread, whisky and black bun, and it is the occasion on which Auld Lang Syne came to be sung. In Japan, Ōmisoka was held on the final day of the 12th lunar month until the country switched to the Gregorian calendar in 1873, when it was set as 31 December. Houses are cleaned, debts repaid, long noodles eaten for the crossing from one year to the next, and at midnight most Buddhist temples strike a large bell 108 times; millions then visit the shrines of Shinto in the first days of the New Year.

Chinese New Year is still set by the traditional lunisolar calendar. It falls on the new moon between 21 January and 20 February, and records of its first celebration can be traced to the Warring States period. In Europe the season closes by stages, at Twelfth Night, at Candlemas, where a German saying about a hedgehog's shadow was the origin of Groundhog Day, and at Maslenitsa, the Eastern Slavic week before Great Lent, in which a straw effigy is burned in a bonfire. The season has also gone on acquiring feasts. Kwanzaa, held from 26 December to 1 January, was created in 1966 by the activist Maulana Karenga, drawing on his research into African harvest festival traditions; he at first meant it as an alternative to Christmas, but many African Americans who celebrate it also observe Christmas.

Midwinter in Song

The season is kept in song as much as in ritual. The first known Christmas hymns may be traced to 4th-century Rome, and by the 13th century popular Christmas songs in vernacular languages had developed in France, Germany and particularly Italy; the earliest English examples of the Christmas carol appear in a manuscript of 1426. Carols grew in popularity after the Protestant Reformation, when reformers such as Martin Luther promoted congregational singing, and the printed collections of the 19th century widened their appeal.

The oldest winter songs in the Institute's collection show the faiths sharing tunes. Ma'oz Tzur, the Hebrew poem sung at Hanukkah after the lights are lit, is commonly dated to the 13th century, though recent research points to the 12th. Its best-known tune was identified as an adaptation of an old German folk song that was widespread among German Jews as early as 1450, and the same folk melody was the first that Luther used for his chorales. In dulci jubilo, thought to have been written around 1328 by the mystic Heinrich Seuse, alternates medieval German with Latin; its tune, first found in a manuscript of about 1400, was printed in a Lutheran hymnal in 1533.

Many carols are older songs put to new use. Personent hodie is probably a reworking of the 12th-century song for Saint Nicholas, and was probably rewritten for the Feast of the Holy Innocents. Good King Wenceslas set a poem of 1853 to the tune of a 13th-century spring carol; its critics hoped the tune would be given back to spring. Deck the Halls put English words of 1862 to the Welsh air "Nos Galan", a song of New Year's Eve. The Coventry Carol comes from a guild play performed in summer. Shchedryk was given English words about ringing bells in 1936 and became closely associated with Christmas in the English-speaking world, while in Ukraine it kept its New Year meaning.

Carols crossed between peoples as the feast did. The Huron Carol was written, probably in 1642, by the Jesuit Jean de Brébeuf at Sainte-Marie among the Hurons, in the language of the Huron-Wendat people and to the tune of a French folk song. Silent Night, first performed in the parish church of Oberndorf on Christmas Eve 1818, was carried abroad by travelling families of folk singers and has been translated into about 300 languages. Songs could also be taken over. In Nazi Germany many carols were rewritten to promote the regime's ideology and to remove references to the Jewish origins of Jesus; Es ist ein Ros entsprungen, a carol that Catholics and Protestants had both sung, became a secular text of sunlight falling on the Fatherland.

The newest song of the season in the collection is a secular one. White Christmas was first performed on Christmas Day 1941, a few weeks after the attack on Pearl Harbor. Writers on popular music see it as a turning point: the song, written by a Jewish immigrant to the United States, showed that secular Christmas songs could succeed commercially and established the themes of home and nostalgia that run through them. Like the feast it belongs to, the repertory has gone on taking in what each age brought to it.

What the Data Shows

When this essay was written, in November 2026, the Institute's month page for December listed 25 festivals and observances, from Advent to Ōmisoka. January listed five (Epiphany, Twelfth Night, Lohri, Makar Sankranti and Chinese New Year), February three (Candlemas, Chinese New Year and Maslenitsa), October three and November five. Most of the December entries carry no date of their own, because a custom seldom has a first year: only Saint Nicholas, dated to the 4th century, the Nativity, c. 6–4 BCE, and Kwanzaa, 1966, are dated. Nine entries name the winter solstice in their text, and the table sets out what each says of it.

The day pages hold 3,679 events in all, of which 300 fall in December; January has the most, 364, and November the fewest, 279. The page for 25 December lists 10 events, 5 births and 8 deaths. Its events show how the date has been used: Aurelian's temple in 274, the earliest recorded Christmas in 336, the coronation of Charlemagne as emperor in 800 and of William the Conqueror as king of England in 1066. The pages for the other days of this essay are of much the same size: 13 December has 10 events, 6 births and 8 deaths; 17 December, the first day of the Saturnalia, 9, 5 and 8; 21 December 10, 7 and 5; 22 December 8, 7 and 3; 31 December 11, 6 and 8; and 6 January 9, 7 and 7. None of the events on 21 or 22 December concerns the solstice. The month and its feasts are gathered on the Institute's page for December and on the page for 25 December.

Songs in History lists 30 songs for December; the words of 28 are printed, and all 30 have a performance. By language, 18 are English, 5 Latin, 4 German, and one each Spanish, Wendat, Italian, Welsh, French, Ukrainian, Hebrew and Scots; a song may carry more than one language. By the year they are sorted under, 3 date from before 1500, 8 from 1500–1699, 7 from 1700–1799, 9 from 1800–1899 and 3 from 1900 on. The oldest are Ma'oz Tzur (13th century, or between 1160 and 1190), In dulci jubilo (words c. 1328), Personent hodie (usually dated from a manuscript of 1360) and the Wexford Carol (probably 15th or 16th century); the newest are Shchedryk, first performed on 29 December 1916, We Wish You a Merry Christmas, whose famous version was published in 1935, and White Christmas. These lists are selections, not censuses. The festivals were chosen one by one, feasts that move through the year with a lunar calendar, such as Passover and Ramadan, are left off the month pages, and a list in which 18 of 30 songs are English measures what was printed and recorded in that language as much as what was sung in the dark of the year.
