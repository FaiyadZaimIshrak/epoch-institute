== story
"Scarborough Fair" (Roud 12, Child 2) is a traditional ballad of northern England. Its words have much in common with the Scottish ballad "The Elfin Knight", which Francis James Child collected as his second ballad and which has been traced back to 1670; in it an elf threatens to carry off a young woman unless she performs an impossible task, and she answers with impossible tasks of her own. Dozens of versions existed by the end of the 18th century. Many name other places, such as Wittingham Fair or Cape Ann, or no place at all, under titles like "The Lovers' Tasks" or "My Father Gave Me an Acre of Land". The mention of the fair at Scarborough and the herb refrain belong to 19th-century versions, and may have been borrowed from "Riddles Wisely Expounded" (Child 1). The herb refrain may also have grown out of an older refrain by mishearing.

The Scarborough or Whittingham Fair form was most common in Yorkshire and Northumbria, where it was sung to several tunes, often in the Dorian mode. Frank Kidson printed a version in Traditional Tunes (1891), saying he had collected it at Whitby; his words are the best-known text. The tune best known today came from Mark Anderson (1874–1953), a retired lead miner of Middleton-in-Teesdale in County Durham, who sang it to Ewan MacColl in 1947. No recording of Anderson's version was made. MacColl printed it in a book of Teesdale folk songs and recorded it with Peggy Seeger. In the United States John Lomax recorded a related version, "The Cambric Shirt", from Georgia Ann Griffin in Florida in 1939, and other field recordings followed in Arkansas (1958), New York (1966) and Bristol (1976).

The first commercial recording was by Gordon Heath and Lee Payant, Americans who ran a Paris nightclub, on an album of 1955; A. L. Lloyd and MacColl recorded it with Kidson's tune in 1956, and Audrey Coppard was the first to record Anderson's tune the same year. In the English folk revival Martin Carthy learned it from MacColl and Seeger's songbook and recorded it on his debut album in 1965. Before that, Bob Dylan had borrowed the melody and some lines from Carthy's arrangement for "Girl from the North Country" (1963).

In London in 1965 Paul Simon learned the song from Carthy. Simon & Garfunkel set it in counterpoint with "Canticle", a reworking of Simon's anti-war song "The Side of a Hill", and released "Scarborough Fair/Canticle" in 1966; it reached a wider public on the soundtrack of The Graduate in 1968. The copyright named only Simon and Garfunkel. Carthy felt the traditional source should have been credited, and although the duo later paid his publisher a settlement, Carthy himself received nothing. The rift lasted until Simon and Carthy sang it together at the Hammersmith Apollo in October 2000. The ballad has since been recorded by singers and bands from folk to metal, and used in films, television and a video game.
== meaning
A singer sends a message to a former lover at the fair: if she will perform a list of impossible tasks, such as making a shirt without a seam, he will take her back. In answer, she sets him impossible tasks of her own. Beneath the riddling play the song speaks of love that has been lost and cannot be won back.
