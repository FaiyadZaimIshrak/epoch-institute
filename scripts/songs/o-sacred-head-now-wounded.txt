== story
"O Sacred Head, Now Wounded" is a Christian Passion hymn based on a long medieval Latin poem, "Salve mundi salutare", whose sections address in turn the parts of Christ's body hanging on the Cross. Its last part, from which the hymn is taken, addresses Christ's head and begins "Salve caput cruentatum". The poem was long attributed to Bernard of Clairvaux (1091–1153) and is now attributed to the medieval poet Arnulf of Leuven (died 1250). Dieterich Buxtehude chose stanzas from its seven cantos for the text of his Membra Jesu Nostri.

The Lutheran hymnist Paul Gerhardt (1607–1676) translated the whole poem into German, reworking it as a more personal contemplation of Christ's death on the cross. It first appeared in Johann Crüger's hymnal Praxis pietatis melica in 1656, and its closing section, beginning "O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden", has become a hymn in its own right.

Its music had a secular start. Hans Leo Hassler wrote the tune around 1600 for a love song, "Mein G'müt ist mir verwirret", printed in his Lustgarten Neuer Teutscher Gesäng in 1601; in 1656 Crüger took it for Gerhardt's hymn and simplified its rhythm. Johann Sebastian Bach arranged the melody and used five stanzas of the hymn in four settings in his St Matthew Passion. He also used it in his cantatas Sehet, wir gehn hinauf gen Jerusalem, BWV 159, and Komm, du süße Todesstunde, BWV 161, and set it to other words in Parts I and VI of his Christmas Oratorio. Franz Liszt arranged it for the sixth station of his Via crucis, Rued Langgaard wrote variations on it for string quartet, Edmund Rubbra used it in the final chorus of his Ninth Symphony, "Sinfonia Sacra", and Mauricio Kagel quoted it at the end of his Sankt-Bach-Passion, written for the tercentenary of Bach's birth in 1985.

The first English translation, "O Head so full of bruises", was made in 1752 by John Gambold, an Anglican vicar in Oxfordshire. The American Presbyterian minister James Waddel Alexander (1804–1859) translated it again in 1830 as "O sacred head, now wounded", and his version became one of the most widely used in 19th- and 20th-century hymnals. Henry Williams Baker made another from the German for Hymns Ancient and Modern in 1861, Catherine Winkworth one for her collection Lyra Germanica in 1855, and in 1899 the poet Robert Bridges translated the Latin afresh; his version is used in the Episcopal hymnals of 1940 and 1982 and the New English Hymnal (1986). Karen Lynn Davidson's version, which draws on parts of 2 Nephi in the Book of Mormon, is sung in the hymnal of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.

The tune has carried other words too, from Timothy Dudley-Smith's hymn "We come as guests invited" to secular songs: Tom Glazer's protest song "Because All Men Are Brothers" (1947), Paul Simon's "American Tune" and John K. Samson's "Stop Error".
== meaning
A meditation on the suffering of Christ on the cross. The singer addresses the wounded, thorn-crowned head of Christ and dwells on his death as a personal grief. The mood is grave and sorrowful.
