== story
Ganggangsullae is an ancient Korean dance with song, performed only by women, that began as a rite for a good harvest and has become a cultural symbol of Korea. It is performed mostly in South Jeolla Province on the south-western coast, at Chuseok and at Daeboreum, when the full moon rises above the mountains, and also at New Year and Dano. It is thought to go back to a time when the ancient Koreans believed that the Sun, the Moon and the Earth ruled the universe, and danced under the brightest full moon of the year to wish for a fertile harvest; since the moon is largest at Chuseok, the dance became a notable event of that holiday. The meaning of the name is not clear. Some read it as native Korean words, in the Jeolla dialect "in circles" and "patrol", that is "beware of your surroundings"; others as a phrase in Hanja warning that a powerful enemy is about to invade, a reading for which there is little evidence.

Under the Joseon dynasty, as Confucian rule narrowed the lives of women, so that even noblewomen had to cover their faces and might not go out of the house, the dance gave women a chance to go outside and sing together, away from those unseen restraints. A story of the 16th-century Japanese invasion of Korea tells that Admiral Yi Sun-sin had women dance it in military uniform on Mount Okmae, so that Japanese scouts overestimated the strength of the Korean troops.

Young and old women, in traditional Korean clothes, join hands in a circle under the moon and turn, without instruments in the old manner. A lead singer sings a line and everyone answers with the refrain "ganggangsullae", in the call-and-response form of most Korean folk songs; they sing of their hardships, their loves and their wishes. The tempo rises from the slow jinyangjo through the moderate jungmori to the fast jajinmori, and the circle, which stands for the full moon, turns into spirals, concentric rings and lines before closing again, with folk games such as the Gatekeeper Play and Stomping Roof Tile; the dance can last until dawn. A slow "late" Ganggangsullae and a quick Jajeun-Ganggangsullae are danced, and a middle-tempo Jung-Ganggangsullae only around Haenam and Jindo. UNESCO inscribed Ganggangsullae as an intangible cultural heritage of Korea in 2009. It is taught in music classes in local schools, and the Korean pop group ATEEZ has drawn on it in its performances.
== meaning
A call-and-response song for a dance in a ring: the leader's lines tell of the rising harvest moon, of the women's hardships and loves, and the circle answers with the refrain of the dance's name. It begins slow and quiet and grows fast and joyful as the night goes on.
