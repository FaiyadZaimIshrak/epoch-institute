== story
According to the Book of Judges, Deborah was a prophetess, the fourth Judge of Israel before the monarchy and the only female judge named in the Hebrew Bible; she gave her judgments beneath a date palm between Ramah in Benjamin and Bethel in Ephraim. The people of Israel had been oppressed for twenty years by Jabin, the king of Canaan, whose capital was Hazor. Deborah summoned Barak, son of Abinoam, from Kedesh in Naphtali and told him that God commanded him to muster ten thousand men of Naphtali and Zebulun on Mount Tabor. Barak would not go without her; she agreed, but declared that the glory of the victory would then belong to a woman. Sisera, the commander of Jabin's army, gathered nine hundred iron chariots and was utterly defeated; he fled on foot to the tent of Jael, who killed him while he slept. Judges 4 tells this story in prose, and the account ends with forty years of peace in the land.

Judges 5 tells the same story in poetry. This passage, the Song of Deborah, is a victory hymn sung by Deborah and Barak, and it differs a little from the prose account. It names six tribes that took part, Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir (a group associated with the tribe of Manasseh), Zebulun, Issachar and Naphtali, where Judges 4 has only Naphtali and Zebulun; it does not mention Jabin; it rebukes three other tribes, Reuben, Dan and Asher, for staying away, and does not mention Gad, Simeon or Judah. The biblical scholar Michael Coogan writes that for the song's redactors, the death of the Canaanite general at the hands of a woman "is a further sign that Yahweh ultimately is responsible for the victory". Victory hymns are a convention of the Hebrew Bible, but this one is unusual in celebrating the victory of two women, Deborah the prophetess and Jael the warrior; Jael has been compared with the heroine of the Book of Judith.

The song is commonly counted among the oldest texts of the Bible, and with the Song of the Sea in Exodus it is sometimes cited as the earliest example of Hebrew poetry. Its date is disputed: many scholars place it as early as the 12th century BCE, others between about 700 and 450 BCE, and some as late as the 3rd century BCE. The rabbinic chronology Seder Olam Rabbah places Deborah's forty years as judge from 1107 to 1067 BC. Reading the archaeology, scholars have set the war with Sisera in the second half of the 12th century BC or of the 11th. Sisera is not a Semitic name and the story is set "in the days of Shamgar", a hero known for fighting the Philistines, so some, among them Łukasz Niesiołowski-Spanò, believe it is really about the Sea Peoples; a layer of Hazor from about 1200 BCE shows signs of a catastrophic fire. Israel Finkelstein placed the song's background in the early 10th century BCE, linking it with the destruction of Megiddo around 1000–985 BCE, while others find that it fits the 13th century BCE, the age of the literature of Ugarit.
== meaning
A hymn of victory that gives the triumph to the God of Israel. It recalls the hard days before the rising, praises the tribes that came to fight and shames those that stayed at home, and tells how the Canaanite army was routed and its general killed by Jael. The mood is exultant and fierce, and its heroes are two women.
