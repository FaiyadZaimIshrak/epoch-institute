== story
"Sare Jahan se Accha", formally "Tarānah-e-Hindi" ("Anthem of the People of Hindustan"), is an Urdu patriotic song for children written by the poet Muhammad Iqbal in the ghazal style of Urdu poetry. It was published in the weekly journal Ittehad on 16 August 1904. Iqbal was then a lecturer at Government College, Lahore, and the next year a student, Lala Har Dayal, invited him to preside over a function there; instead of giving a speech, Iqbal sang the poem. It is an ode to Hindustan, the land that today comprises Bangladesh, India and Pakistan, and it carries a yearning for and attachment to that land, a sense of cultural memory and an elegiac quality. It quickly became an anthem of opposition to the British Raj, and in 1924 it was published in Iqbal's Urdu book Bang-i-Dara.

In 1905 the 27-year-old Iqbal saw the future society of the subcontinent as pluralistic, a composite Hindu-Muslim culture. Later that year he left for three years in Europe, which turned him into an Islamic philosopher. In 1910 he wrote another song for children, "Tarana-e-Milli" ("Anthem of the Religious Community"), in the same metre and rhyme scheme, which renounced much of the sentiment of the earlier song: its homeland was no longer Hindustan but the whole world. In 1930, in his presidential address to the Muslim League's annual conference at Allahabad, he supported a separate nation-state in the Muslim-majority areas of the subcontinent, an idea that inspired the creation of Pakistan.

The song has remained popular in India. Mahatma Gandhi is said to have sung it over a hundred times while imprisoned at Yerawada Jail in Pune in the 1930s. In the 1930s and 1940s it was sung to a slower tune. In 1945, while working in Mumbai with the Indian People's Theatre Association and composing for K. A. Abbas's film Dharti Ke Lal and Chetan Anand's Neecha Nagar, the sitarist Ravi Shankar was asked to set the song; he found the existing tune too slow and sad and gave it a stronger one, which is the tune sung today. Lata Mangeshkar later recorded it to a third, quite different tune.

Four of its stanzas became an unofficial national song in India, and Ravi Shankar's version, arranged as a march by Antsher Lobo, was adopted as the official quick march of the Indian Armed Forces. The massed bands of the armed forces play it each year on Independence Day, on Republic Day and at the close of Beating Retreat, and schools sing it at morning assemblies. In 1984 Rakesh Sharma, the first Indian astronaut, used its opening line to describe to Prime Minister Indira Gandhi how India looked from space, and Manmohan Singh quoted the poem at his first press conference as prime minister.
== meaning
An ode to Hindustan as the best of all lands, praising its mountains and rivers and its people's love for it wherever they are. It calls its people, of every faith, one people of one homeland, and reflects on how they have endured through the centuries. The mood is proud and affectionate, with a note of sorrow at its close.
