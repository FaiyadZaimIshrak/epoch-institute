== story
"Personent hodie" is a Christmas carol first published in 1582 in Piae Cantiones, a Finnish song book of 74 medieval songs with Latin texts, collected by Jacobus Finno (Jaakko Suomalainen), a Lutheran cleric, and published by T. P. Rutha. The book grew out of the libraries of cathedral song schools, whose repertory had strong links with medieval Prague, where clerical students from Finland and Sweden had studied for generations. A highly similar melody is found in a manuscript of 1360 from the Bavarian city of Moosburg, and the song is usually dated from that manuscript.

The Latin text is probably a parody, in the musical sense, of a 12th-century song beginning "Intonent hodie voces ecclesie", written in honour of Saint Nicholas, patron saint of sailors and children, whose feast is 6 December. Hugh Keyte and Andrew Parrott point out that two of the verses have an unusual double repeat of a syllable. In the older song these repeats illustrated the three boys and the three girls whom Saint Nicholas saved, the boys from drowning and the girls from prostitution. The text was probably rewritten for the Feast of the Holy Innocents, 28 December, when the choristers and their "boy bishop" traditionally took the places of the senior clergy in the choir stalls. The carol is still often linked with Holy Innocents' Day.

Songs from Piae Cantiones went on being sung in Finland until the 19th century. In Britain the book became known after a rare copy, once owned by Peter of Nyland, was given to the British Minister in Stockholm, who gave it to John Mason Neale in 1852. From that copy Neale and Thomas Helmore published songs in two collections, in 1853 and 1854. Neale's "Let the Song be Begun" uses the melody but not the text of the carol.

The carol became more prominent in England after Gustav Holst arranged it for unison voices and orchestra in 1916. With the accompaniment reduced for organ, his version is often used as a processional hymn in churches and cathedrals and often forms part of the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols; it was sung in the service broadcast from King's College Chapel, Cambridge, in 2011. Holst's setting is sometimes called "Theodoric", after his middle name, Theodore, and under that name it is the tune of Percy Dearmer's hymn "God is love, his the care". The most common English version of the words, "On this day earth shall ring", is by Jane M. Joseph (1894–1929), who published it under the name "James M. Joseph". Elizabeth Poston made another, the "Boys' Carol", in 1965, and the tune was also used for the hymn "Long Ago, Prophets Knew".

Later arrangements include a harmonised choral version by Hugh Keyte and Andrew Parrott in the New Oxford Book of Carols, one by Antony Pitts in the Naxos Book of Carols, two by John Rutter and a Festival Procession by Lara Hoggard with brass, orchestra and organ. Maddy Prior and The Carnival Band recorded it with period instruments on A Tapestry of Carols (1987), and the quartet Anonymous 4 recorded the song with its older "Intonent hodie" text on Legends of St. Nicholas. The jazz musician Wayne Shorter recorded the melody as "12th Century Carol" in 2003.
== meaning
A call for children's voices to ring out today in praise of the child born for us. The verses tell of his birth into the world, of the three Magi who came with gifts, and end by calling on clergy and choirboys alike to sing like the angels, with glory to God in the highest. The mood is loud, bright and processional.
