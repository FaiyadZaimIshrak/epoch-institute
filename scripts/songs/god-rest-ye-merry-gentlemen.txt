== story
"God Rest Ye Merry, Gentlemen", also sung as "God Rest You Merry, Gentlemen" and known as "Tidings of Comfort and Joy", is a traditional English Christmas carol. An early version is in an anonymous manuscript of the 1650s, with a slightly different first line. The earliest known printed edition is a broadsheet of about 1760, Three New Christmas Carols, and the November 1764 issue of the Monthly Review refers to it. Some sources take the carol back to the 16th century, while others date it to the 18th or early 19th century.

The opening words carry an older sense. "God rest you merry" meant "may God keep you in peace and happiness": the Oxford English Dictionary records the phrase from 1534, and it appears in Shakespeare's As You Like It and, as "rest you merry", in Romeo and Juliet, both of the 1590s. As that use of "rest" faded, the line came to be read and punctuated as "God rest you, merry gentlemen", already in a variant printed in 1775 in The Beauties of the Magazines, taken from a song sheet bought from a street caroller. The form "ye" is a later pseudo-archaism.

The tune now best known is a traditional English melody in the minor mode. James Nares wrote it down in the mid-18th century as "The old Christmas Carol"; it first appears in print in a rondo for fortepiano by Samuel Wesley, already reviewed in 1815, and then in a parody published by William Hone in 1820, and the full tune as sung today was published by Chappell in 1855. A second tune, "Cornish", was in print by 1833 and was called "the usual version" by the Oxford Book of Carols of 1928, but it is seldom heard now. In March 1824 The Gentleman's Magazine complained that no carols were heard in London "excepting some croaking ballad-singer bawling out 'God rest you, merry gentlemen'". The solicitor and antiquarian William Sandys did most to make the carol popular, by printing it in his Christmas Carols Ancient and Modern (1833).

Charles Dickens refers to it in A Christmas Carol (1843), changing "rest" to "bless", and George Eliot quotes it in Silas Marner (1861). Gustav Holst used it in his choral fantasy Christmas Day (1910), and Victor Hely-Hutchinson made the third movement of his Carol Symphony (1927) a scherzo on its tune. In the UK the text in Carols for Choirs (1961) has become the usual one. It has been recorded by many singers and bands since Bing Crosby's version of 1942.
== meaning
A Christmas carol that wishes its hearers peace and happiness, in the old sense of its opening words, and brings them the good news of Christmas. The mood is sturdy and festive, though the best-known tune is in a minor key.
