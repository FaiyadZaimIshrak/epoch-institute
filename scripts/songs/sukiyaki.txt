== story
"Ue o Muite Arukō" ("I Look Up as I Walk") was written by the lyricist Rokusuke Ei and the composer Hachidai Nakamura and sung by Kyu Sakamoto, who released it in Japan in 1961. Ei wrote the words while walking home from the 1960 Anpo protests against the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty, putting into them his frustration and dejection at the failure to stop the treaty; he kept them general on purpose, so that they could be heard as being about any lost love. In Japan it topped the Popular Music Selling Record chart of the magazine Music Life for three months and was ranked the number one song of 1961.

Its English title came by way of Britain. In 1963 Pye Records released an instrumental version by Kenny Ball and his Jazzmen and, fearing that English-speaking listeners would find the original title hard to remember or pronounce, called it "Sukiyaki", after a Japanese hot pot of cooked beef. The word has nothing to do with the song; it was chosen because it was short, catchy, recognisably Japanese and familiar to English speakers. A Newsweek columnist compared the renaming to issuing "Moon River" in Japan as "Beef Stew". Capitol Records in the United States and His Master's Voice in Britain kept the title when they released Sakamoto's own recording a few months later.

In June 1963 "Sukiyaki" spent three weeks at the top of the Billboard Hot 100, one of the few songs not in English to do so and the first in a non-European language. It remained the only single by an Asian artist to top the chart until "Dynamite" by the South Korean band BTS in 2020, and it has been described as a metaphor for Japan's emergence on the world stage after the Second World War. With more than 13 million copies sold worldwide, it is one of the best-selling singles of all time. An instrumental version was played by NASA over the radio to the Gemini VII astronauts, making it one of the first pieces of music sent to people in space.

The song lived on in new versions. Janice-Marie Johnson of A Taste of Honey had loved it as a nine-year-old in 1963 and learned its Japanese words by sound. Working from a literal translation supplied by Ei, she wrote new English words about a love that had ended. Capitol's vice-president Cecil Hale resisted the track, and it was added to the album Twice as Sweet (1980) only after Johnson agreed to take no credit or royalties for her lyrics. Released as a single in 1981, it reached number one on the R&B and adult contemporary charts and number three on the Hot 100. The R&B group 4 P.M. took the same English words to number eight in 1994.

In Japan the song became a sign of hope. Japan Post issued a stamp for it on 16 March 1999. After the Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami of 11 March 2011, the Suntory company used it in commercials in which 71 actors and singers sang parts of it to encourage people to keep going, and it was heard the same year in the Studio Ghibli film From Up on Poppy Hill. A remix was performed at the closing ceremony of the Tokyo Olympic Games, held in 2021.
== meaning
A song of private sorrow carried with dignity. Written out of political disappointment, it was open enough to be heard as a song of lost love or of quiet endurance.
