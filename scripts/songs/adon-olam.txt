== story
Adon Olam, "Eternal Lord" or "Sovereign of the Universe", is a hymn of the Jewish liturgy. Who wrote it, and when, is uncertain. It is sometimes attributed to the poet Solomon ibn Gabirol (1021–1058), but there is no solid evidence for this, and its regular metre does not match his other poems; others have named Hai Gaon (939–1038) or even the Talmudic sage Yohanan ben Zakkai. John Rayner suggested that it was written in Spain in the thirteenth or fourteenth century, since it is missing from the prayer book Sefer Abudarham of about 1340, and Reuven Kimelman thinks Abraham ibn Ezra the likeliest author, from its style and its first appearance in Western Europe. It entered the morning liturgy only in the 15th century and has been a regular part of the daily and Shabbat prayers since.

The Ashkenazi text has five stanzas in ten lines, which Morris Silverman took to be the original; Sephardi and Mizrahi prayer books add lines, giving 12, 15 or 16. It is strictly metrical, every line of eight syllables. Seligman Baer thought it was first meant to be said before going to sleep, because of its closing thought, and it has been recited at bedtime, on the deathbed and, in some congregations, quietly and without a tune to announce a death in the community. Its grandeur may have brought it into the service itself, where it is sung at the beginning or the end. Sephardim and British synagogues sing it at the close of the Shabbat and festival morning service, many communities at the end of Mussaf, the German rite at the start of daily morning prayers, and Ashkenazim sometimes in place of Yigdal at the close of the evening service. When it ends a service the congregation sits while singing it, to show that they are in no hurry to leave the house of prayer.

For so familiar a hymn there are few traditional tunes, four or five, the oldest apparently a short melody of Spanish origin. The most common is attributed to the Russian cantor Eliezer Mordecai Gerovitsch, who published it in 1897 marked as an "old form", which suggests he arranged an existing tune. The synagogue composers of the 19th century each wrote several settings, many of them elaborately polyphonic, until congregations turned back to tunes that everyone could sing; Simon W. Waley's (1827–1876) setting for the West London Synagogue was taken up by Orthodox as well as Reform congregations across England and its colonies. Because of its simple metre the hymn can be sung to almost any tune: congregations use seasonal ones, such as Ma'oz Tzur before Hanukkah, and Hebrew schools and summer camps sing it for fun to "Yankee Doodle" or "Jamaica Farewell". In 1976 Uzi Hitman wrote a livelier tune for the 8th Hasidic Song Festival, which became the best-known version in Israel outside the synagogue.

Several English translations keep the Hebrew metre and rhyme, so that they can be sung to the same tunes: a loose one in Prayers, Psalms and Hymns for the Use of Jewish Children (1905), a closer one attributed to Frederick de Sola Mendes in The Jewish Encyclopedia (1906) and the Union Hymnal (1914), and two 21st-century versions that draw on these, in the Koren Sacks Siddur (2009) and the machzor Mishkan HaNefesh (2015).
== meaning
A hymn of praise to God as the eternal sovereign of the universe. It moves from that greatness to personal trust, and closes with the singer placing himself in God's keeping through sleep and waking, which is why it has been said at bedtime and at the deathbed. Its mood is solemn and confident.
