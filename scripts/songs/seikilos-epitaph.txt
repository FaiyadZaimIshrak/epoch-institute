== story
The Seikilos epitaph is a Greek inscription on a marble pillar (stele) from Tralles in western Anatolia, now the city of Aydın in Turkey. It preserves the oldest surviving complete musical composition: older notated music exists, such as the Hurrian songs and the Delphic Hymns, but only in fragments. The stone carries an elegiac couplet in which the stone itself speaks to the passer-by, as in many ancient epitaphs, and below it a short song with signs for the melody written above the words. It is commonly dated to the 1st or 2nd century AD; scholars reading the letter forms have argued firmly for each of the two centuries.

Its last line, now lost, named Seikilos and, in the usual reconstruction, Euterpe, with a verb meaning "is alive" that ancient dedicators used when they had outlived the person they commemorated. On this reading Seikilos set up the stone for a woman named Euterpe, perhaps his wife; others read the name as the Muse of lyric poetry and music, or as Seikilos's father. Armand D'Angour has proposed that the pillar was not a tomb marker at all but a monument Seikilos raised to his own skill as a poet and musician.

The stele was found around 1883 by the Irish engineer Edward Purser while the Ottoman Railway was being built at Aydın, and the archaeologist William Mitchell Ramsay published a description that year. A rubbing made before 1893 was published by Théodore Reinach in 1894. Purser had the damaged base sawn flat so that his wife could stand flowerpots on it, which destroyed the last line; it is known only from the rubbing. The pillar passed to his son-in-law in Buca. During the Burning of Smyrna in 1922, in the Greco-Turkish War, the Dutch consul in İzmir took it, and it travelled by way of Istanbul and Stockholm to The Hague, where it stayed, presumed lost, until 1966. The National Museum of Denmark in Copenhagen then acquired it, revealed it the following year and still shows it.

The song is diatonic. Thomas J. Mathiesen and Jon Solomon place it in the Iastian tonos, while noting that its arrangement of tones is that of the Phrygian species; others describe it as a segment of the Ionian scale. In 1893 Otto Crusius observed that its melody, like the hymns of Mesomedes, tends to follow the pitch accents of the Greek words, which the Delphic Hymns, published the same year, confirmed. Dots above some notes, the stigmai, are still debated: they may mark the raised half of each bar or a rhythmic stress, and D'Angour, Stefan Hagel and Tosca Lynch disagree on how the song should be barred. The melody is also strikingly close to the Hosanna antiphon of the Roman liturgy for Palm Sunday.
== meaning
A short song about making the most of life: while you live, be seen and do not grieve, because life is brief and time takes its due in the end. Set on a memorial stone, it turns a reminder of death into advice to the living.
