== story
Hans Leip, a schoolteacher from Hamburg conscripted into the Imperial German Army, wrote the words as a poem of three verses in 1915; he is said to have joined the nickname of a friend's girlfriend, Lili, to the name of another friend, Marleen, a nurse. The poem was published in 1937, with two more verses, as "The Song of a Young Soldier on Watch". Norbert Schultze set it to music in 1938, and Lale Andersen first recorded it in 1939 as "Das Mädchen unter der Laterne" ("The Girl under the Lantern"). It sold about 700 copies.

The song's fame came by accident. After the Germans occupied Belgrade in 1941, Radio Belgrade became Soldatensender Belgrad, a forces station heard across Europe and the Mediterranean. A lieutenant of the station, on leave in Vienna, brought back a pile of second-hand records that included Andersen's, and for lack of other recordings the station's commander, Karl-Heinz Reintgen, played it often, at ten each night. Soldiers of both the Axis and the Allies listened. In the Western Desert in 1942 the British officer Fitzroy Maclean heard it as a sound of a Europe far away; later, fighting beside Josip Broz Tito's Yugoslav Partisans, he and his comrades tuned in each night as Belgrade became their goal, until one evening in late 1944 the broadcast fell silent. A South African leaflet printed an anonymous English translation as the theme song of the Eighth Army and the 6th Armoured Division, and Andersen's record later sold over a million copies.

Both sides also used the song as propaganda. In early 1942 Andersen recorded it in English with words by Norman Baillie-Stewart, a former British officer working for German propaganda. In 1944 the American Office of Strategic Services had Marlene Dietrich record it in German for broadcasts meant to demoralise German soldiers; she sang it to Allied troops across Africa, Italy and Europe, and her recording, released by Decca in 1945, made it her signature song for the rest of her career. Tommie Connor wrote the English words "Lily of the Lamplight" in 1944, Allied soldiers in Italy turned the tune into "The D-Day Dodgers", and the Soviet officer Lev Kopelev wrote a German parody, now lost, to discourage enemy troops; the poet Joseph Brodsky later based a Russian poem on it.

After the war the song kept its place in memory and ceremony. It is the slow march of the Special Air Service and of Princess Patricia's Canadian Light Infantry, and its melody, first sung by the Siliwangi Division of the Indonesian Army as it marched from West Java to Yogyakarta after the Renville Agreement of 1948, is that division's official march. Humphrey Jennings made a film about it in 1944, Rainer Werner Fassbinder told Andersen's story in Lili Marleen (1980), and in 2005 a seven-CD set gathered nearly 200 versions.
== meaning
A soldier's love song of separation and homesickness, tender and melancholy, in which soldiers on both sides of the Second World War heard their own longing.
