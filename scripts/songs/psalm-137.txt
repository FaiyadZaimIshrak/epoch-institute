== story
After Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon took Jerusalem in 597 BC, and in the campaigns that followed, people of the Kingdom of Judah were deported to Babylonia, where they were held until some time after the fall of Babylon in 539 BC. Psalm 137 gives voice to that exile. It is a communal lament of nine verses, set by the rivers of Babylon, the Euphrates, its tributaries and the Tigris, in which the exiles mourn Jerusalem, refuse their captors' demand for a song, vow never to forget the city, and end with a call for violent revenge on its enemies. Rabbinical sources attributed it to the prophet Jeremiah, and the Septuagint heads it "For David. By Jeremias, in the Captivity." The Greek and Latin Bibles number it Psalm 136; in Latin it is known by its opening words, "Super flumina Babylonis". The Methodist writer Joseph Benson read the harps of the psalm as standing for all instruments of music and its singers as probably Levites, the musicians of the Temple's service.

The psalm has a fixed place in Jewish practice. It is recited before the Grace After Meals on weekdays (Psalm 126 replaces it on the Sabbath, holidays and festive meals) and before Tisha B'Av, the fast that mourns the destruction of the Temples in Jerusalem. Verses 5 and 6 are said at Jewish weddings just before a glass is broken in mourning for the Temple, and it is one of the ten psalms of the Tikkun HaKlali of Rebbe Nachman of Breslov.

Christian churches have prayed it for centuries: at Matins in the Eastern Orthodox Church, solemnly on the Sundays before Lent, in the Coptic book of hours, and at Wednesday Vespers in the Roman Breviary following the Rule of Saint Benedict. Its last verses have long troubled its users. After the Second Vatican Council the last three verses were removed from Catholic liturgical books as incompatible with the Gospel message, the 1962 Book of Common Prayer of the Anglican Church of Canada removed them too, and many musical settings leave out the final verse.

Composers have set it in every period: Latin motets by Palestrina, Lassus and Victoria; a German rhymed paraphrase by Wolfgang Dachstein, first published in 1525, which became a Lutheran hymn and was also taken up in Ashkenazi Jewish culture; Salamone Rossi's Hebrew setting; settings by Schütz and an organ chorale prelude by Bach. In America William Billings turned it into a lament for Boston under British occupation. Lord Byron paraphrased it in his Hebrew Melodies in 1815, and it inspired the chorus of Hebrew slaves, "Va, pensiero", in Verdi's opera Nabucco (1842). In 1970 Brent Dowe and Trevor McNaughton of the Jamaican reggae group the Melodians based the Rastafarian song "Rivers of Babylon" partly on its opening verses; Boney M.'s version of 1978 made it widely known.

The psalm has also been carried into politics and protest. Pope Gregory X quoted it when he left the Crusade upon his election in the papal conclave of 1268–1271. Frederick Douglass, in his speech "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?", compared being asked to speak on the Fourth of July to the exiles of the psalm being asked to sing in a foreign land.
== meaning
A lament of exiles far from their homeland: they grieve for Jerusalem, will not make music for those who hold them captive, and bind themselves never to forget the city. Its mood moves from quiet mourning to an oath of loyalty and, at the end, to a bitter curse calling for revenge on the city's enemies, including violence against their children.

== notes
Status: pd. Scripture of the Hebrew Bible; author unknown (rabbinical attribution to Jeremiah). The KJV (1611) is public domain in most countries but under Crown patent in the UK; the Hebrew and Septuagint texts are in the article's table. Avoid the NIV (Bible Gateway) and the USCCB NAB text, both linked in the article: they are in copyright.
year: the article gives no date of composition, only that it expresses the exile (from 597 BC, until after 539 BC). -570 is my midpoint reading; the editor may prefer -597 or -539.
local: the Latin incipit, by which the article says it is known in Latin. The Hebrew opening words appear in the article only as the title of Rossi's setting; the psalm has no other title. Both are the first words of the text; if even an incipit counts as words of the song, use "Psalm 137" here.
Titles of later works (Rivers of Babylon, If I Forget Thee, Jerusalem, Byron's poem, Cohen's song) echo phrases of the psalm; I gave them only as titles of those works and left out Byron's poem title. The editor may trim them. I did not quote the KJV opening line, the "strange land" phrase, or the line Gregory X quoted, all of which the article quotes.
The Septuagint superscription ("For David. By Jeremias, in the Captivity.") is quoted in the story: it is a heading, not the psalm's words, but drop it if preferred.
Joseph Benson: the article quotes him without a work or year, so he is not in sources.
The Siege of Jerusalem: the article says "Nebuchadnezzar II's successful siege of Jerusalem in 597 BC"; I kept BC in events to match.
kids: no, because the psalm ends with a call for violence against children.
Left out: most 20th/21st-century items (Sublime, Sinead O'Connor, Mad Men, Mountain Goats, video games, comics), the long literature list beyond a few, and Bell's comment on the curse.
Composer "traditional": the psalm was sung (Levite musicians, by Benson's reading) but no original melody is known; the article does not discuss one.
