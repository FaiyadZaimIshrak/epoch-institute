== story
"O Tannenbaum" ("O fir tree"), known in English as "O Christmas Tree", is a German Christmas song built on an older folk song that had nothing to do with the holiday. It draws on a long tradition in which the fir tree, green in every season, stood for faithfulness. As early as the 16th century songbooks held a text that gave rise to a folk song, "O Tannenbaum, du trägst ein' grünen Zweig" ("O fir tree, you wear a green branch"). The folk song collector Ludwig Erk found three different melodies for it in different regions of Germany, which he set out in the 1856 edition of his Deutscher Liederhort; the tunes were written down only in the 18th and 19th centuries, but Melchior Franck had already quoted a line of the song on one of them in 1605, in his quodlibet "Nun fanget an".

In 1820 the German educator August Zarnack printed "Der Tannenbaum" as No. 51 in the second volume of his collection of folk songs for schools. The old folk song may have inspired it, but Zarnack's text was a lover's complaint, setting the faithfulness of the fir tree against the fickleness of a young woman, and, as folklorists of German Romanticism sometimes did, he may have written much or all of it himself in what he took to be a folk idiom. He set it to a melody published earlier with the song "Es lebe hoch der Zimmermannsgeselle".

The words known today are by Ernst Anschütz, a teacher and organist in Leipzig, who published them in his Musikalisches Schulgesangbuch in 1824. He kept Zarnack's first verse and wrote two more, linking the tree's faithful green to the joy and hope of Christmas, so that in German the song names Christmas only in its second verse. Anschütz printed it with the same "Zimmermannsgeselle" tune, but in time it came to be sung to the melody of "Lauriger Horatius", an old student song. As the custom of decorating Christmas trees spread in these years, it became one of the best-known Christmas songs in both German and English, although it is not, strictly, about such a tree. Some versions sung today change "treu" ("faithful") to "grün" ("green") and the Christmas line to one about winter, and with the third verse, with its lessons drawn from religion, left out, the song can be sung as a secular one.

The tune has carried many other texts. It is the tune of "The Red Flag", the anthem of the British and Irish Labour Parties, of the former state songs "Maryland, My Maryland" and "Florida, My Florida", of "Michigan, My Michigan" and "The Song of Iowa", of the "Ode to Labrador", of college songs in the United States and China, of the Boy Scouts' "Scout Vespers", of a Swedish schoolchildren's song about the bus driver, and of "Democrats, Good Democrats", a campaign song for Grover Cleveland in 1884.
== meaning
The song speaks to the fir tree and praises its faithful green, set in Zarnack's first verse against human fickleness. In Anschütz's added verses that green becomes a sign of the joy and hope of Christmas, and the third verse draws a lesson of faith from it. The mood is warm and simple, a song for home and school.
