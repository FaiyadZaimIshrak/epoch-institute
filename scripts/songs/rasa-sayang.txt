== story
"Rasa Sayang", literally "loving feeling", is a folk song of the Malay Archipelago, popular in Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore. In Indonesia it is sung as "Rasa Sayange". Its verses are pantun, the traditional Malay verse form: four lines rhyming ABAB with equal syllable counts, in which the first two lines set up the rhyme and the last two carry the message, often unrelated to the first pair. Only the opening verse breaks the regular syllable length.

In Brunei, Malaysia and Singapore it is known as a Malay folk song, also called "Rasa Sayang Hey", related in form to Dondang Sayang and other pantun-based songs. In Indonesia it is known as an Ambonese Malay song from Maluku: the "-e" ending of "Sayange" is a feature of Moluccan Malay, heard also in "Ambon Manise" and "Nona Manise", which the Malaysian version replaces with "hey".

Its early history is known from films, broadcasts and press reports. A British documentary of 1938, Five Faces, used it as background music, and it was played on Penang radio in 1939. In 1958 an unnamed singer in British Malaya told The Straits Times of having first sung it before an audience more than twenty years earlier. During the Second World War it appeared in the Japanese film Marai no Tora (1943), about a Japanese secret agent in Malaya. Usmar Ismail used it in his Indonesian films Darah dan Doa (1950) and Lewat Djam Malam (1954). In 1958 the World Assembly of Youth conference in New Delhi adopted it as its theme tune, and in 1959 Malay students from Malaya sang it in Sydney at a fundraising exhibition for UNICEF. Cathay Keris produced the Malay comedy film Rasa Sayang Eh in Singapore in 1959; in 1960 George de Fretes recorded it on Indonesian Classics No. 1, and a Hindi version, sung by Lata Mangeshkar and Mohammed Rafi, was used in the film Singapore. The Lokananta record company gave it, on a souvenir LP of Indonesian folk songs, to the participants of the 4th Asian Games in Jakarta in 1962. In 1989 the Singaporean singer-songwriter Dick Lee recorded a version with descriptions of Singaporean culture.

The question of whose song it is came to a head in 2007, when the Malaysian Tourism Board used it in a commercial for its "Truly Asia" campaign. Some Indonesians accused Malaysia of taking Indonesian heritage; Indonesian news sites reported it as a song of the Maluku Islands that had appeared in early Indonesian films and recordings. In November 2007 around a thousand people demonstrated outside the Malaysian embassy in Jakarta against the use of "Rasa Sayang" and other cultural items, such as Reog Ponorogo, in such advertisements, and the Indonesian government began an inventory of songs as the country's cultural property. Malaysia answered that the song is sung throughout the archipelago and belongs to its peoples, Malaysians and Indonesians alike: Tourism Minister Adnan Mansor called it a folk song of the Nusantara, of which Malaysia is part, and Rais Yatim, Minister of Culture, Arts and Heritage, recognised it as shared between Indonesia and Malaysia.
== meaning
A light, affectionate song of feeling for someone dear, built from pantun verses: each opens with an image from nature or daily life that sets the rhyme and ends with a message to the listener. The Malaysian and Indonesian versions differ in some verses and in the refrain's ending. The mood is warm and playful.
