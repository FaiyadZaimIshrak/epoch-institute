== story
The Odes of Solomon are a collection of 42 odes, anonymous but long attributed to Solomon. Most scholars date them to between 70 and 125 CE, though estimates run from the 1st to the 3rd century and many settle on the 2nd. Earlier scholars thought they were first written in Greek or Hebrew; the consensus now is that the original language was Syriac or Aramaic and that they came from the region of Syria. Most scholars believe the writer was a Jewish Christian, very likely a convert to Christianity from the Essene community, because the odes resemble both the writings found at Qumran and the Gospel of John; James H. Charlesworth concludes that he was an Essene convert to the Johannine community, and some have argued that he had seen John the Baptist. Some read Ode 4 as referring to the closing of the temple at Leontopolis in Egypt, which would date it to about 73 CE. Possible references to the odes, perhaps even quotations, in the letters of Ignatius of Antioch, and possible allusions in Justin Martyr and Irenaeus, support an early date.

For centuries the odes were known only in part. After portions were found quoted in the Pistis Sophia, a Gnostic text discovered in 1773, scholars searched for fuller copies. In 1909 James Rendel Harris discovered a pile of forgotten leaves of a Syriac manuscript lying on a shelf in his study; all he could recall was that they came from the "neighbourhood of the Tigris". This manuscript, now Cod. Syr. 9 in the John Rylands Library, is the most complete text: it begins partway into Ode 3, the first two odes being lost, runs to the end of Ode 42, and goes on with the older Jewish Psalms of Solomon until its end is lost too. It is a late copy, no earlier than the 15th century. Harris published the odes from the Syriac (a second edition appeared in 1911), and again with Alphonse Mingana in two volumes in 1916 and 1920.

The odes were perhaps composed to be sung in worship. In the Syriac manuscripts every ode ends with a hallelujah, and the Harris manuscript also marks the word in the middle of an ode with the Syriac letter hê; plural commands suggest that a congregation is sometimes addressed. Several commentators find clear early baptismal imagery in them, above all water, from floods and drowning to the drinking of living water and the well-spring, together with the language of conversion and initiation, so that some call them a collection of baptismal hymns; Charlesworth has led the criticism of that view. In his words, their key characteristic is a joyous tone of thanksgiving for the coming of the promised Messiah and for the present experience of eternal life and love. They stress spreading the knowledge of God and the conversion of others.

Their theology has long been debated. Some scholars once proposed a Gnostic origin, pointing to the frequent word "knowledge" and some unusual images, but that view has come under heavy scrutiny, and Henry Chadwick, Emerton and Charlesworth hold that the odes have nothing to do with Gnosticism. They call Christ the pre-existent Word, the Son of Man and the Son of God, speak of Father, Son and Holy Spirit, allude to the crucifixion and the descent into Hades, and, depending on their date, may hold the earliest attestation of the virgin birth outside the Bible. Eric Segelberg (1958) noted rites they share with the Mandaean Qolasta prayers, among them baptism in water, drinking of water, investiture and ritual meals. In 2010 the composer John Schreiner released The Odes Project, a two-disc adaptation of the odes into modern music.
== meaning
A book of short hymns of praise and thanksgiving, in which a single voice rejoices in the coming of the Messiah, in salvation and in the love between the Lord and the believer. Christ appears as the Word who existed before the world and as the giver of living water; water, springs and floods run through the whole book. The mood is joyous, intimate and mystical, and every ode closes with a hallelujah.
