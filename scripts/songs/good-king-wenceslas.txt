== story
"Good King Wenceslas" is a Christmas carol that tells of a tenth-century ruler of Bohemia who goes out in harsh winter weather to give alms to a poor peasant on the Feast of Stephen, 26 December. The legend behind it is a story about Saint Wenceslaus I, Duke of Bohemia (907–935). Wenceslas was held to be a martyr and a saint immediately after his death, and within a few decades four biographies of him were in circulation. These helped to shape the medieval idea of the rex iustus, the "righteous king" whose power rests on his piety; a preacher of the 12th century wrote that Wenceslas rose every night, barefoot and with a single chamberlain, to go round the churches and give alms to widows, orphans and prisoners. In his lifetime he was only a duke. The Holy Roman Emperor Otto I conferred the royal dignity and title on him after his death, which is why the legend and the song call him a king.

The tune is far older than the words. It belongs to "Tempus adest floridum" ("The Blooming Time Is Here"), a spring carol of the 13th century, first published in 1582 in Piae Cantiones, a Finnish book of seventy-four songs compiled by Jacobus Finno, headmaster of Turku Cathedral School, and published by Theodoric Petri. A text that begins in much the same way is found in the German manuscript collection Carmina Burana. The book was unknown in England until, in or around 1853, G. J. R. Gordon, the British envoy and minister in Stockholm, gave a rare copy of the 1582 edition to John Mason Neale, Warden of Sackville College, East Grinstead, and to the Reverend Thomas Helmore.

Neale belonged to the Tractarian Oxford Movement and wished to bring Catholic ceremony, saints' days and music back into the Anglican church; the gift let him use medieval melodies for new Anglican hymns. The matter of his carol came from a poem by the Czech author Václav Alois Svoboda, published in Prague in 1847. In 1849 Neale had already told the legend of Saint Wenceslas and his page in prose, in Deeds of Faith: Stories for Children from Church History. In 1853 he turned that story into a poem of five eight-line stanzas and, with Helmore as music editor, fitted it to the tune from Piae Cantiones, adding the reference to Saint Stephen's Day. It first appeared that year in Carols for Christmas-Tide, published by Novello & Co. Its words have nothing to do with those of the spring carol.

That substitution has been criticised ever since. H. J. L. J. Massé wrote in 1921 that there was nothing traditional in it as a carol. The editors of the Oxford Book of Carols of 1928, Percy Dearmer, Martin Shaw and Ralph Vaughan Williams, called it a "rather confused narrative" that owed its popularity to the tune, repeated earlier critics' charge of "doggerel", and hoped that it would pass into disuse and the tune be given back to spring. Elizabeth Poston, in the Penguin Book of Christmas Carols, thought Neale's moral verse ill matched to a light dance measure. Others have defended it: Brian Scott wrote that the Oxford editors were wrong, and Jeremy Summerly and Nicolas Bell have argued that the verse "just works".

The carol did not pass into disuse. It has been recorded many times, from Bing Crosby in 1949 and the Beatles' Christmas Record to versions by Mannheim Steamroller (1984), R.E.M., Loreena McKennitt (1995) and many others, and William Lloyd Webber included it among his Songs without Words. The same tune also carries the Christmas hymn "Mary Gently Laid Her Child" by Joseph S. Cook (1859–1933).
== meaning
A story in verse, told partly as a conversation between a king and his page. Looking out on a bitter winter night, the king sees a poor man gathering fuel and sets out with his page to carry him food, drink and firewood; when the page falters in the cold, he is able to go on by walking in the king's footprints. The carol closes by urging the rich and powerful to help the poor. The mood is brisk and warm-hearted.
