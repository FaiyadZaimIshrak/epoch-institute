== story
"Die Gedanken sind frei" ("Thoughts Are Free") is a traditional German song about freedom of thought. The idea in its title is old: it was voiced in antiquity and again in the Middle Ages by the poets Walther von der Vogelweide, Dietmar von Aist and Freidank. The song itself first appeared on leaflets around 1780, with four verses to which a fifth was later added. An early version, a dialogue between a prisoner in a tower and his beloved, was printed as "Song of the Persecuted in the Tower, after Swiss songs" in Achim von Arnim and Clemens Brentano's folk-poetry collection Des Knaben Wunderhorn, about 1805. The words and the tune appear together in Lieder der Brienzer Mädchen, printed in Bern between 1810 and 1820. Hoffmann von Fallersleben took it into his collection of Silesian folk songs in 1842, as a song from Neukirch bei Schönau, and his version became the most popular.

From the time of the Carlsbad Decrees and the age of Metternich, it became a popular protest song against political repression and censorship, above all among the banned student fraternities, the Burschenschaften. After the failed German revolution of 1848 the song was banned. In 1898 Gustav Mahler set the Wunderhorn text for voice and orchestra.

Under the Nazi dictatorship it mattered to parts of the German resistance. In 1935 the guards at the Lichtenburg concentration camp ordered prisoners to put on a performance for Hitler's 46th birthday, and the imprisoned lawyer Hans Litten recited "Die Gedanken sind frei" in answer. In 1942 Sophie Scholl of the White Rose resistance group played it on her flute outside the walls of Ulm prison, where her father Robert was held for calling Hitler a "scourge of God". The American singer Pete Seeger recorded an adaptation in 1966.
== meaning
A song of inner freedom: no prison or ruler can reach what a person thinks. Its mood is calm and quietly defiant.
