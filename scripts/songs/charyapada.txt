== story
The Charyapada is a collection of mystical poems, songs of realisation in the tantric Vajrayana tradition of Buddhism from Assam, Bengal, Bihar and Odisha. It was compiled between about 1000 and 1200 CE in late Apabhraṃśa, or the Abahaṭṭha dialects, at a time when the north-eastern Prakrits had not yet divided, or were only beginning to divide, into later languages. Scholars of Assamese, Bengali, Magahi, Maithili and Odia all find features of their languages in it, and the songs are claimed as early literature in each: Luipa was from Kamarupa, and Sarahapa is said to have come from Rani near present-day Guwahati; Rahul Sankrityayan held that most of the poets were from Bihar and wrote an early Magahi, and K. P. Jayaswal took the language for an early Maithili. The dating is debated. Haraprasad Shastri placed the songs in the 10th century, Suniti Kumar Chatterji and Prabodh Chandra Bagchi between the 10th and 12th, Sukumar Sen possibly as late as the 14th, while Muhammad Shahidullah put them between the 7th and 11th and Sankrityayan between the 8th and 11th.

The songs were composed by 22 of the Mahasiddhas, or Siddhacharyas, each named at the head of his pada (verse), probably by the commentator Munidatta, who wrote a Sanskrit commentary on them; the raga to which each song was to be sung is named before it. Shastri, who rediscovered a palm-leaf manuscript in the Nepal Royal Court Library in the early 20th century, called their riddling idiom "twilight language" (sandhya-bhasha), "half-expressed and half-concealed", after Munidatta's commentary; Vidhushekhara Shastri later named it "intentional language" (sandha-bhasha). Shastri published the manuscript's 47 padas with the commentary through the Bangiya Sahitya Parishad in 1916, as part of his Hajar Bacharer Purano Bangala Bhasay Bauddhagan O Doha ("Buddhist Songs and Couplets"), under the title Charyacharyavinishchayah. Its title page, colophon and several leaves, with padas 24, 25 and 48, are lost; the manuscript is now in the National Archives of Nepal.

The songs were also kept in the Tibetan Buddhist canon. Prabodh Chandra Bagchi published a Tibetan translation of 50 verses, which supplies the missing padas, among them pada 25 by the poet Tantripāda, whose work had been lost, and the whole of pada 23. Tibetan tradition assigns the translation of the songs to Vairocanavajra, a 12th-century monk of Nalanda; the Tibetan text names Shilachari as translator of the songs and Chandrakirti or Kirtichandra as translator of Munidatta's commentary, the Charyagiti-koshavrtti. Some of the ragas the manuscript names are extinct, and some may be other names for ragas still known today.
== meaning
Songs of the Vajrayana path to realisation, written in a deliberately veiled language: everyday scenes and figures carry a hidden spiritual sense meant for the initiated, so that each song works as a riddle about the nature of mind and liberation.
