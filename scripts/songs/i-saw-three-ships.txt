== story
"I Saw Three Ships (Come Sailing In)" is an English Christmas carol, number 700 in the Roud Folk Song Index. The earliest printed version is from the 17th century, possibly from Derbyshire. The song was probably known traditionally as "As I Sat On a Sunny Bank", and it was particularly popular in Cornwall. The words sung today are those of a version of nine verses published in 1833 by the English lawyer and antiquarian William Sandys.

The carol has the ships sail into Bethlehem, although the nearest body of water is the Dead Sea, about 20 miles (32 km) away. Several explanations have been offered. The three ships are thought to go back to those that carried the purported relics of the Biblical Magi to Cologne Cathedral in the 12th century. Another possible reference is to King Wenceslaus II of Bohemia, whose coat of arms showed three silver galleys on blue. A third suggestion is that the ships are the camels of the Magi, since camels are often called "ships of the desert".

Countless traditional versions have been collected, sung to many different melodies, as is usual with folk songs and carols. In the 1910s the English folklorists Cecil Sharp and Janet Blunt noted the tunes and words of dozens of versions, mainly in the south of England. In the early 1930s the American collector James Madison Carpenter recorded several slightly different English versions, several of them in Cornwall and one in Gloucestershire, and in 1956 Peter Kennedy recorded a man named John Thomas singing it in Camborne, Cornwall. A 19th-century version has the three ships come sailing by on New Year's Day, with three pretty girls aboard who entertain at a wedding held that day.

The song also crossed the Atlantic. Jean Ritchie, a musician from the Appalachian Mountains, was recorded by Alan Lomax in 1949 singing a version learned from her Kentucky family, whose ancestors seemingly brought it from England; she recorded it again on her 1959 album Carols of All Seasons. Hers is the only traditional American version to have been recorded, though the song was known in the United States in earlier decades, particularly in the south. In 1952, collecting in the British Isles with her husband George Pickow, Ritchie met the Irish traditional singer Elizabeth Cronin in Macroom, County Cork, who sang a version called "The Bells of Heaven".

The carol is a staple of choirs. An arrangement by Martin Shaw appears in The Oxford Book of Carols, and the Carols for Choirs books have arrangements by Sir David Willcocks and by John Rutter. Simon Preston and Sir Philip Ledger wrote arrangements that the Choir of King's College, Cambridge, has sung at the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols. It has also been recorded by popular musicians, among them Nat King Cole in 1960, Jon Anderson in 1985 and Sting in 1997, and is used as a counterpoint in the musical Caroline, or Change.
== meaning
The singer sees three ships sail in on Christmas morning, carrying Christ and his mother to Bethlehem, and calls on bells, angels and all people to rejoice. It is a bright, dancing carol built on repeated lines.
