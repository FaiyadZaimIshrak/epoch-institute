== story
In 1795 the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth was partitioned for the third time by Russia, Prussia and Austria and erased from the map; the partitioning monarchs pledged never to use the name "Poland" in their titles. Many, even leaders of the Polish Enlightenment, took it as the end of the nation: Hugo Kołłątaj wrote that Poland "no longer belonged to currently extant nations". Polish patriots turned to France, then at war with Austria. In 1796 Józef Wybicki, a leading moderate émigré politician, proposed Polish Legions within the French Revolutionary Army and persuaded General Jan Henryk Dąbrowski, a hero of the 1794 Kościuszko Uprising, to put the plan to the French Directory. In January 1797 the Cisalpine Republic accepted Dąbrowski's offer, and a legion was formed to fight Austria under Napoleon and then march from Italy to Poland to raise a national uprising.

Wybicki reached the Legions' quarters at Reggio Emilia in early July 1797 and wrote the "Song of the Polish Legions in Italy" between 16 and 19 July. He first sang it at a private meeting of officers in the episcopal palace; its first public performance was most probably at a military parade in the cathedral square on 16 July, and it was played again on 20 July as the Legions marched to Milan. Its idea, new in the age of early nationalism, was that a nation could exist without a state of its own as long as its people lived and fought for it. It names military models: Dąbrowski, Napoleon, the 17th-century hetman Stefan Czarniecki and Kościuszko. The melody is a lively mazurka, most probably Wybicki's adaptation of a folk tune popular in the later 18th century; its composer is not known. It was long wrongly credited to Michał Kleofas Ogiński, until his march for the Legions was found in 1938 and proved to be a different piece.

The song caught on quickly with officers, mostly émigré nobles, and with ordinary soldiers, most of them Galician peasants captured from the Austrian army. Dąbrowski wrote to Wybicki on 29 August 1797 that the soldiers liked it more and more. The Legions themselves never marched home: France used them against uprisings in Italy and Germany and later in Haiti, where war and disease destroyed them. When Dąbrowski and Wybicki arrived in Poznań on 6 November 1806, crowds greeted them with the song, and it became one of the most popular patriotic songs of the Duchy of Warsaw. Its words kept changing: after the Franco-Russian alliance at Tilsit in 1807 the stanza naming Russia as Poland's enemy was dropped, and so was the stanza on Kościuszko, who had refused to support Napoleon.

Through a century of foreign rule after 1815 it was sung in every national uprising; in the November Uprising of 1830–1831 it was chanted on the battlefields, and at least 16 new versions were written. Dąbrowski's name was at times replaced by later leaders, from Józef Chłopicki to Józef Piłsudski and Władysław Sikorski. The Great Emigration carried it to Western Europe. In the partitioned lands it became risky to sing: Prussia banned Polish patriotic songs in 1850, and between 1873 and 1911 German courts passed 20 sentences for singing this one; under Russian rule public performances often ended with police intervention. Adam Mickiewicz placed it twice in Pan Tadeusz (1834), and in 1842 he explained its opening idea to his students in Paris.

After Poland regained independence in 1918 the song became the de facto anthem of the Second Polish Republic and was officially adopted in 1927, ahead of rivals such as "Boże, coś Polskę", "Warszawianka" and "Rota". At the inauguration of the United Nations in 1945, to which no Polish delegation had been invited, the pianist Artur Rubinstein opened his concert by protesting its absence and playing the anthem loudly and slowly; President Aleksander Kwaśniewski recalled this in 2005. Today it is one of three national symbols defined by the Polish constitution, played in F major on public occasions in Kazimierz Sikorski's arrangement, and broadcast every midnight on Polish Radio's First Programme.
== meaning
A song of hope and resolve: the nation survives in its people even when it has no state of its own. It calls the soldiers to fight under Dąbrowski to win back what foreign powers have taken and to march home, holding up past heroes as models and picturing the families waiting for their return. The mood is brisk and confident, carried by a dance rhythm.
