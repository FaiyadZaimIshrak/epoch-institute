== story
"It's a Long Way to Tipperary" is an English music hall song, credited to Jack Judge and Harry Williams. Judge, whose parents were Irish and whose grandparents came from Tipperary, was a popular semi-professional music hall performer; Williams, severely disabled since a childhood fall, wrote verse and songs and played the piano and mandolin. The two met at the Malt Shovel in Oldbury, Worcestershire, and wrote 32 music hall songs together, many at Williams's home, The Plough Inn in Balsall Common. Judge could not read or write music, and Williams taught him the tunes by ear. In January 1912, performing at the Grand Theatre in Stalybridge, Judge accepted a five-shilling bet that he could compose and sing a new song by the next night, and on 31 January he sang "Tipperary" for the first time. It was written as a sentimental ballad for Irish people living in London. Bert Feldman published it in October 1912 as "It's a Long, Long Way to Tipperary" and promoted it as a march.

The authorship has long been disputed. After Williams's death in 1924 Judge claimed sole credit, saying that Williams's name had been added to repay a debt. Williams's family showed that the tune and most of the words already existed in a manuscript of 1909, "It's a Long Way to Connemara", by the two men, and in 2012 they campaigned to have Williams credited again; the family estate still receives royalties. In 1917 Alice Smyth Burton Jay sued the publishers Chappell & Co., claiming the tune came from a song of hers for the 1909 Alaska–Yukon–Pacific Exposition; with Victor Herbert as expert adviser, the court dismissed the suit in 1920.

Florrie Forde sang the song in 1913 but dropped it from her act; it was nevertheless the hit of that year. When the First World War began, British soldiers sang it on the march to Mons, and the Daily Mail correspondent George Curnock reported on 18 August 1914 that he had seen the Connaught Rangers singing it through Boulogne on 13 August. Newspapers printed the chorus, and the song became a patriotic favourite of civilians. John McCormack's recording of November 1914 spread it worldwide. Commentators held up its cheerful, unmilitary tone against the songs of the enemy armies, but as the war dragged on many soldiers came to dislike it: the veteran and anthologist F. T. Nettleingham wrote that it was "never Tommy's song", and the gap between the civilians who called it the soldiers' anthem and the men at the front became part of its history. In 1916 survivors of HMS Tipperary, sunk at the Battle of Jutland, were identified by their rescuers because they were singing it in their lifeboat, and in 1942 British prisoners trapped in the sinking Japanese ship Lisbon Maru are reported to have sung it.

The song remained the sound of that war in later memory. It is heard in Jean Renoir's La Grande Illusion (1937), Oh! What a Lovely War, Gallipoli (1981) and Das Boot (1981), and it forms part of the regimental march of Princess Patricia's Canadian Light Infantry. Its tune was reused for labour songs, university fight songs and the hymns of football clubs in Argentina and Brazil. Signs in the town of Tipperary in Ireland greet visitors with a joke on its title.
== meaning
A lament of an Irish worker in London who misses his homeland. Unlike the popular songs of earlier wars, such as the Second Boer War, it calls for no glorious deeds and dwells instead on the longing for home.

== notes
Status pd: published 1912, so public domain in the United States; Harry Williams died in 1924. The article does not give Jack Judge's death year, so the status outside the US is not settled by the source.
Authorship: Judge's own account (1933 interview) places the writing in the Newmarket Tavern, Stalybridge, on 31 January 1912; the family's 1909 "Connemara" manuscript contradicts the one-night story. I give both.
Alice Smyth Burton Jay's song: I did not name the opening words of its chorus, which the article quotes, nor any words of the Tickle Mary parody.
The quotation "never Tommy's song" is Nettleingham's, as the article gives it; the editor may prefer a paraphrase. The article names no work of his, so he is not in sources.
Mons and Boulogne: the article says soldiers "marching to Mons" sang it, without date.
Left out: the many television uses (Mary Tyler Moore Show, Peanuts, Mystery Science Theater 3000, The Simpsons, Archer, a game mod).
