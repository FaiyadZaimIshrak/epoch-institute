== story
The Palästinalied ("Palestine Song") is a song written in the early 13th century by Walther von der Vogelweide, the most celebrated lyric poet of Middle High German literature. It was written at the time of the Fifth Crusade (1217-1221). It is one of the few songs by Walther for which a melody has survived, and that melody has been suggested to be a contrafactum, a new text set to an existing tune, of the 12th-century troubadour Jaufre Rudel's song Lanquan li jorn.

The song tells the Christian gospel from the point of view of a pilgrim setting foot in the Holy Land. Its closing stanza turns to the crusades themselves: it notes that Christians, Jews and Muslims (the "heathens" of the text) all claim the land, and declares the Christian claim the just one, asking God to grant it.

The words survive in several medieval manuscripts that do not agree on their length. The oldest is the Kleine Heidelberger Liederhandschrift (manuscript A, c. 1270), with seven stanzas; the Codex Manesse (manuscript C, c. 1304) has nine, and other manuscripts add four more, for thirteen distinct stanzas in all. One of these is clearly later than the original, and scholars do not agree whether the other five not found in manuscript A are Walther's. The melody is first written down in the Münster fragment (manuscript Z), about a century after Walther's death. The Carmina Burana manuscript (c. 1230) already gives the first stanza with neumes, though too few to rebuild the tune, as an appendix to the Latin song Alte clamat Epicurus, which was to be sung to the same melody.

From the 1970s, and especially with the rise of medieval rock, neofolk and related styles in the late 1980s and 1990s, the Palästinalied became a staple of such music and is now well known to modern audiences, chiefly through German bands such as Ougenweide (1974), Corvus Corax (1991), Qntal (1995), Estampie (1996) and In Extremo (1998). A 2002 compilation, Palästinalied-Projekt, gathers twenty performances, and the first movement of Lou Harrison's String Quartet Set takes much of its melody from the song.
== meaning
The song speaks as a pilgrim who has at last reached the land where Christ lived, and it retells the gospel story as bound to that land. Its mood is of awe and fulfilment. Its close moves from devotion to the politics of the crusades, asserting the Christian right to the land against the other faiths that claim it.
