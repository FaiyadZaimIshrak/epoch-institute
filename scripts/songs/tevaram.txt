== story
The Tevaram is the name given to the first seven volumes of the Tirumurai, the twelve-volume canon of Tamil Shaivism: 796 hymns of 8,284 stanzas by the three most prominent Shaiva poet-saints, Sambandar, Appar and Sundarar. The "Tevaram trio" are counted among the 63 Nayanars, the revered poet-saints of Shiva. Appar and Sambandar lived around the 7th century and Sundarar in the late 7th or early 8th, though scholars' dates range from the 6th to the 9th. In the Pallava period they travelled across the Tamil country, singing in temples and public places and drawing communities of believers into an emotional devotion to Shiva, at a time when Hindus, Jains and Buddhists competed for royal and urban patronage. Their work is a chief source for the Shaiva bhakti movement of early medieval South India. The name may be read as "the garland of the lord" or as "create love towards the lord".

The first three volumes are Sambandar's: 383 or 384 hymns, all that survive of a reputed 16,000. Born to a family of Shaiva Brahmins at Sirkazhi, he is remembered in the hagiographies as a child prodigy who composed hymns as soon as he could speak, travelled with the musician Tiru Nilakanta Yazhpanar, who set his verses to the yal, and was called to Madurai by Queen Mangayarkkarasiyar to contest the Jain monks at the Pandya court; he died at about sixteen, around 655 CE. Appar, of a Vellala peasant family, turned to Jainism and headed a Jain monastery under the name Dharmasena before his sister Thilagavathiar brought him back to Shiva; his first hymn was sung at the temple of Atikai, and his three volumes hold 313 hymns, of the 3,130 stanzas that survive out of 4,900. It was Sambandar who called him Appar, "father". Sundarar, the third, was adopted by a Pallava feudatory family; his 100 hymns, the seventh volume, tell of his marriages to the temple dancers Paravai and Cankali, his blindness after a broken vow and the return of his sight. The hagiographies of all three, above all the Periya Puranam, are full of miracles, and how far they can be relied on, even for the saints' centuries, is disputed.

Each hymn, or patikam, has ten or eleven four-line stanzas with a refrain, and a closing signature verse. The hymns are arranged by the 23 panns to which they are sung, to a stringed instrument such as the Tamil yal; professional singing of them at the great Shiva temples has been a Tamil tradition since at least the 11th century. They name the places where Shiva was praised: the 275 Paadal Petra Sthalams, the temples their verses revere, still count among the great Shiva shrines, and the hymns drew a sacred geography that tied the Tamil Shaivas to Shaivism across the subcontinent. They praise the Vedas and Sanskrit along with Tamil, and the Madras Tamil Lexicon calls the Tevaram a "Tamil Veda". Inscriptions show the hymns sung in temples by the 8th century, one of them of Nandivarman II, and later kings made provision for singers of patikams.

The hymns lived largely by oral tradition until the 10th century. Tradition holds that Rajaraja I of the Chola dynasty (985–1013 CE), hearing short excerpts at his court, set out to recover them, and that the priest Nambiyandar Nambi found the palm leaves, half eaten by white ants, in a chamber of the Chidambaram Nataraja temple. Nambi arranged the trio's hymns as the first seven books of the canon and set their musical modes with the help of a woman of the Panar caste from the village of Tiru Nilakanta Yazhpanar. Rajaraja appointed 48 singers, the pidarars, and became known as Tirumurai Kanda Cholan, "one who saved the Tirumurai"; a record of Rajendra I names a supervisor of the Tevaram. From the 13th century the monasteries passed the hymns on to the odhuvars, singers of the Vellala community trained in Tevaram schools, and in 1918 eleven more songs were found engraved in a stone temple at Tiruvidavayil. Odhuvars still sing the Tevaram in the Shiva temples of Tamil Nadu after the daily rituals.
== meaning
Hymns of personal devotion to Shiva: praise of the god at named temples and holy places, longing, repentance and the joy of the devotee, with a signature verse telling who sang the hymn, why, or what singing it brings. Sambandar's verses are ardent and inventive in metre, Appar's simple and emotional with images drawn from farming life, and Sundarar's warm and at times humorous.
