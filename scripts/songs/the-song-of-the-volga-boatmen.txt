== story
"The Song of the Volga Boatmen" is a traditional Russian song of the burlaks, the barge-haulers of the Volga River, who dragged boats upstream from the bank. In Russian it is named after its refrain, a hauling call. Ilya Repin's painting Barge Haulers on the Volga shows such burlaks at their labour in Tsarist Russia.

The composer Mily Balakirev wrote the song down from Nikolay Aleynikov in Nizhny Novgorod in 1860 or 1861, and published it with his own arrangement in his collection of Russian folk songs in 1866. He printed only one verse, the first; the other two were added later. The first released recording was probably made in Russia in 1900 by Alexander Makarov-Yunev for Gramophone.

The bass Feodor Chaliapin made it famous, in an arrangement by Feodor Koenemann; his version, released several times (1922, 1927, 1936), became one of Russia's most popular songs, and it has been a concert favourite of bass singers ever since. Composers took it up as well. Alexander Glazunov wrote a piece on Balakirev's tune in 1905 and used it for a theme of his symphonic poem Stenka Razin, and Max Bruch made it the last movement of his Suite on Russian Folk Melodies. In April 1917, after Tsar Nicholas II had abdicated, Sergei Diaghilev asked Igor Stravinsky to orchestrate it for wind instruments to open a concert that would otherwise have begun with the anthem "God Save the Tsar"; Stravinsky worked through the night, helped by Lord Berners and Ernest Ansermet. Manuel de Falla's arrangement was published in 1922 at the request of the diplomat Ricardo Baeza, who was working with the League of Nations to relieve the more than two million Russian refugees displaced and imprisoned during the First World War; all proceeds went to that relief.

Abroad the song travelled through singers such as Vladimir Rosing and Paul Robeson, who sang it in translation. In 1941 the Glenn Miller Orchestra released a swing arrangement by Bill Finegan that reached No. 1 on the Billboard pop chart. Because the song was not in copyright, it was not caught by that year's ASCAP boycott, which gave it more radio play. Its opening notes have since been used in films, cartoons and video games, often to suggest heavy toil or gloom, and sometimes with new humorous words.
== meaning
A song for hauling: its slow, even beat lets a team of men pull together on the towline, and its refrain is the call on which they heave. The mood is one of weight and endurance under hard labour on the river.

Status pd fits: traditional words, one verse printed 1866, and the article says the song was "not in copyright" in 1941. The two later verses were "added at a later date" by an unnamed hand; the editor should check that whatever text is imported is the old one. The article does not explain how the burlaks worked beyond "barge-haulers" and Repin's painting; "dragged boats upstream from the bank" and "towline" in story and meaning are my gloss of barge-hauling and should be checked or cut. The Russian title is the refrain; I did not translate it, keeping to the no-lyrics rule (the article gives "Yo, heave-ho!"). Types: work only. Max Bruch's suite and Glazunov's Stenka Razin have no years in the article. Kids: yes; it is about hard labour, not violence. Left out: Leonid Kharitonov's 1965 recording, the Boston Pops' 1937 recording, Perestroika (game), the "Birthday Dirge". Balakirev's 1866 book is named in the article as the first publication, not as a cited reference; the editor may drop it from sources.
