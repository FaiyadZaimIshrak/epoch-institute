== story
After the stained glass of the parish church of Roquemaure in France was restored, the local priest, Maurice Gilles, asked Placide Cappeau to write a new text for the coming Christmas Midnight Mass. Cappeau wrote the poem, "Minuit, chrétiens", on 3 December 1847 during a stagecoach journey from Mâcon to Dijon. It draws on the Nativity of Jesus and reflects his social and abolitionist views. Through his connection with the singer Emily Laurey he was introduced to the composer Adolphe Adam, then known for works such as Giselle, who set it to music within days. Cappeau called the finished work "Cantique de Noël", and it was first performed at Midnight Mass in Roquemaure in 1847. The first form of its opening stanza cast the Jews in a negative light against the Christians; it was replaced, in a change described as a "happy correction".

The song spread quickly. An organ arrangement was published around 1855, and by 1864 the Catholic music journal Revue de Musique Sacrée reported it sung in churches at Midnight Mass and also in the streets, at social gatherings and in bars. After the revolution of 1848 the poet Alphonse de Lamartine called it "a religious Marseillaise", a comparison that troubled some observers. Church leaders criticised its militant tone and its theology, objecting in particular to its picture of an angry Father, and it was excluded from Catholic hymnals. Much of the criticism was aimed at its makers: Cappeau for his politics, his reputation and his later break with Christianity, and Adam through false rumours that he was Jewish, repeated as late as 1930 in an article by the composer Vincent d'Indy. In 1864 the same journal called it "debased and degenerated". After the Second World War the composer Auguste Sérieyx still condemned those who performed it, and by 1956 it had been removed from many dioceses.

Abroad it fared differently. The Canadian composer and folklorist Ernest Gagnon heard it at a Midnight Mass at the Church of Saint-Roch and introduced it to Canada in 1858, with a performance in Quebec by the eldest daughter of René-Édouard Caron; solo performances at Midnight Mass became a tradition there. On 22 September 1936 Archbishop Joseph-Guillaume-Laurent Forbes banned it from the churches of the Archdiocese of Ottawa, along with other music, to promote Gregorian chant. In the United States the critic and minister John Sullivan Dwight, influenced by Ralph Waldo Emerson, wrote the best-known English version in 1855, changing the French text well beyond a direct translation. As an abolitionist he admired the song's emphasis on human equality; its themes resonated during the American Civil War, and his Dwight's Journal of Music helped make it popular in the North. Another English version, "O Night Divine", was made by Hart Pease Danks in the late 19th century.

Accounts have gathered around it. The inventor Reginald Fessenden's violin performance of it in 1906 is often called the first song broadcast on radio, though this is not formally documented, and a legend holds that French troops sang it on Christmas Eve 1870 in the Franco-Prussian War, bringing a brief ceasefire. The carol historian William Studwell called it the most popular carol in France. In French and Canadian tradition it is sung as a solo at Midnight Mass, and listeners wait to hear whether the singer will reach its high climax. It has been recorded in many genres, and a 2010 survey by Zogby International ranked it the most popular Christmas song in the United States.
== meaning
A Christmas hymn that hails the midnight of Christ's birth as the hour of humanity's redemption. Later verses turn to his teaching of love and to the hope that oppression and slavery will end. The mood is solemn and rises to a triumphant close.
