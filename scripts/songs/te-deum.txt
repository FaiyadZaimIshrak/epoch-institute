== story
The Te Deum is a Latin Christian hymn of praise, named from its opening words. Tradition ascribes it to Saint Ambrose (died 397) or Saint Augustine (died 430), and it is sometimes called the Ambrosian Hymn, though Ambrose's authorship is unlikely. Scholars of the 19th century proposed Hilary of Poitiers and Nicetas of Remesiana; manuscripts from the 10th century on link the hymn with the name Nicetas, a case defended in the 1890s by Germain Morin, but hymnologists of the 20th century, especially Ernst Kähler (1958), showed that link to be spurious. The hymn almost certainly dates from the 4th century and its author is unknown. Its structure resembles a eucharistic prayer, and it has been proposed that it began as part of one.

The hymn was central to the Ambrosian hymnal, which spread through the Latin Church with the rite of Milan between the 6th and 8th centuries. It entered the Benedictine order's Old Hymnal in the 6th century and was kept in the Frankish Hymnal of the 8th, dropped from the New Hymnal that prevailed from the 10th century, and restored in the 12th. In the traditional office it is sung at the end of Matins on Sundays and feasts outside the penitential seasons; since the reforms of Pope Paul VI it closes the Office of Readings on most Sundays, solemnities and feasts. It is also sung in Anglican Morning Prayer, by Lutherans, Methodists and many Reformed churches.

The Te Deum is the core of a short service of thanksgiving, often held at short notice for good news: a military victory, the signing of a peace treaty, a royal birth or coronation, the election of a pope or the canonisation of a saint. In Sweden such a service may be held in the Royal Chapel for royal births and jubilees; in Belgium one is held every 21 July, the national day, the date on which Leopold I officially became king of the Belgians; in Luxembourg one marks the Grand Duke's Official Birthday; and Argentina, Chile and Peru hold one on their national days.

The hymn was first sung to a Gregorian chant melody, and many composers have set it since: Lully, who was fatally injured while conducting his setting for the court of Louis XIV, Charpentier, Handel, Haydn, Mozart, Berlioz, Verdi, Bruckner, Dvořák and Britten among them. The prelude of Charpentier's setting H.146 became the theme music of the European Broadcasting Union's Eurovision broadcasts. William Walton wrote his Coronation Te Deum for the coronation of Elizabeth II in 1953, and Puccini set its opening at the end of Act I of Tosca. The 18th-century German hymn Großer Gott, wir loben dich is a free version of it, sung in English as "Holy God, We Praise Thy Name".
== meaning
A hymn of praise that follows the outline of the Apostles' Creed. It names all who praise God, from the ranks of heaven to the Church on earth, recalls the life, death, resurrection and glory of Christ, and ends with petitions for mercy and protection drawn from the Psalms, which were added to the original hymn later.
