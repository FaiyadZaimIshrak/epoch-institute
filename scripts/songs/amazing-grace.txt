== story
John Newton was born in Wapping, London, in 1725 and went to sea at eleven. Pressed into the Royal Navy, he deserted, was traded as crew to a slave ship and took up slave trading. For a time he was himself enslaved by the Sherbro people of present-day Sierra Leone and forced to work on a plantation. In March 1748, aboard the Greyhound in the North Atlantic, a violent storm nearly sank the ship, and Newton called out to God for mercy. He marked this as his conversion, but it was not immediate: he went on in the slave trade for several more voyages, now as a captain, buying people in African ports and carrying them to North America, holding the same view of slavery as his contemporaries. He stopped sailing after collapsing at about thirty.

Newton taught himself theology while working as a customs agent in Liverpool and was ordained in the Church of England in 1764 as curate of Olney, Buckinghamshire, a poor lace-making village. There he and the poet William Cowper wrote hymns for weekly prayer meetings. Newton wrote this one in late 1772 to illustrate a New Year's Day sermon, probably first used on 1 January 1773, on 1 Chronicles 17:16–17. It is not known whether it had any music; it may have been chanted. It appeared in 1779 in Olney Hymns, to which Newton gave 280 of 348 texts. After leaving Olney in the 1780s Newton became an outspoken abolitionist and worked with William Wilberforce, whose campaign led to the Slave Trade Act 1807. Some scholars read the hymn's first stanza as Newton's reckoning with his part in the slave trade, though Newton is not known to have connected the hymn to anti-slavery views.

The hymn stayed obscure in England but spread in the United States, where four versions were printed between 1789 and 1799 and it was sung at the camp meetings of the Second Great Awakening. More than twenty tunes circulated until 1835, when William Walker set the words to "New Britain" in his shape-note tunebook Southern Harmony, which sold about 600,000 copies. Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) has Tom sing it, with a verse not by Newton that had been passed down orally in African American communities. The Cherokee sang a translation by Samuel Worcester on the Trail of Tears, and it was printed in hymnals given to soldiers in the Civil War. Edwin Othello Excell's arrangement, published between 1900 and 1910, became the standard form in American churches.

Its first recording was by the Sacred Harp Choir in 1922. Mahalia Jackson's 1947 recording was widely broadcast, and during the civil rights movement she sang it for marchers; in 1964 Fannie Lou Hamer led marchers in Mississippi singing it. Judy Collins recorded it in 1970 in opposition to the Vietnam War, and it reached number 15 on the Billboard Hot 100; an instrumental by the Royal Scots Dragoon Guards spent five weeks at number one in the UK in 1972. Writers have called it an emblematic black spiritual, sung as an expression of deliverance from slavery. It is sung at funerals and memorials, and in 2015 President Barack Obama sang it at the memorial service for Clementa Pinckney, one of the nine people killed in the Charleston church shooting.
== meaning
A first-person hymn of thanks: the singer looks back on a life of wrongdoing and despair and credits his rescue to God's undeserved mercy. It moves from confession to relief and then to trust that this mercy will carry him through future hardship and into eternity. It draws on the Gospel stories of the prodigal son and of a blind man healed.
