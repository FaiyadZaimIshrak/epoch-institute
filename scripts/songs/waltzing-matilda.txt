== story
In 1895 Andrew Barton Paterson, a Sydney solicitor who published verse as "The Banjo", travelled to Winton in central-western Queensland to visit his fiancée Sarah Riley. At a gathering there Christina Macpherson, a school friend of Sarah's, played a tune that caught his attention, and he wrote a first verse to it on the spot. She had heard it at the Warrnambool races in Victoria on 24 April 1894: the march "Craigielee" by the English-born Australian Thomas Bulch, whose first strain came from James Barr's 1818 setting of "Thou Bonnie Wood o Craigielee" by the Paisley weaver-poet Robert Tannahill. Christina picked it out by ear on a piano, imperfectly, and in doing so gave it the AABA form in which it is still sung; at her family's Dagworth Station, which had no piano, she played it on an autoharp. The rest of the song was written over three or four weeks in August and early September 1895, at Dagworth and nearby, and was sung at Winton and at Oondooroo Station, where Herbert Ramsay performed it dressed as a swagman. Paterson's notebook draft and Christina's manuscripts survive in the National Library of Australia.

The words tell of a swagman, an itinerant worker, who takes a sheep and dies in a billabong rather than be caught by the squatter and the troopers. The year before, during the renewed shearers' strike of 1894, striking shearers had burned the woolshed at Dagworth on 2 September, and the same day the union leader Samuel Hoffmeister was found shot dead at a billabong near Kynuna, a death the inquest ruled suicide and that has been questioned since. Historians such as Ross Fitzgerald see the strike behind the song; others, among them Peter and Sheila Forrest in 2008, argue that Paterson wrote it as part of a flirtation with Christina Macpherson and that the strike reading is a later political appropriation. Claims that Paterson reworked an older bush song have no evidence behind them, and a supposed source in an Irish jig shows no resemblance. A belief that the song was first sung on 6 April 1895 is wrong.

The song spread slowly at first. Harry Nathan published the first setting, with altered words and a new variation of the tune, on 20 December 1902. On 12 January 1903 Paterson sold the rights, with other pieces, to Angus & Robertson for five pounds; the tea merchant James Inglis bought them soon after and asked Marie Cowan, wife of one of his staff, to turn the song into an advertising jingle for Billy Tea. Her version, published in 1903 with a few more changes to the words and a brisk accompaniment, became the best-known form of the song. A different "Queensland version", keeping Paterson's words to another tune of unknown origin, is sung by folk singers. The first recording was made in 1926 by John Collinson and Russell Callow.

"Waltzing Matilda" has never been Australia's official anthem, but from 1976 to 1984 it was one of the "national songs" allowed in place of "God Save the Queen" at civilian functions. In the 1977 plebiscite on a national song it came second with about 28%, behind "Advance Australia Fair". Paul Keating later called it Australia's unofficial national song. It is the quick march of the 1st Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment, and the official song of the US 1st Marine Division, which was stationed in Australia in the Second World War; it is sung at rugby internationals, the women's national soccer team is called the Matildas after it, and since 2003 its words have been printed microscopically in Australian passports. In 2012 Winton held the first Waltzing Matilda Day on 6 April. The National Film and Sound Archive says it has been recorded more often than any other Australian song.
== meaning
A ballad of a wandering bush worker who takes a sheep, is cornered by the landowner and the police, and chooses death in a waterhole over capture; his ghost is said to linger there. It is told lightly, with dry humour, and has been read both as a whimsical ditty and as a story of the poor against the powerful.
