== story
The words of "Umi Yukaba" come from a chōka, a long poem, by Ōtomo no Yakamochi in the Man'yōshū, the 8th-century anthology of Japanese poetry (poem 4094; book 18). The poem celebrates the imperial edict on the discovery of gold in Michinoku Province (present-day Tōhoku) in 749. The distant ancestors of the Ōtomo clan were known as masters of the royal Kume guard, and the passage reflects their pledge to serve their sovereign; the part later set to music corresponds closely to the words the edict itself quotes, as recorded in the Shoku Nihongi. Two endings of the passage survive, one from the poem and one from the edict, and the scholar of the Man'yōshū Nakanishi Susumu suggested that the Ōtomo family may have sung both in answer to each other.

In 1880 Tōgi Sueyoshi, a musician of the Imperial Household Ministry, set the words under the title "Umi Yukaba" (海行かば); his setting can be heard in the trio of the "Warship March" (Gunkan kōshinkyoku), and before the war it served as the salute for general officers. The best-known melody is Kiyoshi Nobutoki's of 1937, commissioned by the Japan Broadcasting Corporation as the theme of the National Spiritual Mobilization Emphasis Week that the government had declared; it was broadcast from 13 to 16 October 1937. The song became popular in the armed forces, especially the Imperial Japanese Navy, and was widely sung during the Second World War, often by kamikaze pilots before take-off.

What fixed the song in people's minds was its use on the radio in the Pacific War: Imperial General Headquarters announcements that reported the annihilation of units fighting to the last always opened with it, though it was also played for the success of the attack on Pearl Harbor; other victories were announced with songs such as "Battōtai" and the "Warship March". It was also sung to send soldiers off and to receive the ashes of the dead, which is why some in Japan regard it as a military song and others as a requiem. In the war years the religious leader Taniguchi Masaharu opposed it, which led to trouble between lecturers of his movement, Seichō-no-Ie, and the Special Higher Police. Yasujirō Ozu used it in the last scene of his film "There Was a Father" (1942); the occupation's censors removed the sound after the war, and the original is known from a print the Soviet army took from Manchuria.

After Japan's surrender in 1945 the Allied occupation banned "Umi Yukaba" with other military songs (gunka). After the occupation it came to be widely played again in military circles, including by the Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force. Its words are used in the Yasukuni no mai, a kagura dance of modern making, and Ōbirin school used its melody for its school song until 1958. Nobutoki's published song collections long left it out, a silence the musician Aikawa Yumi criticised in her writings and the notes to her recordings. The quarrel over its character continues: in 2018 the education board of Yachiyo, Chiba Prefecture, at first withdrew its support from a concert of Japanese songs because the programme included what it called a military song, and in September 2025 the Takarazuka Revue dropped a solo of it from a show after criticism that a military song was unsuitable.
== meaning
A passage from an 8th-century poem expressing the Ōtomo clan's pledge to serve their sovereign; solemn. In the 1930s and 1940s it was used to send soldiers off and to mourn the dead, and it is remembered both as a war song and as a requiem.
