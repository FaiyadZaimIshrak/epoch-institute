== story
"Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika" ("Lord Bless Africa") was composed in 1897 by Enoch Sontonga, a Xhosa teacher at a Methodist mission school near Johannesburg. He wrote it as a hymn of a single stanza in Xhosa, in B-flat major, with four-part harmony carrying a repetitive melody that draws on both Western hymn writing and South African melody. It was first sung in public at the ordination of John Hlengani Mboweni as a pastor in 1899, and Mboweni made the first translation, into Xitsonga, the same year. In 1927 the poet Samuel Mqhayi added seven more Xhosa stanzas, and the hymn was first published that year.

The choir of Ohlange High School, whose co-founder served as the first president of the South African Native National Congress, took the hymn up, and the Rev. John Langalibalele Dube's Ohlange Zulu Choir popularised it at concerts in Johannesburg. It was sung to close the Congress's meeting in 1912, and by 1925 it was the official closing anthem of the organisation, later the African National Congress (ANC). Solomon Plaatje, a founding member of the ANC, had it recorded in London in 1923, and Moses Mphahlele published a Sotho version in 1942. It spread as a church hymn and as an anthem of political meetings, and independence movements across southern Africa sang it, including in Chichewa in Malawi and Zambia.

Under apartheid the hymn was the ANC's official anthem and a symbol of the anti-apartheid movement; many regarded it as the unofficial national anthem of South Africa, standing for the suffering of the oppressed majority. Because of its link to the ANC, the government banned it. During the 1970s and 1980s it was sung regularly at the meetings of the worldwide Anti-Apartheid Movement, which made it known far beyond Africa. The anthropologist David Coplan wrote that it came to symbolise, more than any other piece of expressive culture, the struggle for African unity and liberation in South Africa.

Several states adopted it at independence. A Swahili translation, "Mungu ibariki Afrika", was first officially performed on 8 December 1961 in the new national stadium in Dar es Salaam, before 75,000 people, as Tanganyika's independence from the British Empire approached: near midnight the Union Jack was lowered to "God Save the Queen" for the last time, and the new flag was raised to the hymn's tune. It remained the anthem after Tanganyika's union with Zanzibar in 1964 formed Tanzania. Zambia used the hymn as its anthem from independence in 1964 until 1973, when new words, "Stand and Sing of Zambia, Proud and Free", replaced the old ones on the same melody. Zimbabwe adopted a Shona and Ndebele version, "Ishe Komborera Africa", at independence in 1980 and replaced it in 1994; Namibia used the hymn at independence in March 1990 and replaced it in 1991 with "Namibia, Land of the Brave".

In 1994, after the end of apartheid, President Nelson Mandela declared both "Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika" and the former anthem, "Die Stem van Suid-Afrika", national anthems of South Africa, and the two were played together. In 1996 a shortened combination of the two was released as the new national anthem under the constitution, and it was adopted in 1997. It opens with lines in Xhosa and Zulu, continues in Sesotho, then uses a section of "Die Stem" in Afrikaans and closes in English. Its official name is "The National Anthem of South Africa", although it is often called "Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika". The original melody is still used in the anthems of Tanzania and Zambia.
== meaning
A prayer asking God to bless Africa, written in the form and language of a church hymn. Sung in parts by a whole gathering, it came to stand for unity and for the hope of liberation as much as for its religious words.
