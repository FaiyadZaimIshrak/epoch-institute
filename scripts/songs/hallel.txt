== story
Hallel (Hebrew for "praise") is a Jewish prayer made of Psalms 113 to 118, recited word for word as one unit on joyous occasions as an act of praise and thanksgiving. It is sometimes called the Egyptian Hallel, because Psalm 114 opens with the Exodus from Egypt. The Full Hallel, all six psalms with a blessing before and after, is recited on the first days of Passover, on Shavuot, on all seven days of Sukkot, on Shemini Atzeret and Simchat Torah, and on the eight days of Hanukkah; the sages gave a short word, "BeBeTaCh", for remembering the days. It is said in the morning service after the Amidah, and in all communities during the Passover Seder.

At the Seder the Talmud records a dispute between the schools of Hillel and Shammai: Shammai's school held that only the first psalm should be read before the meal, Hillel's the first two, the rest following the Grace After Meals, and Jewish law follows Hillel. The New Testament says that after the Last Supper Jesus and his disciples sang a psalm or hymn before going out to the Mount of Olives, and this may have been the Hallel; Jesus, like any literate Jew of the 1st century, may well have known how to chant the Hallel psalms, which were an integral part of the Passover.

A Partial Hallel, which leaves out the first eleven verses of Psalms 115 and 116 (Yemenite Jews leave out more), is recited on the last six days of Passover and on the New Moon (Rosh Chodesh); on these days Hallel was at first not said at all, and the shorter form is a later custom. The Talmud explains the shorter Passover Hallel by the Temple sacrifice, which was the same each day of the festival; other sources say the Full Hallel is left out on the seventh day in consideration of the Egyptians who died at the crossing of the Red Sea. Hallel is not said on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, for the Talmud asks whether it is fitting to sing joyful praises while the King sits in judgement with the Books of Life and Death open before him, nor on Purim, for which the Talmud gives three reasons. Many communities, especially those of religious Zionism, recite it on Israeli Independence Day, as the Chief Rabbinate of Israel instructs, and some on Jerusalem Day.

The name Great Hallel is given to Psalm 136, every verse of which ends with the same refrain on God's enduring mercy and which names twenty-six of his acts of kindness; it is recited at the Seder after the standard Hallel and was most probably sung antiphonally in Temple worship. Jewish tradition has many well-established melodies for Hallel: some of its psalms are sung aloud while others are said quietly. Its psalms have often been set in classical music, Psalm 114 by Josquin des Prez and Mendelssohn, verses of Psalm 115 by Bach in his cantata Der Herr denket an uns, Psalm 117 by Vivaldi and Mozart and Psalm 118 by Léonin and Pérotin. Michael Isaacson's An American Hallel, a full setting for chorus with expressions of praise and gratitude by Americans of past and present, was first performed by the Carolina Master Chorale under Tim Koch in the autumn of 2009.
== meaning
Six psalms of praise and thanksgiving sung as one song: they call on all servants of God and all nations to praise him, remember how Israel left Egypt, trust in God rather than in idols, give thanks for rescue from death and end in a festive song of thanksgiving. The mood is joyful and grateful, fitting the festivals that remember deliverance.
