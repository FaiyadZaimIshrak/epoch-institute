== story
"El Cóndor Pasa" ("The Condor Passes") is an orchestral piece from the Peruvian zarzuela, or musical play, of the same name. The Peruvian composer Daniel Alomía Robles wrote its music in 1913, basing it on traditional Andean music, the folk music of Peru; the script of the play, written in prose in two acts, was by Julio de La Paz, the pen name of the Lima dramatist Julio Baudouin. It was first performed in public at the Teatro Mazzi in Lima. On 3 May 1933 The Edward B. Marks Music Corp. registered a piano arrangement of the play's most famous melody at the Library of Congress.

The melody travelled far beyond the play. It has been estimated that more than 4,000 versions of it have been produced around the world, with some 300 different sets of words. In 1965 the American musician Paul Simon heard it played by the band Los Incas in Paris, where both were performing at the Théâtre de l'Est parisien. Simon became friendly with the band, toured with them and produced their first American album. When he asked to use the piece, the band's director Jorge Milchberg, who was registered as co-author of the arrangement and collected royalties for it, told him wrongly that it was a traditional Peruvian composition.

Simon & Garfunkel recorded it as "El Cóndor Pasa (If I Could)", with English words credited to Simon over Los Incas' instrumental track, on their 1970 album Bridge over Troubled Water. As a single in the United States it reached number 18 on the Billboard pop chart and number 6 on the Easy Listening chart. In late 1970 Robles' son, the Peruvian filmmaker Armando Robles Godoy, brought a successful copyright suit against Simon, on the ground that his father had composed the piece and copyrighted it in the United States in 1933. He called the case almost friendly and the error an honest mistake, and later wrote new Spanish words for the song, taking Simon's version as a reference.

Many musicians have recorded it since, among them Peruvian guitarists and singers, the Argentine guitarist Eduardo Falú in a setting for solo guitar, and singers from Plácido Domingo to Celia Cruz. In 2004 Peru declared the song part of its national cultural heritage, and it is now considered the second national anthem of Peru. Because Robles died in 1942, his original version has been in the public domain since 1 January 2013.
== meaning
In its original form the piece has no words: it is an orchestral melody drawn from the folk music of the Andes. The many later sets of words, in Spanish, English and other languages, are separate works by their own writers.
