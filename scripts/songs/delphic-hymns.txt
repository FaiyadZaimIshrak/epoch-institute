== story
The Delphic Hymns are two hymns to Apollo from ancient Greece that survive, with their music, in substantial fragments. They were carved on the south outer wall of the Athenian Treasury at Delphi and found there in 1893 by the French archaeologist Théophile Homolle. Henri Weil restored the Greek text and Théodore Reinach transcribed the music into modern notation; the work was eased because the two hymns use different notations, the first the vocal and the second the instrumental. They were long dated c. 138 and 128 BC, but recent scholarship holds it likely that both were written for the Athenian Pythaid of 128 BC, a solemn procession of the Athenians to Delphi held on special occasions, usually after certain omens.

The damaged heading of the First Hymn was long read as saying only that its composer was an Athenian. A careful reading shows instead the name Athenaios son of Athenaios, so the hymn is the earliest unambiguous notated music of the Western world whose composer is known by name if it is indeed the older of the two. Athenaios is listed as a singer. The Second Hymn, headed Paean and Prosodion to the God, is by Limenios son of Thoinos, an Athenian kithara-player, and is dated to 128 BC. The difference in their trades may explain why one wrote in the singers' letter notation and the other in the players' special symbols, which are read today through the treatise of Alypius, a writer on music of the 3rd century AD.

Both hymns are a single line of melody in cretic, five-beat metre. The First is a paean in three verses followed by a dance section that is almost all lost; according to Pöhlmann and West it moves between the Greek Phrygian and Hyperphrygian modes, wandering in its middle verse among closely spaced notes. The Second has ten sections with frequent changes of mode, the first nine a paean and the last a prosodion, a processional song, in aeolic rhythms. The Second Hymn's mode is the Greek Lydian at its start, moving to the Hypolydian and to chromatic forms in later sections.

These hymns have been studied closely by musicologists and often performed on replicas of ancient instruments. The First Hymn was first performed in public in June 1894, one year after its discovery, at the international athletic congress at the Sorbonne in Paris that founded the modern Olympic Games. Gabriel Fauré made two versions of it. Arda Mandikian recorded the fragments at Delphi in 1950, and later recordings include those of Atrium Musicæ de Madrid (1979), De Organographia (1997) and Ensemble Kérylos under Annie Bélis (1996 and 2016).
== meaning
Both hymns call the Muses down from Mount Helicon to Delphi to sing of Apollo, and tell the god's story: his birth on Delos, his killing of the serpent that guarded the oracle's tripod, and his defence of Delphi against an invading army of Gauls. The First Hymn describes the Athenians' sacrifice and the music of pipes and lyre; the Second closes with a prayer that Apollo and Artemis protect Athens and Delphi and that the rule of victorious Rome endure.
