== story
The roots of "We Shall Overcome" are unclear and many. It was long thought to descend from "I'll Overcome Some Day", a hymn by the Reverend Charles Albert Tindley of Philadelphia, a Methodist Episcopal minister, first published in 1901, written in an idiom rooted in African American folk traditions for congregations many of whom had been freed from slavery a generation before. A letter in the United Mine Workers Journal of February 1909 recalled strikers opening every meeting with prayer and a song called "We Will Overcome", the first sign of such a song in a secular and mixed-race setting. Its tune echoes the spiritual "No More Auction Block for Me" (also known as "Many Thousands Gone"), printed by Thomas Wentworth Higginson in The Atlantic Monthly in June 1867; scholars also hear the Catholic hymn "O sanctissima" (published 1792) in its first half and the 19th-century hymn "I'll Be All Right" in its second. As Victor Bobetsky wrote in 2015, it owes its existence to many ancestors.

The modern song took shape in a strike. In October 1945 members of the Food, Tobacco, Agricultural, and Allied Workers union (FTA-CIO), mostly African American women, began a five-month strike against the American Tobacco Company in Charleston, South Carolina. Through the cold, wet winter of 1945–1946 one of the strikers, Lucille Simmons, ended each day's picketing by leading a slow, long-meter version of the hymn "We'll Overcome (I'll Be All Right)". Zilphia Horton, music director of the Highlander Folk School in Monteagle, Tennessee, a school that trained union organisers, learned it from her and made it her custom to close each evening's meeting with it. It was printed as "We Will Overcome" in the People's Songs Bulletin with her introduction. Pete Seeger learned it from her in 1947 and changed "will" to "shall", because, he said, it opened the mouth wider and sang better; he later wrote that Septima Clarke, Highlander's director of education, had always preferred "shall" too. Joe Glazer recorded it for the CIO in 1950, and in 1952 Laura Duncan and the Jewish Young Singers made the first recording under the title "We Shall Overcome".

It became the anthem of the civil rights movement through Highlander. Frank Hamilton took up Seeger's version and passed it to Guy Carawan; in 1957 Seeger sang it at Highlander before an audience that included Martin Luther King Jr., who remarked how it stayed with him. When Carawan succeeded Horton as music director in 1959, the school's young student activists, many of them teenagers, gave it the words and rhythm known today as they sang it through police raids on Highlander and spells in jail in 1959–1960. Carawan taught it at the founding meeting of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee in Raleigh, North Carolina, in 1960, and from there it spread by word of mouth to become the movement's unofficial anthem.

In August 1963 Joan Baez led a crowd in singing it at the Lincoln Memorial during the March on Washington. On 15 March 1965, after the "Bloody Sunday" attacks on marchers at Selma, President Lyndon Johnson used its words before Congress. King recited them in his last sermon in Memphis on 31 March 1968, and more than 50,000 people sang it at his funeral days after his assassination on 4 April. Farmworkers sang it in Spanish in the strikes and grape boycotts of the late 1960s, and Robert F. Kennedy led anti-apartheid crowds in it from the roof of his car in South Africa in 1966.

The song travelled far beyond the United States. The Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association took its title as a slogan, and marchers sang it before the Bloody Sunday shootings of 1972. In Prague in 1989, during the Velvet Revolution, hundreds of thousands sang it in English and Czech in Wenceslas Square. In India Girija Kumar Mathur translated it into Hindi as "Hum Honge Kaamyab"; in Bengali, Hemanga Biswas's "Amra Korbo Joy" and Shibdas Bandyopadhyay's "Ek Din Shurjer Bhor", recorded by the Calcutta Youth Choir during the Bangladesh War of Independence of 1971, became widely sung, the latter a favourite of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman; in Kerala it became "Nammal Vijayikkum", the struggle song of the Students Federation of India. On 4 March 2025 Representative Al Green and other members of Congress sang it in protest during President Donald Trump's address to a joint session.

Its ownership was long disputed. In 1960, on the advice of the publisher The Richmond Organization (TRO), Horton, Carawan, Hamilton and Seeger registered a copyright in an arrangement, with the royalties going to a We Shall Overcome Fund administered by Highlander for African American cultural organising in the South; Seeger called it a defensive measure and never claimed to be its author. In April 2016 the We Shall Overcome Foundation, led by the producer Isaias Gamboa, which had been refused permission to use the song in a documentary, sued TRO and its Ludlow Music, arguing that the 1948 "We Will Overcome" had passed into the public domain in 1976 for lack of renewal. On 8 September 2017 Judge Denise Cote of the Southern District of New York found the registered first verse too little different from the earlier text to carry a copyright of its own, and on 26 January 2018 TRO-Ludlow settled, giving up any claim to the melody or the words and agreeing that they were dedicated to the public domain.
== meaning
A song of steadfast hope and collective resolve in the face of injustice. Its mood is slow, solemn and determined, made to be sung together.
