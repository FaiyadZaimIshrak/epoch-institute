== story
The song begins with "Mbube", written by the South African singer Solomon Linda. Born in rural Natal, Linda was a migrant worker and beer-hall singer who led an a cappella group, the Evening Birds, and worked as a packer at Eric Gallo's record-pressing plant in Johannesburg. At their second session in Gallo's studio, in 1939, the Evening Birds recorded "Mbube" in three takes without rehearsal. Its Zulu words are said to recall a time in Linda's childhood when he chased a lion while herding cattle. It sold over 100,000 copies in Africa over the next nine years, the first South African "hit" in the music historian Veit Erlmann's view, and it shaped isicathamiya, the a cappella singing of migrant workers' hostels; mbube became the name of a whole style of male choral singing. Just after the recording, however, Linda sold his rights to Gallo for ten shillings, unaware of what the deal meant. He collapsed on stage in 1959 and died three years later, aged 53, with almost nothing; his family could not afford a tombstone.

Gallo later sent a bundle of records to Decca Records in the United States, where the ethnomusicologist Alan Lomax saved "Mbube" from being thrown away and handed it to the folk singer Pete Seeger. Seeger misheard the Zulu chorus, and his group the Weavers released the song as "Wimoweh" in December 1951, with a brass accompaniment by Gordon Jenkins; it reached No. 6 in the US. Its publisher, the Richmond Organization, claimed it was a folk song and credited it to a fictitious "Paul Campbell", so none of the royalties went to Linda. In 1961 the Tokens, a teenage doo-wop group from Brooklyn, asked George David Weiss to remake "Wimoweh". He dropped its shrieks and hollers, kept the chant, made Linda's final improvised notes the new tune and added English words. Produced by Hugo Peretti and Luigi Creatore, "The Lion Sleeps Tonight" came out in October 1961 as a B-side and rose to No. 1 in the US and several other countries, again with no credit to Linda. Miriam Makeba sang "Mbube" at President John F. Kennedy's birthday in 1962.

Covers followed for decades: by the mid-2000s some 150 artists had recorded the song, among them Robert John (No. 3 in the US, 1971) and Tight Fit (No. 1 in the UK, 1982). In 1994 it was used in Disney's film The Lion King, then in the 1997 stage musical and the 2019 remake. In a court case between the American publishers, begun in 1990, Judge John Keenan ruled the Tokens' version a separate composition and ordered ten percent of its performance royalties paid to Linda's family; by 1992 Abilene Music held its rights.

Linda's family remained poor; five of his eight children had died. In 2000 the South African journalist Rian Malan wrote an essay in Rolling Stone on the song's origins, estimating that it had earned some $15 million in royalties, and François Verster's documentary A Lion's Trail told Linda's story. With the South African government's support, the family sued Disney in July 2004 for using the song in The Lion King. Their lawyer, Owen Dean, argued that under the Copyright Act 1911 the rights had reverted to Linda's heirs 25 years after his death. Shortly before the trial set for February 2006, the case was settled with Abilene Music: the family received a lump sum for royalties from 1987 onwards and a share of future income until 2017, "The Lion Sleeps Tonight" was acknowledged as derived from "Mbube", and Linda was named its co-composer. "Mbube" fell into the public domain in South Africa in 2012. The story has since been told in the 2019 Netflix documentary ReMastered: The Lion's Share, and Beyoncé's 2020 film Black Is King, made in part after she learned that Linda had gone unrecognised, uses the original "Mbube".
== meaning
A light pop song about a sleeping lion, sung over a repeated chant taken from the Zulu original. Its mood is playful and dreamy.
