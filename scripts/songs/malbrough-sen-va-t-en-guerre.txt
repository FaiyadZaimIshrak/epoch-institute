== story
"Malbrough s'en va-t-en guerre" ("Marlborough is going off to war"), also known as "Mort et convoi de l'invincible Malbrough" ("The death and burial of the invincible Marlborough"), is a French folk song. It is a burlesque lament on the death of John Churchill, 1st Duke of Marlborough (1650–1722), written on a false rumour of his death after the Battle of Malplaquet in 1709, the bloodiest battle of the War of the Spanish Succession. It tells how Marlborough's wife, waiting for him to come back from the war, is brought the news that he is dead, how he was buried, and how a nightingale sang over his grave.

For years it lived only by word of mouth: it is not among the many songs on the events of the day printed in France in the middle of the 18th century. Pierre Beaumarchais set a despairing love song for Cherubino to its tune in his play The Marriage of Figaro (1778), and in 1780 it became very popular; Carl Stamitz ended a sonata for viola d'amore and viola, written in Paris that year, with it. When Prince Louis-Joseph, son of Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette and heir to the throne, was born in 1781, his wet nurse, a peasant named Geneviève Poitrine, sang it as she rocked the royal cradle. The name, the simple words and the melody caught the queen's interest, and she sang it often; everyone took it up after her, the king included, and it was sung in the state apartments of Versailles, in the kitchens and in the stables. Nicolas Dalayrac quoted it in the first act of his opera Renaud d'Ast (1787).

From the court it passed to the tradespeople of Paris, and from town to town and country to country. It became as popular in England as in France, and Johann Wolfgang von Goethe came to hate Marlborough merely because he met the tune everywhere on his travels in France. Through the Bourbon dynasty's influence on the Spanish nobility it spread to Spain, where Marlborough became the easier "Mambrú" and children sang it while playing hopscotch (rayuela). Jacinto Valledor ended a tonadilla on "the life and death of General Malbrú" with the tune, and in 1826 the guitarist Fernando Sor wrote his Introduction and Variations on it, Op. 28. From Spain it went on to Latin America.

The craze lasted for years and faded slowly after the French Revolution, though Napoleon is said to have hummed the tune, among other times when crossing the Memel in June 1812 at the start of his fatal Russian campaign. Beethoven used it to stand for the French in Wellington's Victory. In the United Kingdom the melody took on new words: by the middle of the 19th century it was sung as "For He's a Jolly Good Fellow", often at gatherings of men, and by 1862 those words were familiar in America too, where the same melody also became the campfire song "The Bear Went Over the Mountain". The song itself has been translated into several languages, among them an English version published by the American poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow.
== meaning
A mock funeral song. Marlborough rides off to war and does not come back; his lady waits for him in vain until a messenger brings word that he is dead and tells of his burial, with a nightingale singing over the grave. The bouncing tune and nonsense refrain turn the lament for a feared enemy general into cheerful mockery.
