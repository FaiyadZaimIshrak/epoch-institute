== story
Yigdal, Hebrew for "be exalted", is a Jewish hymn that sets in verse the 13 principles of faith formulated by Maimonides. In various rites it shares with Adon Olam the place of honour at the opening of the morning service and the close of the evening service. It was not the only verse setting of the 13 principles, but it has outlived all the others, in Hebrew or in other languages.

Who wrote it is debated. Leopold Zunz held that it was the work of Daniel ben Judah, who spent eight years improving it before completing it in 1404. Some read a signature in its last line naming a Yechiel son of Baruch, whose identity is uncertain. Hartwig Hirschfeld argued for the poet Immanuel the Roman, who wrote several poems on the 13 principles, among them a 72-line "Poem Based on the 13 Articles" that shares Yigdal's rhythm, rhyme and phrasing. Ashkenazi Jews sing thirteen lines, one for each principle, and repeat the last, on the resurrection of the dead, when the cantor and congregation sing it in alternation; Sephardic Jews sing it together throughout and add a fourteenth line.

It has more traditional tunes than Adon Olam, and older ones. In the Sephardic rite of the Dutch and English communities it is often sung to a melody that stands for the day. In London, for two centuries, Ashkenazi congregations have had a fixed tune for each occasion, all sung in alternation by the cantor and congregation. The best known, the Friday evening tune, may date from the 17th century or earlier and was also used in Germany, Poland and Bohemia; a tune for the evenings of Rosh Hashana and Yom Kippur was probably the work of a Polish cantor of the early 17th century and spread westward; three tunes for the pilgrimage festivals go back at least to the early 18th century, and the choral response of the Passover tune took its final shape from David Mombach. Isaac Nathan used the lively Sukkot tune in 1815 for Lord Byron's "The Wild Gazelle", one of the Hebrew Melodies.

Not everyone sings it. Most Hasidic Jews leave it out, because Isaac Luria reportedly omitted it from his prayer book, rejecting poems later than the Talmudic era as not kabbalistic enough, though following Isaiah Horowitz most treat it as a sacred hymn. Syrian Jews sing it before Kiddush on Friday night rather than at the end of services, and Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik opposed reciting it at the end of prayers as an imitation of the Christian custom of a closing catechism.

The hymn also passed into Christian worship. The Methodist Thomas Olivers heard the cantor Myer Lyon, who also sang on the London opera stage as Michael Leoni, sing it at the Great Synagogue of London, and around 1770 wrote "The God of Abraham Praise", a paraphrase set to the tune now called "Leoni"; John Wesley's hymnbook printed it, much Christianised, in 1785. In the late 19th century Rabbi Max Landsberg and the Unitarian minister Newton M. Mann made a closer translation, "Praise to the Living God", first printed in the Reform Jewish Union Hymnal and since used in many Christian hymnals.
== meaning
A creed in verse: line by line it states the principles of Jewish faith set out by Maimonides, one to a line, ending with the resurrection of the dead. Its mood is one of solemn praise, though some of its tunes are festive.
