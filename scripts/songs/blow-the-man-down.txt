== story
"Blow the Man Down" is an English-language sea shanty, number 2624 in the Roud Folk Song Index. Publications of the time and the memories of sailors printed later put it in use by the 1860s. In July 1867 the Syracuse Daily Courier quoted a line of it, said to be sung for hauling the halyards on a steamship bound from New York to Glasgow.

In 1879 George Haswell, a passenger on a steamship from London to Sydney, wrote down some of the crew's shanties, and they were printed in the ship's own fortnightly newspaper, The Parramatta Sun, with a full set of words for "Blow the Man Down". Haswell's version is about a ship of the Black Ball Line. His article was little read, but it reached Laura Alexandrine Smith, whose large collection of sailors' songs, The Music of the Waters (1888), was one of the first to be widely available. Smith reprinted Haswell's words and gave a second version, presumably collected by herself, said to be sung for hoisting the topsail yards, in which a sailor comes home to England from Hong Kong and meets a girl on "Winchester Street".

Like most shanties of its kind, it had no fixed text. The shantyman sang solo verses, some customary, some floating from one shanty to another and some made up on the spot, and the crew answered each with an unchanging two-part refrain, so the song could run as long as the work required. Felix Riesenberg, who trained and served as an officer in the Merchant Marine in the 1890s, described earlier sailors singing plainer working verses, not about the Black Ball Line, while raising the topsail of a merchant ship to sail from New York to Liverpool, led by a sailor named Jimmie.

Many recordings of former sailors singing it were made in the first half of the 20th century. Percy Grainger recorded a man named Tom Roberts singing a version in Chelsea, London, in 1908, now in the British Library Sound Archive; the folklorist James Madison Carpenter recorded it in England, Scotland and Wales in the early 1930s, recordings kept by the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library; Helen Hartness Flanders recorded versions by old sailors in New England in the 1940s, and Helen Creighton in Nova Scotia in the 1940s and 1950s.
== meaning
A halyard shanty, made to time a crew's pulls on the ropes. In its best-known couplets a sailor walking down Paradise Street meets a pretty girl, who steers him to a fine ship bound for New York; once at sea it proves a Black Ball packet, where the mate knocks him down with a spar, and he ends by warning his hearers never to heed what pretty girls say. Other couplets describe the landsmen who ship aboard the Black Ball and the hard orders that greet them. Every couplet is answered by the crew's refrain; the mood is rough, rhythmic and wry.
