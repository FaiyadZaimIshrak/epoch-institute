== story
"The Twelve Days of Christmas" is an English Christmas carol and nursery rhyme, and a cumulative song: each of its twelve verses adds one new gift from "my true love" and then repeats all the earlier ones, so that each verse is one line longer than the last. The twelve days are those of the Christmas season, from Christmas Day to the day before Epiphany on 6 January. The exact origins and meaning of the song are unknown, but it is highly probable that it began as a children's memory and forfeit game.

The earliest known publications of the words are an illustrated children's book, Mirth Without Mischief, published in London in 1780, and a broadsheet by Angus of Newcastle, dated to the late 18th or early 19th century; their words are almost identical, and neither has music. The book was illustrated with woodcuts, attributed in one source to Thomas Bewick. There is evidence that the carol comes from the North of England, in particular the area around Newcastle upon Tyne: William Henry Husk wrote in 1864 that it was "found on broadsides printed at Newcastle at various periods during the last hundred and fifty years", and many 19th-century citations come from that area. In the northern counties the song was often called the "Ten Days of Christmas", as there were only ten gifts; it was also known in Somerset, Dorset and elsewhere in England, and became widely popular in the United States and Canada. Peter and Iona Opie suggested that the chant may have come from France, and similar cumulative verses are known from Scotland ("The Yule Days"), France ("Les Douze Mois"), Sweden and the Faroe Islands.

Many early sources describe it as a game. James Orchard Halliwell wrote in 1842 that "each child in succession repeats the gifts of the day, and forfeits for each mistake". Husk said that it was commonly used in London for playing Forfeits, each person reciting a portion in turn and anyone who failed paying "some trifling forfeit". Thomas Hughes described such a game in a short story of 1864, and Lady Gomme wrote in 1898 that "The Twelve Days" was played every Twelfth Day night in a friend's house, before the mince pies and twelfth cake, by a mixed company of children and adults seated round the room; each mistake cost a small article belonging to the player, which had to be redeemed afterwards.

Many different melodies have been sung to the words. The now-standard one was popularised by the English baritone and composer Frederic Austin, who arranged a traditional folk melody for solo voice and piano and sang it in his concerts from 1905 onwards; the arrangement was published by Novello & Co. in 1909. Austin wrote that the song had been current in his family in his childhood and that he had recorded both tune and words to the best of his recollection. Later publications state that the music for the fifth day's gift is his own addition to an otherwise traditional melody. Several of his choices in the words became widespread: the word "On" at the start of each verse, "calling" birds on the fourth day where the 1780 text has "colly" birds (a regional word for coal-black, that is, blackbirds), and the order of the last four gifts. A 20th-century variant in which the true love "gave" rather than "sent" the gifts has become common in North America.

The gifts have been much discussed. The Oxford Dictionary of Nursery Rhymes records suggestions that they stand for the food or sport of each month of the year, but thinks it probable that the surviving lines are "merely an irreligious travesty". In 1979 the Canadian hymnologist Hugh D. McKellar suggested that the song was a catechism in code for English Catholics at a time when practising Catholicism was against the law (from 1558 until 1829); he offered no evidence, and Snopes.com concludes that the hypothesis is incorrect, since none of the items would distinguish Catholics from Protestants.

The song has been recorded and parodied many times. Counting every repetition, the true love sends 364 gifts in all. Since 1984 PNC Bank has published the cost of the gifts each year as a tongue-in-cheek economic indicator, the Christmas Price Index, and Donald Knuth used the song in his article The Complexity of Songs.
== meaning
A counting song in which the singer lists the presents sent by a true love on each of the twelve days of Christmas, beginning with a partridge in a pear tree. The presents are birds, rings and then companies of people, and with every verse the list grows by one. It has no story beyond the growing list: the pleasure is in remembering it all and getting it out in one breath.
