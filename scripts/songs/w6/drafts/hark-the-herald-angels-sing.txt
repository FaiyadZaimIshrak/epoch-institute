== story
"Hark! The Herald Angels Sing" is an English Christmas carol written by Charles Wesley in 1739. It is based on Luke 2:8–14 and describes a chorus of angels singing of the birth of Christ. Wesley called it "Hymn for Christmas-Day", and it first appeared in Hymns and Sacred Poems (1739), compiled by Charles and his brother John. His version had ten four-line verses and a different opening, in which the "welkin", the sky, rings with glory to the King of Kings.

The text sung today is the work of several hands. George Whitefield, like the Wesleys an early leader of the Methodist movement, included the hymn in his Collection of Hymns for Social Worship (1753); he left out the eighth and tenth verses and revised the rest, above all the opening couplet, which he changed to the line about the herald angels that gives the carol its name. Whitefield's version was taken into Martin Madan's Collection of Psalms and Hymns (1760) with a new ending to the second verse. The carol first appeared in an Anglican publication in 1782, in a supplement to Tate and Brady's New Version of the Psalms of David. Because the New Version and its supplement were bound up with every copy of the Book of Common Prayer, the carol spread widely and became one of the "great four" Anglican hymns. A last change, to the end of the fourth verse, was made when it entered Hymns Ancient and Modern in 1861. The version most often sung now uses six of Wesley's verses, arranged as three eight-line verses with a refrain.

The tune came later still. Wesley had meant the words to be sung to the same tune as his Easter hymn "Christ the Lord Is Risen Today". In 1855 the British musician William Hayman Cummings, organist at Waltham Abbey Church, adapted a movement from Felix Mendelssohn's Festgesang zum Gutenbergfest (1840), a secular work composed for the 400th anniversary of the invention of the printing press, to fit the words. Since the middle of the 19th century the carol has usually been sung to this melody.

In Britain it is commonly heard in an arrangement that keeps Cummings's harmonisation for the first two verses and adds, in the third, a soprano descant and a new organ harmonisation by Sir David Willcocks, first published by Oxford University Press in 1961 in the first book of Carols for Choirs. For many years it has been the recessional hymn of the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols at King's College Chapel, Cambridge. At St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin, the same service traditionally ends with the hymn sung to "See, the Conqu'ring hero comes" from Handel's Judas Maccabaeus, with brass fanfare, drums and organ, broadcast live each Christmas Eve on RTÉ Radio 1. The Victorian organist W. H. Jude also wrote a setting. The carol is sung by the children's choir in the closing scene of A Charlie Brown Christmas and is heard in The Muppet Christmas Carol.
== meaning
A hymn of praise for the birth of Christ, based on the angels' song to the shepherds in the Gospel of Luke. It calls all nations to join the angels in proclaiming the newborn king, and sets out in a few lines what Christians believe about him: God become man, born to live among people. The mood is triumphant.
