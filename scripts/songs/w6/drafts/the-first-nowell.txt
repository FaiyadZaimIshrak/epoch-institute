== story
"The First Nowell" (or "Nowel"), modernised as "The First Noel", is a traditional English Christmas carol of Cornish origin. It most likely dates from the early modern period, although it may be older. Its current form was first published in Carols Ancient and Modern (1823) and in Gilbert and Sandys Carols (1833), collections edited by William Sandys and arranged and edited by Davies Gilbert, who also wrote extra words.

"Nowell" is an Early Modern English word for Christmas, from the French Noël, "the Christmas season", and ultimately from the Latin natalis [dies], "[day] of birth". From the Middle Ages to the early modern period the word was regularly used in the burden, or refrain, of carols; 15th-century examples are found in the Ritson Manuscript, the Trinity Carol Roll and the Selden Carol Book. As a result the word also came to mean a Christmas song or carol.

The melody is unusual among English folk melodies: it consists of one musical phrase sung twice, followed by a refrain that is a variation on that phrase, and all three phrases end on the third of the scale. Writing in the Journal of the Folk-Song Society in 1915, Anne Gilchrist noted that the tune was not recorded before Sandys's publication. On the evidence of a set of church gallery parts found in Westmorland, she suggested that it may have begun as a treble part to another carol, "Hark, hark what news the angels bring", which was passed down by ear and later remembered as the melody rather than as a harmony. A conjectural reconstruction of that earlier version is printed in The New Oxford Book of Carols.

Today the carol is usually sung in a four-part hymn arrangement by the English composer John Stainer, first published in his Carols, New and Old in 1871. As with many traditional songs and carols, the words vary from book to book. The version of The New English Hymnal (1986) is the one used in Henry Ramsden Bramley and John Stainer's collection; others are Ralph Dunstan's gallery version in the Cornish Songbook (1929) and that of the Reverend Charles Lewis Hutchins in Carols Old and Carols New (1916). Variations on the tune are included in Victor Hely-Hutchinson's Carol Symphony. In the early 1930s the American folklorist James Madison Carpenter made sound recordings of several traditional versions in Cornwall.

The words join two parts of the Christmas story. The angels' announcement to the shepherds and the shepherds' adoration are told in the second chapter of the Gospel of Luke; the Star of Bethlehem belongs to the story of the Magi, the Wise Men, in the Gospel of Matthew, and does not appear in the story of the shepherds.
== meaning
A carol that tells the Christmas story from its beginning: angels bring the news of the birth to shepherds in the fields at night, and a star leads the Wise Men to Bethlehem, where they kneel and offer their gifts. It ends by calling everyone to praise God together. The refrain repeats the old word for Christmas and announces the birth of a king. The mood is calm and joyful.
