== story
"We Wish You a Merry Christmas" is an English Christmas carol, and its famous version comes from the West Country of England. The greeting "A merry Christmas and a happy New Year" is recorded from the early 18th century, but the history of the carol itself has yet to be made clear. Its origin probably lies in the English custom by which the wealthy people of a community gave Christmas treats to carolers on Christmas Eve, such as "figgy pudding", which was much like the Christmas pudding of today; in the West Country the name meant a raisin or plum pudding, not necessarily one with figs. In the famous version the singers demand the pudding and threaten not to go until they get some.

The song is absent from the carol collections of the West-countrymen Davies Gilbert (1822 and 1823) and William Sandys (1833), from the anthologies of Sylvester (1861) and Husk (1864), and from The Oxford Book of Carols (1928). In The New Oxford Book of Carols (1992) the editors Hugh Keyte and Andrew Parrott call it "English traditional" and "the remnant of an envoie much used by wassailers and other luck visitors", giving no source or date.

A closely related verse, with a different wish at its end, dates from the 1830s. It was sung by mummers, townsfolk who went singing from door to door to ask for gifts. Charlotte Yonge's short story The Christmas Mummers (1858) describes them marching to a farmer's door with the little boys and girls running behind, standing in a row and singing as loudly as they could; they are let in, perform a mummers' play, and are served beer by the farmer's maid. Various sources place this version in different parts of England during the 19th century, and several versions survived into the 20th, when folk song collectors recorded them from George Dunn and Mary Evans of Quarry Bank, Staffordshire (both in 1971), and Miss J. Howman of Stow-on-the-Wold, Gloucestershire (1966). These use tunes quite different from the now famous one.

The carol owes its popularity to Arthur Warrell (1883–1939), a composer, conductor and organist of Bristol and a lecturer at the University of Bristol from 1909. He arranged the tune as an elaborate four-part piece for his own University of Bristol Madrigal Singers and performed it with them in concert on 6 December 1935. Oxford University Press published it the same year under the title "A Merry Christmas: West Country traditional song". Warrell's arrangement uses "I" instead of "we" in the words. It was republished in the collection Carols for Choirs (1961) and remains widely performed. Before Warrell made it known, the famous version was completely unknown outside the West Country.

Traditional versions have been recorded since. In 1971 Roy Palmer recorded George Dunn singing a version close to the famous one, with a familiar chorus but with the song "Christmas Is Coming" as its verses, and in 1973 Amy Ford of Low Ham, Somerset, sang a version called "The Singers Make Bold" to Bob and Jacqueline Patten, again with a similar chorus. Several supposedly traditional recordings follow the famous version exactly, but these are almost certainly derived from Warrell's arrangement.
== meaning
A song of good wishes for Christmas and the New Year, sung by visitors at the door. Having given their greeting, the singers ask for a pudding as their reward and say cheerfully that they will stay until they have it. The mood is merry and a little cheeky.
