== story
"God Rest Ye Merry, Gentlemen", also known as "God Rest You Merry, Gentlemen" and as "Tidings of Comfort and Joy", is a traditional English Christmas carol. Its author is unknown. An early version survives in an anonymous manuscript of the 1650s, whose first line differs slightly from that of later texts. Some sources claim that the carol goes back to the 16th century; others date it later, to the 18th or early 19th century. The earliest known printed edition is a broadsheet, Three New Christmas Carols, dated to about 1760, and the first reference that can be dated precisely is in the Monthly Review of November 1764. A variant text was printed in 1775 in The Beauties of the Magazines, reproduced from a song-sheet bought from a caroler in the street.

The phrase "God rest you merry" meant "may God grant you peace and happiness". The Oxford English Dictionary records it from 1534 onwards, and it appears in Shakespeare's As You Like It, while "rest you merry" appears in Romeo and Juliet; both plays date from the 1590s. This use of the verb "rest", meaning to keep or cause to remain, is typical of the language of the 16th and 17th centuries. Today "merry" is often misread as an adjective describing the gentlemen, and the line is often punctuated accordingly; the 1775 variant already does so. The form with "ye" is a false archaism, since "ye" was only ever a subject pronoun.

The carol is sung to two tunes. The better-known traditional melody is in the minor mode. It had been associated with the carol since at least the mid-18th century, when James Nares recorded it in a hand-written manuscript under the title "The old Christmas Carol". Its earliest printed edition appears to be a rondo arrangement for fortepiano by Samuel Wesley, which was reviewed in 1815; soon after, it appeared in a parody published by William Hone in 1820, in a form that differs from the present melody in the third line. The full current melody was published by Chappell in 1855. A second tune, known as "Cornish", was in print by 1833 and was called "the usual version" in the Oxford Book of Carols of 1928, but is seldom heard today.

In the early 19th century the carol was a song of the streets. An article in The Gentleman's Magazine of March 1824 complained that no Christmas carols were heard in London "excepting some croaking ballad-singer bawling out 'God rest you, merry gentlemen', or a like doggerel". The English solicitor and antiquarian William Sandys is responsible for its popularity: he included it in Christmas Carols Ancient and Modern (London, 1833), a collection of carols that he had gathered. Charles Dickens refers to it in his novella A Christmas Carol (1843), where the verb "rest" is replaced by "bless", and George Eliot quotes it in her novel Silas Marner (1861).

Gustav Holst included the carol in his choral fantasy Christmas Day (1910), and the third movement of Victor Hely-Hutchinson's Carol Symphony (1927) is a scherzo on its tune. The version adopted by Carols for Choirs (Oxford University Press, 1961) has become the standard text in the United Kingdom. The carol has been recorded by many popular singers and bands, among them Bing Crosby, who recorded it on 8 June 1942.
== meaning
A carol that greets its hearers with a wish for peace and happiness and tells them not to be dismayed, because Christ was born on Christmas Day. It goes on to tell the Christmas story: the birth in Bethlehem and the angel who brings the news to the shepherds. Each verse ends with a refrain of comfort and joy. The mood is steady and consoling, though the usual tune is in a minor key.
