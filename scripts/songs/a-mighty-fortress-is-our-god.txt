== story
Martin Luther, the Protestant Reformer and a prolific writer of hymns, wrote both the words and the tune of "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott" between 1527 and 1529. The words are mostly his own, although the opening paraphrases Psalm 46. The earliest hymnal that survives with the hymn is Andrew Rauscher's of 1531. It is believed to have appeared in Joseph Klug's Wittenberg hymnal of 1529 under the title of the 46th Psalm, and before that in Hans Weiss's Wittenberg hymnal of 1528, but no copy of either remains. Because Luther's hymns were printed soon after he wrote them, these lost books support the dating to 1527–1529.

Why Luther wrote it is not known. The hymnologist John Julian recorded four theories: that Luther and his companions sang it entering Worms for the Diet in April 1521 (Heinrich Heine); that it honoured Luther's friend Leonhard Kaiser, executed on 16 August 1527 (K. F. T. Schneider); that the Lutheran princes sang it entering Augsburg for the Diet of 1530 (Jean-Henri Merle d'Aubigné); or that it belongs to the Diet of Speyer of 1529, where the Lutheran princes protested against Emperor Charles V's wish to enforce the Edict of Worms. The historian John M. Merriman suggests instead that it began as a martial song against the Ottoman forces. Nineteenth-century musicologists doubted that Luther wrote the tune, but the scholarly consensus now holds that he did. His original melody was strongly rhythmic; a later, even-metred version is the one most churches sing.

The hymn came to be called the "Battle Hymn of the Reformation". In 1834 Heinrich Heine called it the Marseillaise of the Reformation. In Germany it was also sung as a patriotic song, for example at the Wartburg Festival of 1817, since many Protestants saw the Reformation as a national as well as a religious movement. It is reported to have been a battle hymn of the Lutheran king Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden in the Thirty Years' War, an idea taken up by 19th-century poets, but no primary source supports it. It had been translated into Swedish by 1536, presumably by Olaus Petri, and in the late 19th century it became an anthem of the early Swedish socialist movement.

The hymn has been translated into English at least seventy times. Myles Coverdale made the first translation in 1539; Frederick H. Hedge's of 1853 and Thomas Carlyle's became the most popular outside Lutheranism, while North American Lutherans long sang a composite translation from the 1868 Pennsylvania Lutheran Church Book and, more recently, that of the 1978 Lutheran Book of Worship. The hymn remains a staple of Protestant hymnbooks; it is also a suggested hymn for Catholic Masses in the United States and appears in the Catholic Book of Worship of the Canadian Catholic Conference (1972). Since 2022 its history has been presented in the permanent exhibition of the Lutherhaus in Eisenach.

Composers have used the tune for five centuries: Bach in an organ setting (BWV 720), in four-part settings and in his chorale cantata BWV 80 for Reformation Day; Buxtehude, Pachelbel, Telemann and Handel; Mendelssohn in the finale of his Reformation Symphony (1830); Meyerbeer in Les Huguenots (1836); Wagner in his Kaisermarsch (1871); and later Reger, Debussy, Glazunov, Vaughan Williams and Julius Eastman.
== meaning
A hymn of trust in God as a refuge and stronghold, written in the language of siege and battle. It sets the believer's own weakness against the power of the devil and the world, and holds that God's word and kingdom will stand whatever is lost. Its martial imagery is why later listeners heard it as a song of the Reformation's struggle.
