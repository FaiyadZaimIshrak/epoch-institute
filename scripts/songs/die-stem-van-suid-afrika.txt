== story
In May 1918 C. J. Langenhoven wrote an Afrikaans poem called "Die Stem" ("The Voice"). The Reverend Marthinus Lourens de Villiers set it to music in 1921; the tune that was accepted was his second, since the first did not satisfy Langenhoven. In the 1920s the South African Broadcasting Corporation played it at the close of each day's broadcasts, together with "God Save the King". Betty Steyn first recorded it, in England in 1926, and it was first sung in public on 31 May 1928, at a raising of the new South African flag. The poem first had three verses; at the government's request Langenhoven added a fourth, on a religious theme.

In 1938 South Africa proclaimed it one of two co-national anthems, alongside "God Save the King". From 1952 it was sung in English as well as Afrikaans, both versions having official status. "God Save the Queen" was dropped as a co-national anthem in May 1957, leaving "Die Stem" the sole national anthem from 1957 to 1994; the British anthem remained the royal anthem until 1961, when South Africa ceased to be a Commonwealth realm.

These were the years of apartheid, and the song became bound up with it. Black South Africans generally disliked it, seeing it as triumphalist, and strongly associated it with the apartheid regime. P. W. Botha, state president in the 1980s, was fond of it and had his entourage sing it on a visit to Switzerland.

As apartheid was dismantled in the early 1990s, its use dwindled. The African National Congress (ANC) regarded it as offensive because of its link with apartheid and minority rule, and it was agreed that it would not be sung at rugby matches; yet at a test match against New Zealand in 1992 the crowd sang it during a silence for victims of political violence, and a recording was played over the loudspeakers, which caused controversy. At the 1992 Summer Olympics in Barcelona the South African team used "Ode to Joy" and a neutral flag instead. In 1993 a commission considered 119 entries for a new anthem and chose none. After the 1994 general election "Die Stem" kept its official status, sharing it equally with "Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika", long a hymn of the ANC. When the old flag was lowered for the last time at Parliament in Cape Town, "Die Stem" was sung in Afrikaans and English as the new flag was raised, and a black choir sang it at the 1994 presidential inauguration in Pretoria and at the 1995 Rugby World Cup final.

Playing two anthems could take five minutes. In 1997, with the new constitution, a single national anthem was adopted that joins part of "Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika" and part of "Die Stem"; it is still in use. The song's place in it remains debated: some South Africans want its lines removed because of their link with apartheid, while others defend them as part of the reconciliation that followed. In 2012, when organisers in the United Kingdom mistakenly played "Die Stem" at a women's field hockey match, the South African team and staff reacted with outrage. The Afrikaans version remains popular with Afrikaner nationalists and far-right groups such as the Afrikaner Weerstandsbeweging, and in the apartheid era "Die Stem" was also the name of a far-right periodical.
== meaning
An uplifting hymn of devotion to the fatherland and to God. One verse pledges dedication to the Afrikaners (left out of the English version) and another recalls the Great Trek of the Voortrekkers; the fourth verse, added at the government's request, is religious.
