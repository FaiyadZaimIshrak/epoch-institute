== story
"La Bamba" is a Mexican folk song from the state of Veracruz and an example of son jarocho, a style of the region that combines Spanish (Andalusian), Indigenous, Afro-Mexican and Afro-Caribbean musical elements. It is typically played on one or two arpas jarochas (harps) with the jarana jarocha and the requinto jarocho, both string instruments. Its tune stays the same, but its words vary greatly, because performers often improvise verses as they play; versions by popular groups such as Mariachi Vargas de Tecalitlán and Los Pregoneros del Puerto have survived because of their fame.

The song is said to have begun as satire on the late and futile efforts of the viceroy of Mexico to defend the people of the port of Veracruz from pirates. His ships were old and leaky, and in the song a young sailor frantically works the pump, boasting that he, who keeps the ship afloat, is really its captain; the fast rhythm and hand movements of the dance comically imitate pumping water. The name also recalls a Spanish dance called bamba, popular at the time, which sounds close to bomba, a pump. The oldest known references to the song come from the town of Alvarado. Other explanations of the name, none certain, link it to the Spanish verb bambolear, "to sway", or to Africa through the slave trade: to the Bamba people of Central Africa or the Bambara of West Africa.

In a traditional version performed at weddings and in ballet folklórico shows, couples use complicated footwork to tie a bow that stands for their union. The custom of improvising verses comes from here: wedding guests were encouraged to make the song their own for the newlyweds, though verses taken as insults could start fights, and even the drawing of weapons.

The oldest known recording was made by Álvaro Hernández Ortiz, under the name El Jarocho, and released by Victor Records in Mexico in 1938 or 1939. According to a 1945 article in Life, the American bandleader Everett Hoagland brought the song and its dance to Ciro's nightclub in Mexico City, where it became popular; the presidential candidate Miguel Alemán Valdés adopted it for his successful campaign, and later in 1945 Arthur Murray introduced the music and dance at the Stork Club in New York City. Andrés Huesca and his brother Victor recorded a popular version around 1945–46, and the song appeared in the Mexican film Rayando el Sol (1946) and the MGM musical Fiesta (1947). In the 1950s the folk singers William Clauson and Cynthia Gooding recorded it in the United States.

Ritchie Valens learned the song as a boy from his cousin Dickie Cota. Proud of his Mexican heritage, he at first hesitated to merge it with rock and roll, fearing to commercialise a folk song; he spoke little Spanish and learned the words by sound. His 1958 rock and roll version, issued by Del-Fi as the B-side of "Donna", became a top 40 hit in the United States, and it is the only song not in English on Rolling Stone's list of the 500 greatest songs of all time. In 2018 the Library of Congress chose it for the National Recording Registry. Los Lobos recorded the song for La Bamba, the 1987 film of Valens's life, ending with a coda in the traditional style of Veracruz; their version topped the Billboard Hot 100.
== meaning
A fast, playful dance song whose verses are made up afresh by the singers, often to honour or tease a newly married couple. Its oldest story mocks the powerful through a boastful sailor working the pump of a leaking ship.
