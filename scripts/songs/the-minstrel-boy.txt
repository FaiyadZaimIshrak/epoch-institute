== story
"The Minstrel Boy" is an Irish song by the poet Thomas Moore (1779–1852), published in 1813 in his Irish Melodies, a project that ran from 1808 to 1834. It is set to an air called "The Moreen", which Moore said was traditional, but his publication of 1813 is the first record of the tune and the only source for that statement; the scholars who have studied his sources have traced no earlier one. Charles Villiers Stanford claimed a source in his "restored" collection of 1895, but Veronica Ní Chinnéide and Una Hunt judged his research careless, and Alfred Moffatt's Minstrelsy of Ireland corrected several of his attributions in 1898. The tune appears under Moore's title in George Thomson's collection of Scottish and Irish airs of 1816, which has led to a suspicion, unsupported by any document, that Thomson had sent it to Moore.

The Irish Melodies were immensely popular in Ireland and Great Britain, reaching a wide audience, and "The Minstrel Boy" was among the most famous of them. After Moore's death large special editions of "Moore's Melodies" sold at high prices, with lavish illustrations, green leather bindings and embossed shamrocks and golden harps, in North America too. Moore himself came to be called "The Minstrel Boy", the title of Leonard Strong's biography of him in 1937.

Like several of the Melodies the song is melancholy and nostalgic. Its central image is a harp, a romantic symbol of Ireland, torn apart, and unlike the political songs of some of Moore's contemporaries it promises no restoration: a warrior bard is silenced by slavery. Early American audiences favoured its themes of freedom and liberty and passed over its references to slavery, although Moore, who had travelled through the United States and Canada the decade before, had written in 1806 that the slavery still practised there had cast aside his hope for America's future greatness. Critics have differed: Matthew Campbell finds its harp, sword and "land of song" over-egged and its bathos far from the delicacy of "The Last Rose of Summer", while George Bernard Shaw called the words "visionary". Rafael María de Mendive translated the whole of the Irish Melodies into Spanish, published in New York in 1863; his "El trovador", shaped by his Cuban nationalism, kept the references to slaves at a time when slavery had not yet been abolished in Cuba. During the American Civil War an unknown author added a third verse that is sometimes sung.

The song entered later music and writing. Stanford quoted its first verse as the preface to his Fourth Irish Rhapsody, John Philip Sousa used it in his "Mother Hubbard March" (1885), and Benjamin Britten wove distorted forms of the tune through his opera Owen Wingrave. James Joyce parodied it in Finnegans Wake and quoted it in Ulysses, and William Brough parodied the words in his burlesque Lalla Rookh (1857). John McCormack, Paul Robeson, Tom Clancy and Shane MacGowan recorded it, and Joe Strummer's version, arranged by Hans Zimmer, was used in the film Black Hawk Down. Sir John Pope Hennessy had the 27th Inniskillings play it at formal occasions in Hong Kong; Sean Connery and Michael Caine sing it in the film The Man Who Would Be King (1975), and it was played at the opening of the World Trade Center Memorial on 11 September 2011, the tenth anniversary of the attacks.
== meaning
A young minstrel, a warrior bard, goes to war, and his harp, the romantic symbol of Ireland, is torn apart rather than sound in slavery. The song mourns a nation's lost freedom, proud and melancholy, with no promise that it will be restored.
