== story
In the autumn of 1862, at a gathering of Serbian and Ukrainian students in a Kyiv apartment, the ethnographer Pavlo Chubynskyi heard Serbian students sing Svetozar Miletić's "Srpska pesma" and, according to a memoirist who was there, wrote his poem on the spot. Scholars think it was also influenced by the Polish patriotic song "Poland Is Not Yet Lost" (1797), popular among the peoples of the former Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth then struggling for independence; the January Uprising began a few months later. The earliest Ukrainophiles took up the words at once, and in 1862 the head of the gendarmes, Prince Vasily Dolgorukov, exiled Chubynskyi to Arkhangelsk Governorate for his "dangerous influence on the minds of commoners".

The poem was first published in 1863 in the fourth issue of the Lviv journal Meta, which wrongly attributed it to Taras Shevchenko. It became popular in what is now Western Ukraine, and Mykhailo Verbytskyi, a priest of the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church and a prominent composer, set it to music in 1863. The first public choral performance was at the Ruska Besida Theatre in Lviv in 1864, and the words were first printed with his music in 1865. That year it was sung in Przemyśl, then in the Austrian Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria, at a commemoration of Shevchenko; this performance is the origin of Ukraine's National Anthem Day, held each year on 10 March. Mykhailo Zazuliak, an emigrant from Lviv living in New York, performed it as a folk song in 1915, and Columbia issued one of its first recordings in 1916.

In the first half of the 20th century, during attempts to found a Ukrainian state from lands of the Russian Empire, Poland and Austria-Hungary, the song served as the anthem of the Ukrainian People's Republic from 1917 (though other anthems were also used until 1921), of the West Ukrainian People's Republic, and in 1939 of Carpatho-Ukraine. After the Ukrainian SSR joined the Soviet Union in 1922 the song was banned, and it was rejected as a republican anthem in order to suppress separatist feeling. Between 1944 and 1949 Soviet authorities had a new anthem of the Ukrainian SSR written to a prescribed outline, with music by Anton Lebedynets and words by Pavlo Tychyna and Mykola Bazhan; it was adopted on 21 November 1949.

The song was sung as the de facto anthem at the inauguration of Leonid Kravchuk, the first president of independent Ukraine, on 5 December 1991. Parliament adopted it as the state anthem on 15 January 1992, and the Constitution of 28 June 1996 named Verbytskyi's music. The words followed only on 6 March 2003, when the Verkhovna Rada passed the Law on the State Anthem proposed by President Leonid Kuchma, by 334 votes of 450: the first stanza and refrain of Chubynskyi's poem, with the first line slightly changed.

The anthem became especially popular after the Orange Revolution of 2004 and the Euromaidan protests of 2013, when protesters, led by the singer Ruslana, sang it once an hour in the early weeks; Nadia Diuk called it the clarion call of that revolution. The composer Valentyn Sylvestrov heard in it the beginning of a church liturgy, a hallelujah. After the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022 several orchestras in Europe and North America played it in solidarity with Ukraine.
== meaning
A patriotic song declaring that Ukraine, its glory and its freedom live on, and calling its people to stand together for their freedom. Solemn and hymn-like rather than martial.

== notes
Status pd: words 1862, music 1863; the article gives no death years, but neither man can be in copyright. The official text is the 2003 law's slightly modified first stanza and refrain; the editor should confirm which version is imported (1862 original or the 2003 official text).
Title: the article's title is "National anthem of Ukraine"; the lead calls it "The State Anthem of Ukraine". The local title in Ukrainian ("Державний гімн України") is not in the article - it is a plain rendering of "State Anthem of Ukraine"; the article gives the Ukrainian law title "Закон «Про Державний гімн України»", from which it is taken. The romanization "Derzhavnyi himn Ukrainy" is mine. The Cyrillic of the original title is not in the article (only the romanizations "Shche ne vmerla Ukraina" / "Šče ne vmerla Ukrainy"), so only the romanization is given.
The meaning block is general; the article says only that the incipit recalls the struggle for national self-identity and independence.
Dates: the music was composed in 1863 and first printed with the words in 1865, but the first choral performance was in 1864. The lead gives the 1865 Przemyśl performance as the origin of 10 March Anthem Day.
Zazuliak/Zazulyak: the article spells the name both ways; I used Zazuliak in the story and the list's spelling in == inspired. It dates his performance 1915 and a Columbia record 1916.
Constitution: the article says it "designated Verbytskyi's music" on 28 June 1996; the lead says parliament adopted "Shche ne vmerla Ukrainy" on 15 January 1992 and the post-independence section says the anthem "had only officially consisted of Mykhailo Verbytskyi's music" until 2003.
Sources: the saved text has no footnote mapping; I listed the works whose titles match the facts used (1860s, Soviet anthem, Verbytskyi, general history, Euromaidan). Nadia Diuk's World Affairs article is named in the text without a title, so it is not listed.
