== story
"La Carmagnole" is a French song created and made popular during the French Revolution, together with a wild dance of the same name that may have come into France with the Piedmontese. It was first sung in August 1792, and new verses were added in the revolutionary years of 1830, 1848, 1863–64 and 1882–83. Its authors are not known. The name comes from the short jacket worn by the working-class militants called sans-culottes, taken from a Piedmontese peasant costume named after the town of Carmagnola.

It was sung mainly as a rallying cry and an entertainment of the revolutionaries, and it mocked King Louis XVI, Queen Marie Antoinette and the monarchists. After the storming of the Tuileries Palace on the night of 9–10 August 1792, when the people of Paris forced the king to flee to the National Assembly and massacred his Swiss Guard, the crowd danced and sang the Carmagnole to celebrate. It was also used against the Revolution's opponents: a common punishment was to make nobles, princes and clergy sing and dance it. It served as a battle song as well; at the Battle of Jemappes on 6 November 1792 the sans-culottes in the army charged singing "La Marseillaise" and "La Carmagnole", and the victory brought all of Belgium under the revolutionary armies.

Singing was a central way of taking part in the Revolution, and the French took pride in it as a source of their success. The Carmagnole was sung after victories and at popular festivals such as the planting of liberty trees, of which some 60,000 were planted during the Revolution. Like "Ah! ça ira", it was popular because its words were simple enough for people who could not read to learn and join in.

The song outlived the Revolution. The exile Carl Schurz recalled French friends in England dancing the Carmagnole after Napoleon III's coup of 2 December 1851. On the Greek island of Samos, then under Ottoman rule, it gave its name to the Karmanioloi, a faction inspired by the Revolution's liberal and democratic ideals that opposed the old landholding notables and led Samos to join the Greek War of Independence in 1821. It appears in Charles Dickens's A Tale of Two Cities, Baroness Orczy's The Scarlet Pimpernel and Gertrud von Le Fort's The Song at the Scaffold, is sung in Umberto Giordano's opera Andrea Chénier and Eugene O'Neill's The Iceman Cometh, and Victor Hugo set it against "La Marseillaise" in Les Misérables.
== meaning
A mocking song of triumph over the king, the queen and their supporters, sung with a wild dance. Its mood is sarcastic and defiant, and its words were simple enough for anyone to learn and join in.
