== story
"Guan ju" is the first poem of the Shi Jing, the Classic of Poetry, and one of the best-known poems in Chinese literature. It has been dated to the seventh century BC, which makes it one of China's oldest poems, though not the oldest in the anthology. It opens the "Zhou nan" section of the "Airs of the States", the 160 poems that make up the larger part of the anthology's 305, and is typical of them: three stanzas of four-syllable lines. Its title comes from its first line, which calls up ospreys crying on a river islet. At heart the poem is about finding a good and fair maiden as a match for a young noble. Each stanza sets an image from nature beside the human scene without comment, a device early commentators called xing.

The earliest comment on it is in the Analects, where Confucius praises it for joy without wantonness and sorrow without self-injury; Brooks and Brooks date that passage to 342 BCE, perhaps when the poem first came to prominence. A legend held that Confucius himself chose the songs of the Shi Jing from three thousand for their moral worth, and Confucian readers came to treat the poems as morally instructive. Three early schools, of Lu, Qi and Han, read it in different ways. The Lu school, probably dominant under the Han dynasty, took it as political criticism of King Kang of Zhou, who was said to have risen late one morning; the historian Sima Qian wrote that it was composed when the house of Zhou was in decline. This reading lasted into the fifth century CE.

The Mao school, named after the early annotators Mao Heng and Mao Chang, prevailed and is the only tradition that survives whole. It read the poem as praise of Tai Si, queen of King Wen, founder of the Zhou dynasty, and its notes were edited by Kong Yingda in the seventh century as Mao shi zheng yi. Zheng Xuan, a century after the Maos, held that the young lady was not the queen but palace ladies whom the queen sought as further consorts for the king. The Song dynasty Neo-Confucian Zhu Xi largely agreed with the Mao school but thought the poem had been composed by the ladies of the palace. His contemporary Zheng Qiao called it simply an old folk song. A text found at Mawangdui in 1973, the Wuxingpian, reads it instead as a poem about bringing sexual desire into line with the customs of one's community.

Western scholars began to study the Shi Jing in the second half of the nineteenth century. Séraphin Couvreur, its first French translator, kept to the Mao reading, but James Legge, whose English translation appeared in 1871, rejected the Mao prefaces. Marcel Granet, in his 1911 study Fêtes et Chansons anciennes de la Chine, read it as a song of a rural courting festival; Qu Wanli saw a man about to be married, his bride greeted at the end with wedding music. Arthur Waley, C. H. Wang and Pauline Yu rejected the allegorical readings, while Edward L. Shaughnessy read the fish-eating osprey as an image of the search for a partner.

The poem has been alluded to throughout Chinese literature, one of the earliest times by Cai Yong (died 192) in his "Qingyi fu". Three of its phrases have become well-known four-character idioms (chengyu), still quoted in modern Chinese writing and speech, and the poem remains a common subject of study in Chinese studies.
== meaning
A poem of courtship: a young noble's longing for a fitting bride, first unfulfilled and then fulfilled, set beside images from nature. Later readers have taken it as a wedding song, a festival courting song, a moral lesson on restraint, or praise of a virtuous queen.
