== story
The words of "Kimigayo" are an anonymous waka poem first found in the Kokin Wakashū, an anthology of about 920, which makes them the oldest words of any national anthem. The poem was copied into many later anthologies and sung by people of every rank as a wish for long life, at birthdays and other informal occasions, so no music was written down for it until the 19th century. Its opening words were changed in the Kamakura period, when samurai sang it as a festive song; in the Edo period it was popular among the people, and was sung as a New Year song in the Ōoku of Edo Castle and in the Satsuma domain. There the kimi, "lord", meant the Tokugawa shōgun, the Shimazu rulers of Satsuma, guests of honour or the members of a drinking party, not the emperor.

In 1869 John William Fenton, bandmaster of a British military band in Yokohama, noticed that Japan had no national anthem and suggested one to Iwao Ōyama, an officer of the Satsuma domain, who chose the old poem, perhaps for its likeness to the British anthem. Fenton had two or three weeks to set it, and played it before the emperor in 1870. The government dropped his tune as lacking solemnity; others hold that it was unsingable for Japanese voices. It is still performed every year at Myōkōji temple in Yokohama, a memorial to him. In 1880 the Ministry of the Imperial Household adopted a new melody by Yoshiisa Oku and Akimori Hayashi, often credited to Akimori's father and supervisor Hiromori Hayashi. Based on a mode of court music but influenced by Western hymns, it was given Western harmony by the German musician Franz Eckert. The government adopted it as the national anthem in 1888, and by 1893 it was sung at public school ceremonies.

After the Meiji Restoration, when men of Satsuma led the government, the song came to be understood as wishing the emperor a long reign. Some objected at the turn of the 20th century: an Osaka paper in 1904 called it a song of the imperial family, not of the state, and the Christian leader Uchimura Kanzō argued that an anthem should express the feelings of the people. The Japanese came to know it widely as their anthem through the celebrations of victory in the First Sino-Japanese and Russo-Japanese Wars. During the Second World War, schoolchildren in Japan and its colonies were ordered to sing it and salute Emperor Hirohito every morning.

The Allied occupation issued no directive restricting the song, unlike the Hinomaru flag, and it stayed the de facto anthem; NHK used it to open and close its broadcasts. Under the 1947 Constitution the emperor became a symbol of the state, and the Ministry of Education neither gave the song a new meaning nor renounced the old one. In 1950 the Ministry called its use at schools desirable but not required; it first called it the national anthem in 1977, and in 1989 required respect for it at school ceremonies, with penalties for officials who did not comply. In 1999, after a Hiroshima school principal killed himself over a dispute between his school board and his teachers about the flag and anthem, the government of Keizō Obuchi passed the Act on National Flag and Anthem, enacted on 13 August 1999 after a 403 to 86 vote in the House of Representatives. The ruling Liberal Democratic Party and Komeito supported it; the Social Democratic and Communist parties opposed it, citing the symbols' links with the war era. The government defined kimi as the emperor as symbol of the state and the people's unity; Kazuo Shii of the Communist Party argued that an anthem about the emperor did not suit a democracy.

The Act does not say how respect is to be shown, but a Tokyo Metropolitan Government directive of October 2003 requires teachers to stand and sing at graduation ceremonies. Between 23 October 2003 and 2008, 410 teachers and school workers in Tokyo were punished for refusing; in 2006 the Tokyo District Court ordered compensation for punished teachers, and the Metropolitan Government appealed. Osaka teachers refused to sing in 2010, 2011 and 2012. On 30 May and 6 June 2011 the Supreme Court of Japan ruled that requiring teachers to stand and sing was constitutional. The anthem is played at sporting events and before the awards at sumō tournaments, and many students say they cannot understand its old language.
== meaning
A short classical poem that wishes long life to the "kimi", the one addressed. The word first meant a lord or a dear one; from the Meiji era to 1945 the song was understood as wishing the emperor a long reign, and since 1999 the government has read it as a wish for the lasting peace and prosperity of Japan under the emperor as symbol of the state.
