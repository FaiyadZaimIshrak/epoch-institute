== story
The Gita Govinda is a work of songs composed in Sanskrit by the 12th-century Hindu poet Jayadeva. It tells of Krishna and Radha and the gopis, the cowherd women of Vrindavan: Krishna's love for Radha, his straying and his return to her, a story read as the human soul turning away from God and coming back. The poem has twelve chapters, each named for a mood of Krishna, from exuberant to exultant, divided into twenty-four prabandhas; the songs within them are ashtapadis, groups of eight couplets. It also sets out the eight moods of the heroine, the Ashta Nayika.

It is one of the earliest musical texts in which the author names the raga (mode) and tala (rhythm) for every song. Most of the ragas and talas Jayadeva indicated, such as Mangala Gujjari and the āṭhatāli rhythm, are unique to the tradition of Odissi music and have stayed in use in Odisha for centuries. The work was known at Puri soon after it was written: the earliest commentary, Udayanacharya's Bhavavibhavini Tika, was written in Odisha around 1190. Many more followed, among them the Rasikapriya of Rana Kumbha and, in the Gaudiya Vaishnava school, the commentaries of Prabhodananda Sarasvati and of Chaitanyadasa.

At the Jagannatha temple of Puri the Gita Govinda is sung every night during the Badasinghara, the temple's last ritual of the day, to traditional Odissi ragas and talas. Jayadeva himself is said to have sung in the temple, and the tradition has continued unbroken since his time. After him its singing became a required temple service performed by the Maharis or Devadasis, recorded in inscriptions, in the temple chronicle Mādalā Pānji and in other official documents. In Kerala musicians adapted the ashtapadis into sopana sangeetham, a form sung in temples, and the poem's moods of the heroine have inspired many compositions and dances in India's classical dance traditions.

The poem became known in Europe through Sir William Jones's English translation, published in the Transactions of the Asiatic Society in Calcutta in 1792, which named Kenduli in Kalinga (ancient Odisha) as the poet's birthplace. F. H. van Dalberg's German version, made from Jones, was read by Goethe, and Friedrich Rückert began a verse translation in 1829. Later English versions include Edwin Arnold's The Indian Song of Songs (1875) and Barbara Stoler Miller's Love Song of the Dark Lord (1977). Miniature paintings of the Gita Govinda are held in museums from Bhubaneswar and New Delhi to New York, Zürich and Paris.
== meaning
A cycle of songs on the love of Krishna and Radha: their longing, separation, jealousy and reunion, told through the moods of the lovers and of Radha's companion. Devotees read the lovers' parting and return as the soul's separation from God and its return. The mood moves from yearning to joy.
