== story
"Auld Lang Syne" is a poem in Scots that Robert Burns wrote in 1788 on the basis of an older Scottish folk song. Burns sent it to the Scots Musical Museum that year, describing it as an old song that had never been printed or written down until he took it down from an old man. Part of it was indeed collected rather than composed: a ballad "Old Long Syne", printed by James Watson in 1711, is close to Burns's first verse and chorus, and both probably come from the same old song. The rest is usually attributed to Burns himself. The title phrase, roughly "old long since" or "days gone by", also appears in poems by Robert Ayton and Allan Ramsay.

The words first had another tune, traceable to about 1700, which Burns thought mediocre. The melody sung today was first printed in 1799 in George Thomson's Select Songs of Scotland, which also moved a verse to its present place. It is a pentatonic Scots folk tune, probably first a quick dance; it is not certain that Burns meant his words for it. William Shield seems to quote it in his opera Rosina (1782), perhaps from a common source such as the strathspey "The Miller's Wedding". Haydn arranged the song for Thomson's collections, and Beethoven published an arrangement in 1814.

Singing it at Hogmanay, the Scottish New Year, quickly became a custom in Scotland and spread across the British Isles, and emigrants from Scotland, England, Wales and Ireland carried it around the world. In the Scottish custom the singers stand in a circle holding hands and cross their arms only at the start of the last verse; crossing hands at parting arose in the mid-19th century among Freemasons and other fraternal societies. Guy Lombardo's band played it on New Year's Eve from 1929 for decades, and his 1947 recording is still played in Times Square after the ball drops.

The song became a general song of endings and farewells: at funerals, graduations, Burns suppers, the close of Scout jamborees and the passing-out parades of military academies across the Commonwealth. It was played at British handovers of power, among them the departure of Hong Kong's last British governor, Chris Patten, on 30 June 1997. Its pentatonic tune suited musical traditions in Asia: it is the melody of Japan's "Hotaru no Hikari", sung at graduations, and of a well-known song by Rabindranath Tagore. From 1919 to 1945 it carried the anthem of the Korean exile government, from 1945 to 1948 South Korea's national anthem was sung to it, and the Maldives used it for their anthem until 1972.

The first recording was made on a wax cylinder in 1898 by Charles Samuel Myers and Alfred Cort Haddon during an expedition to record the music of the Torres Strait Islanders. Members of the European Parliament sang it in January 2020 when the Brexit withdrawal agreement was passed.
== meaning
A song of friendship and memory: it asks whether old friends and old times should be forgotten, and answers by calling for them to be remembered together. The singers share a drink and recall a shared youth that has since been divided by distance, then join hands. The mood is warm, a little wistful, and festive.
