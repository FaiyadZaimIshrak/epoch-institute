== story
Psalm 104 is the 104th psalm of the Book of Psalms; in the slightly different numbering of the Greek Septuagint and the Latin Vulgate it is Psalm 103, known in Latin as "Benedic anima mea Domino". One of the longer psalms, it is traditionally divided into 35 verses. It is the first place in the Old Testament where the word "Hallelujah" occurs, followed by Psalms 105 and 106, which O. Palmer Robertson sees with it as a triad closing the fourth book of the Psalms. In the Masoretic text the Hallelujah stands at the end of its last verse and is read as the opening of the next psalm; the Septuagint and the Vulgate lack it.

The psalm is a hymn of creation whose order follows the creation story of Genesis 1. Commentators find in it a chiastic structure: it opens and closes with praise of God's royal splendour, and between them come the forming of the earth, the waters and the animals, the regular round of the moon and the sun, the sea with its creatures, and the dependence of every creature on God's continuing care. It makes clear that even "the deep" was created by God and did not exist before him, as in Babylonian creation myth, and it names the Leviathan as just another of God's creatures rather than a rival to his authority, as surrounding Canaanite peoples believed. Its last verse wishes that sinners and the wicked be consumed out of the earth.

The psalm bears a notable resemblance to Akhenaten's Great Hymn to the Aten, written some 400 years earlier in Egypt, and it is often cited as a parallel text. Commentators note that in the biblical text the sun and the rest of creation are not gods but creations, so that the psalm is "implicitly antipagan" and seems to comment on such hymns rather than copy them. Mark S. Smith observes that enthusiasm for even indirect influence has cooled in recent decades and that some reject it outright, while Egyptologists such as Jan Assmann and Donald Redford still argue for Egyptian influence.

The psalm has a regular place in Jewish, Eastern Orthodox, Catholic, Lutheran, Anglican and other Protestant worship. The New Testament quotes its fourth verse in the Letter to the Hebrews. Many observant Jews recite it in the morning service of the New Moon (Rosh Chodesh), and many communities after the Sabbath afternoon service between Sukkot and Shabbat Hagadol; single verses stand in the daily prayers. In the Eastern Orthodox Church it opens Vespers every day, read by the senior reader, or sung by the choir with refrains at the All-Night Vigil on feast days; there it is often taken as Adam's song outside the closed gates of Eden, while the priest stands before the closed Royal Doors. The Catholic Church sings it as the responsorial psalm after the creation reading of the Easter Vigil, and again at Pentecost; the Book of Common Prayer appoints it for the evening of the twentieth day of the month and for Evensong on Whitsunday. The theologian Jaime Pérez de Valencia (1408–1490) read its ninth verse as confirming Ptolemy's view that the oceans were surrounded by land, and Johann Gottfried Herder remarked that it was worth studying Hebrew for ten years to read it in the original.

It has often been set to music. John Dowland's English setting appeared in Thomas Est's Whole Booke of Psalmes of 1592, Heinrich Schütz set a metrical German text for the Becker Psalter of 1628, and Bach set two of its verses in his cantata Es wartet alles auf dich (1726). Sir Robert Grant's hymn "O Worship the King", first published in 1833, is based on it, and Philip Glass set it in Hebrew in his opera Akhnaten. Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, commissioned a setting from William Lovelady for his 75th birthday; an abridged version for four-part choir and organ was sung at his funeral in St George's Chapel, Windsor, on 17 April 2021.
== meaning
A hymn that praises God as a king robed in light who made and keeps the world: he set the earth on its foundations and bounded the waters, sends springs and rain for the wild animals, the birds, the herds and the crops of people, made the moon and sun to mark the times of night and day, and filled the sea with creatures. Every living thing waits on him and lives by his breath. The mood is one of wonder and joyful praise, closing in a vow to sing to God for life.
