== story
"Nun danket alle Gott" was written around 1636 by Martin Rinkart, a Lutheran pastor who came to Eilenburg in Saxony at the beginning of the Thirty Years' War. The walled town became a refuge for political and military fugitives, and the overcrowding brought deadly pestilence and famine; armies overran it three times. Rinkart's home sheltered the victims, though he was often hard-pressed to feed his own family. In the severe plague of 1637 he was the only pastor left alive in Eilenburg, burying as many as 50 people in a day and more than 4,000 in that year, his own wife among them.

A prolific writer of hymns, Rinkart printed "Nun danket alle Gott" in his Jesu Hertz-Büchlein (Leipzig, 1636) under the title "Tisch-Gebetlein", a short prayer before meals. Its exact date is debated, but it was widely sung by the time the Peace of Westphalia was signed in 1648. Johann Crüger published it with its tune (Zahn No. 5142) in the 1647 edition of his Praxis pietatis melica; the melody is sometimes attributed to Rinkart but usually considered Crüger's. In English it is sung as "Now thank we all our God", in the 19th-century translation by Catherine Winkworth, mostly to a modified form of the melody.

Johann Sebastian Bach used the hymn in his cantatas, among them BWV 79 and BWV 192, harmonised it for four voices and set it as a chorale prelude, BWV 657, among the Great Eighteen. Felix Mendelssohn made the harmonisation now in standard use in 1840, when he took the hymn, in G major and with its German words, as the chorale of his Lobgesang (Hymn of Praise, Symphony No. 2). Max Reger wrote a chorale prelude on it in 1902, Sigfrid Karg-Elert used it in his Marche Triomphale, John Rutter set it for choir and brass in 1974 and Václav Nelhýbel made a concertato for brass and organ in 1977.

A story holds that after the Battle of Leuthen in 1757 the whole assembled Prussian army took up the hymn, and from it the melody is sometimes called the Leuthen Chorale. Historians and musicologists question the story and identify it as a later invention of Prussian propaganda.
== meaning
A hymn of thanks to God, first printed as a short prayer before meals by a pastor of a town overrun by armies during the Thirty Years' War. It is joyful and steady in mood.
