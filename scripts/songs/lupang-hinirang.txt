== story
"Lupang Hinirang" ("Chosen Land"), first titled in Spanish "Marcha Nacional Filipina" ("Philippine National March") and often called by its opening words, is the national anthem of the Philippines. It began as music without words. Emilio Aguinaldo, head of the Dictatorial Government of the Philippines, commissioned it on 5 June 1898 for the proclamation of independence from Spain, to replace a march he had found unsatisfactory, and gave the task to Julián Felipe. First called "Marcha Filipina-Magdalo", it was renamed "Marcha Nacional Filipina" on its adoption as the national anthem on 11 June 1898. Felipe said he had based it on three pieces: the "Marcha Real" of Spain, the Grand March from Giuseppe Verdi's Aida and "La Marseillaise". The Banda San Francisco de Malabón, now the Banda Matanda of General Trias, played it in public for the first time at the proclamation of independence at Aguinaldo's house in Kawit, Cavite, on 12 June 1898. It was adopted again as the national march of the Philippine Republic in 1899.

The words came a year later. In August 1899 the soldier and writer José Palma wrote the Spanish poem "Filipinas", which was itself drawn from a Kapampangan poem, "Labuad Mapalad" ("Blessed Land"), written in September 1898 by Mariano Proceso Pabalan of Bacolor, Pampanga. "Filipinas" was first published on 3 September 1899, for the first anniversary of the newspaper La Independencia, and was then set to Felipe's march. In the years after the revolution it became widely popular as the anthem's unofficial Spanish words.

After the defeat of the First Republic in the Philippine–American War, the islands came under United States rule. The Flag Act of 1907 forbade the public display of the flags, banners and emblems of the Philippine Republican Army; it did not forbid the march itself, yet in 1909 an entire band was imprisoned for playing it at a fiesta. When the act was repealed in 1919 the march regained its popular place as the national anthem, and the Insular Government had the Spanish words put into English: first by the poet Paz Márquez Benítez of the University of the Philippines, then in the best-known translation, the "Philippine Hymn" by Senator Camilo Osías and Mary A. Lane. It was played beside "The Star-Spangled Banner" until independence in 1946. Under the Commonwealth, Commonwealth Act No. 382 of 5 September 1938 adopted Felipe's music as the national anthem; it concerned only the music. In the 1920s the anthem's time was changed from 2/4 to 4/4 and its key from C major to G to make it easier to sing, though this was never written into law.

Tagalog versions appeared in the 1940s. The first known, "Diwa ng Bayan" ("Spirit of the Country"), was sung during the Japanese occupation; "O Sintang Lupa" ("O Beloved Land") by Julián Cruz Balmaceda, Ildefonso Santos and Francisco Caballo became the official version in 1948. Under President Ramon Magsaysay, Education Secretary Gregorio Hernández formed a commission to revise the words, and the Tagalog "Lupang Hinirang" was first sung on 26 May 1956. With small revisions in the 1960s, this version by Felipe Padilla de León is the one used today. Republic Act No. 8491, the Flag and Heraldic Code, passed on 12 February 1998, made these Filipino words law and set aside the Spanish and English versions: the anthem must be sung in the national language, inside the country or abroad, "with fervor", and schools must see that every student learns it by heart.

Under the Marcos dictatorship, until the EDSA Revolution of 1986, opposition parties, activists and unions opened their protests with the anthem, singing it with the raised clenched fist instead of the hand on the heart. The law requires that it follow Felipe's original arrangement, but his original manuscript cannot be found. The National Historical Institute holds that it should be played in 2/4 time at 100 beats a minute and last 53 seconds, and has praised or criticised singers at televised boxing matches of Manny Pacquiao for going too slow or too fast. Proposals to change its last line, by the musician Joey Ayala in 2013 and Senate President Vicente Sotto III in 2018, met with mixed or hostile reactions. Earlier, in November 1896, the composer Julio Nakpil had written "Marangal na Dalit ng Katagalugan" as a national anthem for Andrés Bonifacio of the Katipunan, but Aguinaldo's governments never adopted it.
== meaning
A hymn of devotion to the homeland, which calls the country a daughter of the eastern sun in Palma's Spanish and ends with its people's readiness to die for it. Its mood is solemn and fervent.
