== story
"Erika" is a German marching song with words and music by Herms Niel, published in 1938 by the firm of Louis Oertel in Großburgwedel, a village north-east of Hanover in Lower Saxony. Its date is often given as "about 1930", but this has never been substantiated; in an interview with the Viennese newspaper Das Kleine Volksblatt, Niel said he was suddenly inspired to write it in the summer of 1938 after a comic mishap on a walk, when his dog ran into a wasps' nest. Erika is both a German woman's name and the German word for heather.

Niel joined the Nazi Party in early May 1933 and rose under the regime to be leading bandmaster of the music corps of the Reich Labour Service. He wrote many marching songs that largely served Nazi propaganda. Joseph Goebbels, the propaganda minister, valued simple popular songs as a tool, and between 1933 and 1945 some 15,000 Nazi musical works were produced, spread through the new mass media of film and radio.

The song was soon taken up by the Wehrmacht as a soldiers' song, and according to the British soldier and historian Major General Michael Tillotson it was the most popular marching song in Germany during the Second World War. After every line, and after each time the name is sung, a three-beat pause is filled by the bass drum or, without a band, by the stamp of marching feet; the German article notes that the sound recalls gunfire and gives the song its character as a war song. It belonged to the run of popular songs with women's names in which soldiers sang of the wives and sweethearts at home, and its picture of the faithful, waiting woman matched the role the Nazi regime promoted for women; soldiers also sang ruder versions. The writer Gregor von Rezzori recalled it on German radio in April 1945 among the reports of victorious "defensive battles", and it appears in many novels about the Nazi period, among them Walter Kempowski's Heile Welt.

Abroad the song has been heard as typically German but remains closely tied to the Wehrmacht: in 1983, at the tenth anniversary of the junta in Chile, a Chilean army band in field grey and Wehrmacht-style helmets played it, and Andrei Eshpai quoted it in his Symphony No. 5 (1985), perhaps to depict the Wehrmacht's advance. In Germany it stayed popular after the war, detached from its origin: it was sung on marches of the Bundeswehr and the Austrian army after rearmament, recorded as a folk-style song, for instance by Tony Marshall in 1974, and is regarded by some as a folk song. It is used in films about the war, among them Steven Spielberg's Schindler's List (1993), and there are versions in Afrikaans, best known by the tenor Gé Korsten, and in Finnish as "Kaarina".

In recent years its use by right-wing groups has drawn condemnation. In June 2025 members of the University of Warwick Conservative Association sang and danced to it at an event, which university representatives condemned; in December 2025 two representatives of a conservative student party at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam were arrested over an assault on a student who, witnesses said, had criticised them for singing it; and on 6 September 2026 the Alternative for Germany's official YouTube channel played a remix of it during its broadcast on the night of the Saxony-Anhalt state election.
== meaning
A marching song about blooming heather and a sweetheart waiting at home, its pauses filled by drumbeats or marching feet; brisk and martial.
