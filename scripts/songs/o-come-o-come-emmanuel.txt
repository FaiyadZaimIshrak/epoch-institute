== story
"O Come, O Come, Emmanuel", in Latin "Veni, veni, Emmanuel", is a Christian hymn for Advent, the season before Christmas, and is often printed among Christmas carols. Its words are a rhymed paraphrase of the O Antiphons, short chants sung with the Magnificat at Vespers on the last days before Christmas. The antiphons go back to monastic life of the 8th or 9th century, and that has led to a common belief that the hymn is just as old; claims that it dates from the 11th or 12th century are often repeated. But the Latin hymn first appears in print in 1710, in the seventh edition of the Psalteriolum Cantionum Catholicarum, published in Cologne. That hymnal, first assembled by the Jesuit Johannes Heringsdorf in 1610 and used widely in Jesuit schools, was a major force in German church music.

The 1710 hymn has five verses, each paraphrasing one antiphon, with a two-line refrain of rejoicing; two antiphons are left out, and the antiphon of Emmanuel is moved to the front, which gave the hymn its name. In 1844 the German hymnologist Hermann Adalbert Daniel included it in the second volume of his Thesaurus Hymnologicus. A seven-verse form, with two added verses of unknown authorship for the missing antiphons, was printed in Joseph Hermann Mohr's Cantiones Sacrae in 1878.

The English hymn grew from Daniel's collection. John Mason Neale published the Latin text in Britain in his Hymni Ecclesiae of 1851, and in the same year the first known English translation, in Mediæval Hymns and Sequences, under a different first line. He revised it for The Hymnal Noted and again, in 1861, for Hymns Ancient and Modern, where it took the first line by which it is known. Thomas Alexander Lacey made a new translation for The English Hymnal in 1906, Henry Sloane Coffin translated the two extra verses in 1916, and a full seven-verse English version appeared in the Episcopal Church's hymnal of 1940.

The words and the tune grew up apart. Thomas Helmore first joined them in The Hymnal Noted in 1851, naming his source as "a French Missal in the National Library, Lisbon"; he gave no way to check it, and some suspected that he had written the melody himself. In 1966 the British musicologist Mary Berry, an Augustinian canoness writing as Mother Thomas More, found the melody in a 15th-century French processional of chants for burials in the National Library of France, where it carries another text, "Bone Jesu dulcis cunctis", as part of a series of tropes. No evidence links the tune to the hymn before Helmore, so the two first came together in English.

The pairing suited its moment. The Hymnal Noted, made up entirely of Latin hymns in English with music drawn mainly from plainchant, was a product of the Oxford Movement in the Church of England, and the hymn was taken up for its medieval air and its fit to the season. Hymns Ancient and Modern, which was used in three-quarters of English churches by 1895, carried it everywhere, and its 1861 form remains the usual one in the English-speaking world. In German-speaking lands, where the text began, other tunes were sung with it for many years: one with a paraphrase attributed to Christoph Bernhard Verspoell, popular in the Diocese of Münster, and one from Düsseldorf (1836) with Heinrich Bone's translation, which Johann Baptist Singenberger took to Catholic communities in the United States. Some American Lutheran hymnals sing it to Dmitry Bortniansky's tune "St. Petersburg".
== meaning
A hymn of longing for the coming of the Messiah. Each verse calls on him by one of the titles of the ancient Advent antiphons and asks him to come, and each ends in a refrain of rejoicing that he will be born. The mood moves from yearning to joy.
