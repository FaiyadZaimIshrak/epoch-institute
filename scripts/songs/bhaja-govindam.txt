== story
"Bhaja Govindam" ("praise Govinda", or "seek Govinda"), also called "Moha Mudgara" ("destroyer of illusion"), is a popular Hindu devotional poem in Sanskrit traditionally attributed to Adi Shankara. Its opening stanza is a refrain repeated after each verse, as Swami Chinmayananda describes it. The twelve verses after it, the Dvadasa-manjarika-stotram, are traditionally Shankara's own; fourteen more, the Caturdasa-manjarika-stotram, are attributed to fourteen of his disciples. Editions differ in the number, order, wording and arrangement of the stanzas, but not in their meaning. The poem is written in a moraic metre, measured in syllabic instants (mātrās) rather than a fixed number of syllables: apart from the first verse, every verse has sixteen mātrās, the padakulakam of the metrical handbook Vrtta-ratnakara.

A traditional story tells how it came to be. Walking with his disciples along a street in Varanasi, Shankara met an old scholar reciting the rules of Panini's Sanskrit grammar over and over. Taking pity on him, he advised the man not to spend his last years on grammar but to turn his mind to God in worship, which alone could free him from the cycle of birth and death; the hymn is said to have been composed then. It shows that Shankara, often regarded as the reviver of the path of knowledge (jnana marga) to liberation, also taught the path of devotion (bhakti marga), the emphasis of the bhakti movement. In his commentary C. Rajagopalachari wrote that knowledge which has matured is spoken of as devotion, and that knowledge which does not become devotion is "useless tinsel". Many scholars hold that the poem sums up, briefly and simply, the substance of Shankara's Vedantic thought, and because its refrain invokes God as Krishna it is sung not only by Shankara's own followers, the Smarthas, but also by Vaishnavas and others.
== meaning
A call to turn from worldly learning and the ego towards devotion to God and surrender to him, which alone frees a person from the cycle of birth and death; urgent in tone, brought back again and again by its refrain.
