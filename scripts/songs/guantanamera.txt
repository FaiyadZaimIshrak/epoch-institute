== story
"Guantanamera", Spanish for "the woman from Guantánamo", is a Cuban patriotic song whose verses come from poems by the Cuban poet José Martí. The official writing credit goes to Joseíto Fernández, who popularized it on the radio from as early as 1929 and used it regularly in one of his programmes; it is not clear when it was first released on record. Fernández claimed to have written the music at various dates, and the consensus places its origin in 1929. Some hold that the song's structure came from Herminio "El Diablo" García Wilson, who could be credited as co-composer; his heirs went to court decades later and lost, and in 1993 the People's Supreme Court of Cuba named Fernández sole composer of the music. On either account, Fernández was the first to spread the song widely.

The version known outside Cuba joined Martí's verses to the tune. Pete Seeger based his reworking on a version by Martí and Julián Orbón, as performed by Héctor Angulo, and meant it for the peace movement at the time of the Cuban Missile Crisis: he urged people to sing it, in Spanish, as a sign of unity between the American and Cuban peoples. The Weavers sang it at their Carnegie Hall reunion concert in May 1963, and shortly afterwards Seeger put it on his live album We Shall Overcome, a recording AllMusic's Stewart Mason calls the definitive version.

The Sandpipers' 1966 recording, based on the Weavers' arrangement, arranged by Mort Garson and produced by Tommy LiPuma, became an international hit, reaching No. 9 on the Billboard Hot 100 and No. 7 in Britain. Celia Cruz (1925–2003) made it one of her signature songs: it appears on at least 241 of her records and compilations, from a 1968 recording on Tico Records onward, and her posthumous autobiography of 2004 recalls singing it nine times. Wyclef Jean's version, with Celia Cruz, Jeni Fujita and a rap verse by Lauryn Hill, added new words and music and was nominated for a Grammy Award.

The song has been recorded by many others, from Joan Baez, Julio Iglesias and Nana Mouskouri to Inti-Illimani, the Buena Vista Social Club and the Gipsy Kings; Bárbara y Dick took it to number one in Argentina. Its tune is also widely used for British football chants, and the Pakistani singer Alamgir adapted it in the 1980s with Urdu words as "Albela Rahi".
== meaning
A song of love for Cuba and of solidarity with the poor of the world. Its mood is warm and patriotic.
