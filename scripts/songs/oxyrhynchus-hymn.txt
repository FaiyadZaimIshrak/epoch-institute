== story
The Oxyrhynchus hymn, catalogued as P. Oxy. XV 1786, is the earliest known manuscript of a Christian hymn in Greek that preserves both its words and its musical notation. The papyrus dates from around the end of the 3rd century AD. It was found in 1918 at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt and published in 1922, and was kept in the Papyrology Rooms of the Sackler Library in Oxford, renamed the Bodleian Art, Archaeology and Ancient World Library in 2022.

The music is written in Greek vocal notation, the system of the classical Greek musicians who came before. The melody is entirely diatonic, spans exactly one octave and is notated in the Hypolydian; rhythmic signs mark the lengths of the notes. The words are set mostly one note to a syllable, with a few short melismas, in a largely anapaestic metre. That educated Greek-speaking Christians wrote their hymn in this notation shows them taking over the musical writing of their pagan predecessors.

The hymn is the only surviving fragment of notated Christian Greek music from the first four hundred years of the Christian era, although the historian and musician Kenneth Levy argued that the Sanctus melody best preserved in the Western medieval Requiem Mass goes back to around the 4th century. With the Phos Hilaron, it is one of the two earliest Christian Greek hymn texts reasonably certain to have been used in worship that are neither taken from the Bible nor modelled on biblical passages.

Ensembles that reconstruct ancient Greek music have recorded it, among them Atrium Musicæ de Madrid under Gregorio Paniagua (1979), Christodoulos Halaris (1992), Ensemble De Organographia (1995) and Ensemble Kérylos under Annie Bélis (1996). In 2025 it was adapted as a contemporary worship song in The First Hymn, a documentary project led by the Australian historian John Dickson, with an arrangement by Chris Tomlin and Ben Fielding based on the original text and melody.
== meaning
A hymn of praise to the Christian Trinity. It calls for silence across creation so that the praise may be heard, a picture of cosmic stillness that older Greek hymns also used.
