== story
"The Holly and the Ivy" is a traditional British folk Christmas carol, number 514 in the Roud Folk Song Index. The song itself can be traced only as far back as the early nineteenth century, but its words reflect a link between holly and Christmas that goes back at least to medieval times. Since then the plant has carried a Christian symbolism: in the carol the holly stands for Jesus and the ivy for his mother, the Virgin Mary. The sharp leaves have been read as a reminder of the crown of thorns and the red berries of drops of blood, and holly and ivy are recorded regularly as church decorations for Advent and Christmas in churchwardens' accounts of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

The words occur in three broadsides published in Birmingham in the early 19th century. An early mention of the title is in William Hone's Ancient Mysteries Described of 1823, which lists it among the Christmas carols "now annually printed" that Hone owned. The complete words appear in a book review of 1849, whose anonymous writer took them from a collection of carols "printed in the cheapest form, at Birmingham", and praised such religious ballads as common in country districts and the humbler quarters of England's great towns, many of them kept only by word of mouth. Sylvester's A Garland of Christmas Carols (1861) printed the words and said they came from "an old broadside, printed a century and a half since", that is, around 1711; Husk's Songs of the Nativity (1864) remarked that the carol had nearly escaped the notice of collectors and was still printed on the Birmingham broadsides.

In traditional communities the words and the melody varied greatly. The early nineteenth-century sources give no music, and several late nineteenth-century collections set the words to an "old French carol" in D minor. The words were sung to many folk melodies; three further tunes were collected in Gloucestershire alone, and recordings survive of Peter Jones of Ross-on-Wye, Herefordshire, and of Bessie Wallace of Camborne, Cornwall, recorded in the early 1930s by James Madison Carpenter. The most popular traditional variant seems to have been "The Holly Bears a Berry", and the "Sans Day Carol", regarded as a variant of the song, has a different tune.

The version now sung everywhere was collected by the English folk song collector Cecil Sharp from Mary Clayton in the market town of Chipping Campden, Gloucestershire. Sharp's manuscript of her singing of the third verse is dated 13 January 1909 and is kept in the Cecil Sharp Manuscript Collection at Clare College, Cambridge. He published the words and the melody in English Folk-Carols in 1911, the book that fixed the carol in its present form. The melody keeps to the six notes of a hexachord.

Sir Henry Walford Davies wrote a choral arrangement that is often sung at the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols and by choirs around the world. Sharp also linked the carol to an older one, which he described as "The Contest of the Ivy and the Holly", a contest between the traditional emblems of woman and man.
== meaning
A folk carol that praises the holly above all the trees of the wood and takes its parts one by one: blossom, berry, prickle and bark. Each is set beside Mary bearing Jesus and beside what he would suffer. A refrain of sunrise, running deer, organ music and choir singing returns after every verse. The mood is bright, with the Passion in the background.
