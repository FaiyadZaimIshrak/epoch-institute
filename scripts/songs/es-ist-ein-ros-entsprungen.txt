== story
"Es ist ein Ros entsprungen" ("A rose has sprung up") is a Christmas carol and Marian hymn of German origin, best known in English as "Lo, How a Rose E'er Blooming" and also called "A Spotless Rose" and "Behold a Rose of Judah". It draws on the prophecies of Isaiah, which in Christian interpretation foretell the Incarnation of Jesus, and on the Tree of Jesse, the traditional image of the descent of Jesus from Jesse of Bethlehem, father of King David, much loved in medieval religious art. The prophecy had featured in Christian hymns since at least the 8th century, when Cosmas the Melodist wrote of the Virgin Mary flowering from the root of Jesse. Because of its prophetic theme the hymn is popular in Advent.

The text dates from the 15th century and its author is unknown. Its earliest source is a manuscript from the Carthusian monastery of St Alban in Trier, now in the Trier City Library, thought to have been in use in Martin Luther's time. It first appeared in print in the Speyer Hymnbook, printed in Cologne in 1599, which also holds the tune generally used; the familiar harmony was written by Michael Praetorius in 1609. A four-voice canon based on his harmony is sometimes attributed to his contemporary Melchior Vulpius. The hymn was first written with two verses. Catholics and Protestants have both sung it, the first with Mary at its centre, the second with Jesus, and it has been printed with many texts and lengths. In 1844 the hymnologist Friedrich Layriz added three stanzas, of which the first has stayed in Catholic and Protestant hymnals.

In the Nazi era many German carols were rewritten to promote Nazi ideology and to remove references to the Jewish origins of Jesus. During Christmas in Nazi Germany this one was turned into "Uns ist ein Licht erstanden" ("A light has arisen for us"), a secular text of sunlight falling on the Fatherland and praise of motherhood.

Composers have returned to the melody often: Johannes Brahms in an organ chorale prelude of 1896, one of his Eleven Chorale Preludes, Op. 122, later orchestrated by Erich Leinsdorf; Arnold Schoenberg in his Weihnachtsmusik of 1921, which sets it against "Stille Nacht"; Hugo Distler in his oratorio Die Weihnachtsgeschichte of 1933; and Jan Sandström, whose 1990 work for two a cappella choirs incorporates Praetorius's setting in the first choir. In English, Theodore Baker's "Lo, How a Rose E'er Blooming" (1894) is sung in American hymnals, and Harriet Reynolds Krauth Spaeth wrote "Behold, a rose is growing". Catherine Winkworth's translation of the first two verses, "A Spotless Rose", became the text of Herbert Howells's motet of 1919. Howells said he wrote it after watching railway trucks shunting on the line below a cottage window in Gloucester, and dedicated it to his mother; its final cadence is much admired, and his fellow composer Patrick Hadley reportedly told him he would like to die to it. Philip Ledger set the same translation in 2002. The tune is also sung with "A Great and Mighty Wonder", John Mason Neale's 1862 translation of a Greek hymn, and the two are sometimes confused.
== meaning
A carol of the coming of Christ. A rose springs from the stem of Jesse, as Isaiah foretold, and the hymn explains that the rose is Mary, the pure maiden, who brings forth the Christ child as a small flower; in Theodore Baker's English the rose stands for the infant Christ himself. The mood is quiet and tender.
