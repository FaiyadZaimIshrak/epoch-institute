== story
"Der gute Kamerad" ("The Good Comrade"), better known by its opening line as "Ich hatt' einen Kameraden" ("I had a comrade"), is a German soldiers' lament. The German romantic poet Ludwig Uhland wrote the words in 1809; their immediate inspiration was the sending of troops from Baden against the Tyrolean Rebellion. In 1825 the composer of Lieder Friedrich Silcher set them to music, on the tune of a Swiss folk song, in honour of those who had fallen in the more recent Wars of Liberation against Napoleon's French Imperial Army.

The words describe the loss of a friend in combat without any political or national cause attached, and twice move from the past tense into the present, as a memory returns. Because of this the song was never tied to one country or one ideology. Through the 19th, 20th and 21st centuries it was sung across the political spectrum, right and left, and it was translated for armed forces in other languages, among them French, Dutch, Spanish and Japanese. The French Foreign Legion adopted it as early as the 19th century, and the Chilean Armed Forces and the National Army of Colombia also use it. In 1880 the scholar Heymann Steinthal described a variant he had heard a housemaid sing, which he thought improved on Uhland's text by evoking the military idea of a fated bullet.

German emigrants took it with them. Around St. Cloud, Minnesota, settled in the 1850s by Catholic immigrants invited by the missionary Fr. Francis Xavier Pierz, the local historian Fr. Colman J. Barry recorded that German-American veterans of the Union Army in the American Civil War would stand and sing it, with tears, in honour of fallen friends at parish feast-day picnics and old-country festivals. Ernst Busch used its tune for his Spanish Civil War song on the death of Hans Beimler, and in 1966 the playwright Carl Zuckmayer took one of its lines as the title of his autobiography, published in English as A Part of Myself.

The song still has a ceremonial place in the German and Austrian armed forces and is part of every military funeral, a tradition dating from at least around 1871. It is played at the funerals of Austrian firefighters, of volunteer firefighters in South Tyrol and at remembrance ceremonies of the Schützenbund there, at the funerals of members of student fraternities (Studentenverbindungen), and sometimes at civilian funerals, mostly of people who had served. On 22 May 2009 a Flemish band played it on Highland bagpipes and drums at a joint Belgian, British and German memorial at the German war cemetery of Langemark in Belgium, where more than 44,000 German and two British soldiers of the First World War lie. It is played on the trumpet at the wreath-laying at the Neue Wache in Berlin on Volkstrauertag, and every 20 July at the Memorial to the German Resistance in the Bendlerblock, in honour of the officers who plotted to kill Hitler, whom the Bundeswehr has honoured since its founding in the 1950s.
== meaning
A soldier remembers a friend lost in combat, with the grief and the guilt of the one who survived. The mood is quiet and mournful rather than warlike, and the shifts into the present tense make the loss feel as if it were happening again.
