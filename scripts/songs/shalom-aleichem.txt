== story
Shalom Aleichem, "Peace be upon you", is a song that many Jews sing every Friday night on coming home from the synagogue. It marks the arrival of the Shabbat by welcoming the angels who are said to accompany a person home on the Sabbath eve, and among religious Jews it is commonly sung before Eshet Ḥayil and the Kiddush. Many sing each stanza three times.

The liturgical poem was written by the kabbalists of Safed in the late 16th or early 17th century; Chaim Leiberman compiled a survey of all its surviving manuscripts, and its earliest known printing is in Tikunei Shabbos. It is thought to rest on a teaching of the Talmud: two angels, one good and one evil, walk home with a person from the synagogue on Friday night. If the house is ready for the Shabbat, with the lamp lit and the table set, the good angel blesses it that the next Shabbat be the same and the evil angel must answer "Amen"; if it is not ready, the evil angel wishes the same and the good angel must answer "Amen".

The words have been argued over. Elijah of Vilna (1720–1789) warned singers not to pause in the middle of one phrase, since a pause changed its sense. Rabbi Jacob Emden, in his prayer book Bet El (1745), objected to the hymn altogether, holding that petitions on the Sabbath and petitions to angels were improper, and he also faulted its grammar and struck out a prefix; a few later prayer books followed him, among them Seligman Baer's Siddur Avodat Yisroel (1868), although the change makes the metre awkward. Jacob Zallel Lauterbach (1873–1942) judged some of the words not original, and Moshe Yair Weinstock and others criticised the last verse for seeming to hurry the angels on their way. Mizrahi and some Sephardi traditions add a verse before the last; Yaakov Chaim Sofer, in his Kaf Hachayim, explained the Sephardi custom of repeating the request for a blessing between the added verses, so that the angels are asked to bless their coming, their rest and their leaving, which answers the complaint that the singer seems to send them away.

There are many tunes. The slow one most people know was written by the American rabbi, composer and conductor Israel Goldfarb on 10 May 1918, as he sat by the Alma Mater statue in front of Low Memorial Library at Columbia University, and published that year in Friday Evening Melodies by Israel and his brother Samuel. It is often taken for a traditional Hasidic melody; in 1963 Goldfarb wrote that many people believed it had come down from Mount Sinai with Moses. A traditional Moroccan Jewish melody is the same as the song El Rey Nimrod. Later versions include a joyful one popularised by Idan Yaniv and Kinderlach in September 2009, a faster tune by Rabbi Shmuel Brazil, and the version Debbie Friedman shared with Rabbi Joy Levitt as one of her last acts, which she believed would be her legacy.
== meaning
A greeting of peace to the angels who, in Jewish teaching, come into the home as the Sabbath begins. The singer welcomes them, asks them to bless the household with peace, and bids them farewell. Its mood is warm and festive, a domestic welcome of the holy day.
