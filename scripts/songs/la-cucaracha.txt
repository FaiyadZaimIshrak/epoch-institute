== story
"La Cucaracha" ("The Cockroach") is a folk song whose refrain tells of a cockroach that has lost two of its legs and can no longer walk properly. Its origins are obscure and Spanish. Because the refrain names no event, it cannot be dated, but its verses were made up anew for each time and place and so can be roughly dated by the events they mention. One of the earliest written references to the song is in the Mexican writer José Joaquín Fernández de Lizardi's novel La Quijotita y su Prima (1819). In his Cantos Populares Españoles (1883), Francisco Rodríguez Marín recorded verses about the Hispano-Moroccan War of 1859–1860, probably made up by Spanish troops on campaign to keep up their spirits; other early verses refer to the Carlist Wars in Spain and to the French intervention in Mexico in 1861.

The song's great period came in the Mexican Revolution, from 1910 to about 1920, when both rebel and government forces invented political verses for it, and most of the verses known today were written. They referred to battles, leaders and the hardship the war brought to civilians, often with hidden meanings. The best-known verses today are two of Pancho Villa's soldiers, aimed at President Victoriano Huerta: in them the cockroach stands for Huerta, who was known as a heavy drinker and was held a traitor for his part in the death of the revolutionary president Francisco Madero. Huerta's own followers sang verses against Venustiano Carranza, Emiliano Zapata's followers had theirs, and civilians, many of them sympathetic to Zapata, sang verses complaining of the war's hardships or naming the factions without taking sides.

"Cucaracha" was also one of the nicknames, with soldaderas, Adelitas and Juanas, for the women who fought in or followed Mexican armies, a role traced back to before the Spanish conquest. Soldiers of Porfirio Díaz's army sang of a soldadera who wanted money for the bullfights; for Villa's men, as the historian Elizabeth Salas writes, the cockroach wanted money for alcohol and marijuana. Salas notes that, unlike the corridos about Villa or Zapata, none of the well-known songs about soldaderas give real names, so few verses tell truly of women in battle or in the camps.

Hundreds of other verses exist, old and new, passed on mostly by ear. The modern song has been adapted to the Mexican corrido, and its melody is known worldwide.
== meaning
A light, mocking dance song: the refrain laughs at a cockroach that can no longer walk, and the verses, made up anew in each era, turn that joke on rival armies, politicians, the hardships of war or daily life. In the Mexican Revolution the cockroach became a figure of ridicule for an enemy leader.
