== story
The tune of "Yankee Doodle" is thought to be much older than its words and was known across western Europe, in England, France, the Netherlands, Hungary and Spain. Its second strain is identical to the Irish tune "All the way to Galway", from which it may have come. There are rumours, unproven, that its earliest words came from a Dutch harvest song of nonsense words in Dutch and English, perhaps as old as the 15th century. The word "doodle" appeared in English in the early 17th century and is thought to come from Low German words for playing music badly or for a fool. In the 1770s a "macaroni" was a man who dressed and spoke in an extravagantly fashionable way, a figure much mocked in the London press.

The song is said to have been first sung by British officers to mock the colonial "Yankees" who served beside them in the French and Indian War. It was written at Fort Crailo, in Rensselaer, New York, around 1755 or 1758, by the British Army surgeon Richard Shuckburgh; one account says he wrote it after seeing the colonial troops of Colonel Thomas Fitch, son of the governor of Connecticut. The British sang it to cast the colonists as simpletons who thought a feather in the hat made them fashionable. Its sheet music asked for the words to be sung through the nose in a West Country drawl. The tune also appeared in 1762 in The Disappointment, a comic ballad opera from Pennsylvania.

In the Revolution the song changed sides. One verse is tied to Thomas Ditson of Billerica, Massachusetts, whom soldiers of the 47th Regiment of Foot tarred and feathered in March 1775 for trying to buy a musket in Boston; Ditson later fought at Lexington and Concord, and Billerica calls itself the home of "Yankee Doodle". After Lexington and Concord a Boston newspaper reported that the pursuing Minutemen had made the British "dance it till we were tired". Pro-British words were set to it after the Battle of Bunker Hill in June 1775, while American troops added verses mocking the British and praising George Washington. The Online Etymology Dictionary dates the current version to 1775 or 1776 and a 15-verse ballad by Edward Bangs, a Harvard student and Minuteman. The song was played at the British surrender at Saratoga in 1777, and by 1781 it had become a song of American national pride.

The song passed into the nursery: a variant was printed in the 1810 edition of Gammer Gurton's Garland, collected by Francis Douce. Before 3 March 1931, when "The Star-Spangled Banner" became the official national anthem, it served with "Hail, Columbia" and "My Country, 'Tis of Thee" as one of the country's unofficial anthems. It is the state song of Connecticut, and a bill introduced in the House of Representatives on 25 July 1999 recognised Billerica as "America's Yankee Doodle Town". The Voice of America uses its tune as an interval signal; in his 1990 memoir Being Red, Howard Fast claimed that he chose it, "as a joke", in 1943.
== meaning
A light, mocking song about a country fellow who goes to town and thinks a feather in his cap makes him a gentleman. In its first use it laughed at the colonists' lack of polish; the same mockery, taken up by the colonists themselves, became a cheerful boast of plain American pride.
