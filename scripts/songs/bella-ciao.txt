== story
"Bella ciao" is an Italian folk song dedicated to the partisans of the Italian Resistance, who fought the occupying troops of Nazi Germany and the Italian fascist forces that collaborated with them. Its origins are not known. One hypothesis is that it grew from a song of the mondine, the women who weeded the flooded rice fields of the Po Valley each year from late April to early June, from the late 19th century to the first half of the 20th. They came from the poorest classes and worked barefoot in water to their knees with their backs bent, for long hours and low pay; their discontent led to protests and riots in the early 20th century. Their song, with different words from the partisan one, is known in variants from the late 19th century, and the earliest written version, from near Vercelli in Piedmont, is dated 1906.

How far the song belongs to the war itself is disputed. There is no documentary trace of the partisan version before its text was published in the magazine La Lapa in 1953, followed by L'Unità in 1957; it is missing from the postwar documents and from major songbooks such as Pasolini's Canzoniere italiano of 1955 and the Canti politici of 1962. The partisans' popular songs, as Roberto Battaglia records, were "Fischia il vento" and the Soviet song "Katyusha". The journalist and former partisan Giorgio Bocca said he never heard "Bella ciao" sung in twenty months of partisan war, and historians of Italian music including Antonio Virgilio Savona and Michele Straniero agree that it was sung rarely if at all; Cesare Bermani and Ruggero Giacomini hold that some brigades sang some version of it.

The song became famous in the 1960s. Giovanna Daffini recorded it in 1962, Yves Montand's recording made it widely known, and in 1964 the group Il Nuovo Canzoniere Italiano presented it at the Festival dei Due Mondi in Spoleto both as a rice-weeders' song and as a partisan hymn. Because it named no party, the partisan song could unite the Catholic, Communist, Socialist and Liberal strands of the liberation struggle; it was even sung at the close of the 1975 Christian Democracy congress that elected the former partisan Benigno Zaccagnini as national secretary. A possible source of the melody is a Yiddish tune, "Koilen", recorded in New York in 1919 by the klezmer accordionist Mishka Ziganoff.

Since then it has been sung around the world, in dozens of languages, as a song of resistance to fascism, injustice and oppression. Chile's Quilapayún recorded it in 1969, Kurdish singers in 1989 and 1991, and in the 21st century it has been sung at protests from Myanmar after the 2021 coup to Colombia, India, Serbia and Germany. The Spanish television series Money Heist, from 2017, brought it new fame, and in March 2020 Italians under the COVID-19 lockdown sang it from their balconies. It has also been taken up by football crowds and by parties of both left and right, and in 2023 Italian trade unionists sang it in protest against Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni.
== meaning
In its partisan form it is a song of farewell and resistance, sad but defiant. An older rice-field song with other words protested the hard labour of the paddies.
