== story
"Strange Fruit" began as a poem by Abel Meeropol, a New York teacher, written in horror at the lynching of African Americans. Lynchings had reached a peak in the Southern United States around the turn of the 20th century, and most of the victims were African American. Meeropol was moved by Lawrence Beitler's photograph of the 1930 lynching of Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith in Marion, Indiana. He published the poem as "Bitter Fruit" in January 1937 in The New York Teacher, the magazine of the New York teachers' union, under the pseudonym Lewis Allan. He usually asked others, such as Earl Robinson, to set his poems, but he set this one to music himself. His wife Anne Shaffer and their friends first sang it at social gatherings, and Meeropol, Shaffer and the Black singer Laura Duncan performed it at Madison Square Garden.

Accounts differ on how it reached Billie Holiday: either Barney Josephson, founder of Café Society in Greenwich Village, New York's first integrated nightclub, or Robert Gordon, who directed her show there, brought it to her. She first sang it at Café Society in 1939. Holiday said that singing it made her fear retaliation, but she kept it because it reminded her of her father, Clarence Halliday, and made it a regular part of her performances. Josephson set rules for it: it closed her set, waiters stopped serving beforehand, the room went dark but for a spotlight on her face, and there was no encore.

Her label, Columbia, would not record it, fearing the reaction of record dealers in the South and of stations of its radio network, CBS, and her producer John Hammond also refused. Holiday sang it a cappella for her friend Milt Gabler, owner of the Commodore label, who was moved to tears; Columbia released her for one session, and she recorded it on April 20, 1939, with Frankie Newton's Café Society Band, the pianist Sonny White improvising a long introduction. She recorded it again for Commodore in 1944. The 1939 record sold a million copies over time and became the best-selling recording of her career. Her 1956 autobiography, Lady Sings the Blues, ghostwritten by William Dufty, suggested that she had helped set the poem to music; David Margolick and Hilton Als dismissed the claim.

In October 1939 Samuel Grafton of the New York Post wrote that if the anger of the exploited in the South ever rose high enough, it now had its Marseillaise. The anti-lynching movement took the song as its anthem. Bills to make lynching a federal crime were repeatedly blocked from the 1930s by filibusters of Southern senators, and activists were urged to mail copies of the song to their senators to build a two-thirds majority. Ahmet Ertegun of Atlantic Records later called it "a declaration of war" and "the beginning of the civil rights movement".

Few artists attempted it in the 1950s and 1960s besides Josh White and Nina Simone, whose 1965 recording on Pastel Blues is among the best known; Georgia Lee's version, possibly the first in Australia, was banned from radio. Later singers include Siouxsie and the Banshees (1987), Jeff Buckley, UB40, Cassandra Wilson and many others. Holiday's recording entered the Grammy Hall of Fame in 1978 and the National Recording Registry in 2002, and Time magazine named it the best song of the century in December 1999.
== meaning
A protest against the lynching of Black Americans in the South. Its mood is quiet, bitter and horrified.
