== story
The Song of Songs, also called the Canticle of Canticles or the Song of Solomon, is a biblical poem, one of the five megillot ("scrolls") in the Ketuvim, the last section of the Tanakh. Unlike the other books of the Hebrew Bible it is erotic poetry: a woman and her lover speak in turn, express their desire, praise each other and invite each other to enjoy, through dialogues, dreams and metaphors, while the women of Jerusalem form a chorus. The title follows an idiom of Scriptural Hebrew for the greatest of its kind, as in "Holy of Holies"; "Song of Solomon" can mean a song of, by, for or dedicated to Solomon. Modern scholarship tends to hold that the lovers are unmarried, and most scholars read the book as a celebration of human love rather than a divine metaphor.

The poem names no author, date or place of origin, and its superscription calling it "Solomon's" cannot be read as strictly as a modern statement of authorship. Since the 1800s most scholars have dated it to the 3rd century BCE. The strongest evidence is its language: Aramaic gradually replaced Hebrew after the end of the Babylonian exile in the late 6th century BCE, and its vocabulary, forms and syntax point to a date centuries after Solomon. It has parallels with Mesopotamian and Egyptian love poetry of the first half of the 1st millennium BCE and with the pastoral idylls of the Greek poet Theocritus, so estimates range from the 10th to the 2nd century BCE. Whether it is one poem or an anthology is still debated. It seems to be rooted in festive performance; connections have been proposed with the "sacred marriage" of Ishtar and Tammuz, and Marvin H. Pope described it as a liturgy of the fertility cults of Mesopotamia and Canaan. Other scholars find in it features of wisdom literature.

The Song was accepted into the Jewish canon read as an allegory of the love between God and Israel, a view dominant for more than a thousand years, and its place in scripture was tied to that reading and to its attribution to Solomon. Rabbi Akiva, in the 1st and 2nd centuries, forbade its use in popular celebrations, saying that whoever sang it in wine taverns as a vulgar song lost his share in the world to come, yet he defended it as the "Holy of Holies" of the Writings. Jewish mysticism after the Zohar of the 13th century gave it a central place, and "Lecha Dodi", a 16th-century liturgical song, takes many passages from it, among them its opening two words. The whole Song is read in Hebrew in synagogues during the intermediate days of Passover, often from a scroll like a Torah scroll; the custom of reading it at Passover was first recorded in 8th-century Jund Filastin. Some read it at the end of the Passover Seder or before the Sabbath.

Christian interpretation as a picture of God's love for his people began with Origen. Gregory of Nyssa wrote fifteen Homilies on the Song, comparing the bride to the soul and the groom to God, and Augustine, following Ambrose of Milan, read it as the wedding of Christ and the Church. Theodore of Mopsuestia read it only literally, as an erotic poem by Solomon to the daughter of Pharaoh; his view was condemned at the Second Council of Constantinople, but from the 18th century a literal reading became the dominant one among biblical scholars. In the 12th century the bride was also read as the Virgin Mary. Outside these traditions, Ibn Hazm (994–1064) dismissed the book, and in 1869 Syed Ahmad Khan argued that a word in its fifth chapter refers to Muhammad; in modern times feminist critics, beginning with Phyllis Trible, have made it an exemplary text.

Composers have set its words for centuries: Palestrina published 29 five-part motets on it in 1584, and there are settings by Francesca Caccini (1618), Heinrich Schütz (1629), Dieterich Buxtehude (1680) and J. S. Bach, Ralph Vaughan Williams's Flos Campi (1925), each movement headed by one of its verses, and many later works. Marc Chagall painted a five-picture cycle on it between 1957 and 1966, now in the Marc Chagall Museum in Nice; Lillian Hellman took the title of The Little Foxes (1939) from it, John Steinbeck named a character of The Grapes of Wrath (1939) Rose of Sharon after one of its epithets, and Toni Morrison called her 1977 novel Song of Solomon.
== meaning
A cycle of love poems in which a woman and a man call to each other, praise each other's beauty in images drawn from gardens, vineyards, flocks and the places of the land, seek each other through the city by night, and warn the women of Jerusalem not to wake love before its time. It closes by setting the strength of love against death itself. The mood is passionate, playful and full of longing; Jewish and Christian tradition read it as an allegory of the love between God and his people.
