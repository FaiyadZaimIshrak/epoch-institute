== story
The Dies irae, "the Day of Wrath", is a Latin sequence, a hymn of the Mass. It is attributed either to the Franciscan Thomas of Celano (1200–1265) or to Latino Malabranca Orsini (d. 1294), lector at the Dominican house of studies at Santa Sabina in Rome; some sources take it back to Gregory the Great, Bernard of Clairvaux or Bonaventure. It dates from the 13th century at the latest. Its earliest known text is in a Franciscan calendar missal now in the national library at Naples, which must date from 1253–1255 because it lacks the name of Clare of Assisi, canonised in 1255. It is a rhymed poem in accented trochaic metre. Its images come above all from the Vulgate text of the prophet Zephaniah, with others from Revelation, Matthew, Thessalonians, Peter and Luke; the Jewish prayer Unetanneh Tokef appears to be related.

For centuries the Dies irae was the sequence of the Requiem Mass in the Roman Rite and part of the liturgy of All Souls' Day. Its last two stanzas differ in form from the rest, and some scholars think they were added to fit the poem for liturgical use. In the reforms that followed the Second Vatican Council, the Consilium charged with them (1969–70) removed the sequence from funerals and other Masses for the Dead; Archbishop Annibale Bugnini explained that texts dwelling on judgment, fear and despair were replaced with ones expressing Christian hope. It is still sung where the Tridentine liturgy of the 1962 Missal is used, and, slightly edited and divided into three parts, remains an optional hymn of the Liturgy of the Hours on All Souls' Day and in the last week before Advent. In the edit a line naming Mary was changed, because modern scholarship no longer identifies Mary Magdalene with the woman taken in adultery.

The 13th-century Gregorian melody is a sombre chant in the Dorian mode and one of the most quoted tunes in music. The earliest surviving polyphonic Requiem, by Johannes Ockeghem, does not include it; the first settings to include it are by Engarandus Juvenis (1490) and Antoine Brumel (1516), and later ones by Charpentier, Delalande, Mozart, Berlioz, Verdi, Britten and Stravinsky. Berlioz's use of the chant in the Symphonie fantastique made it widely known and tied it, especially in the 19th century, to death and terror. Liszt, Saint-Saëns, Mahler, Mussorgsky, Tchaikovsky and Rachmaninoff, in more than a dozen works, quoted it, and it has been used in many film scores and popular works, from the score for Metropolis (1927) and Citizen Kane (1941) to The Shining (1980) and The Lion King.

The poem itself travelled through translation and literature. William Josiah Irons made an English version in 1849 that keeps the rhyme and metre; edited, it is approved for funerals in the Catholic ordinariates for former Anglicans. The Irish priest Bernard Callan put it into Irish around 1800, John Newton made a loose Protestant version, and Ambrose Bierce a satirical one in 1903. Walter Scott used two stanzas in The Lay of the Last Minstrel (1805), Goethe three in the cathedral scene of Faust (1808), and Oscar Wilde wrote a sonnet on hearing it sung in the Sistine Chapel (1881).
== meaning
A meditation on the Last Judgment. It pictures the end of the world, a trumpet calling the dead before the throne of God and a book from which all are judged, and the singer, afraid of being among the condemned, pleads for mercy. Its closing stanzas turn to a prayer for rest for the dead.
