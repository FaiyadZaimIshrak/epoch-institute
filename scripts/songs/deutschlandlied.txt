== story
The melody is Joseph Haydn's, written in 1797 for Lorenz Leopold Haschka's "Gott erhalte Franz den Kaiser", a birthday anthem for Emperor Francis II modelled on Britain's "God Save the King". It remained the anthem of the Austrian emperors, with new words from 1854, until the end of Austria-Hungary in 1918.

In 1841, while on holiday on the North Sea island of Heligoland, then British, the poet and linguist August Heinrich Hoffmann von Fallersleben wrote "Das Lied der Deutschen" as a new text for Haydn's tune. Germany was then divided among the many states of the German Confederation, and after the Congress of Vienna and the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 Metternich's police censored universities and pursued liberals. The poem's first stanza was an appeal to the German princes to put a united Germany before the independence of their small states, and its third stanza asked for a united and free Germany under the rule of law rather than arbitrary monarchy; at the time this was a revolutionary idea. The next year Hoffmann lost his post as librarian and professor in Breslau because of this and other works, and lived in hiding until he was pardoned after the revolutions of 1848.

The song was first sung at an official ceremony in 1890, on Heligoland, when the Heligoland–Zanzibar Treaty made the island German, and it became one of the best-known patriotic songs of the German Empire. It became very popular after the Battle of Langemarck in 1914, when German regiments made up mostly of young students reportedly attacked the British lines singing it and suffered heavy losses. By then critics abroad read its first line as a claim of German supremacy over other peoples, a meaning its author had not intended.

On 11 August 1922 President Friedrich Ebert, a Social Democrat, made the whole song the anthem of the Weimar Republic, as a political trade-off for the conservative right after the black, red and gold flag had been adopted in 1919, though he favoured singing the third stanza only. Under the Nazi regime (1933–1945) only the first stanza was sung, followed by the party's "Horst-Wessel-Lied"; three thousand Germans sang it as Hitler entered the stadium at the opening of the 1936 Berlin Olympics. That stanza became closely identified with the Nazi regime.

West Germany had no anthem at first. In 1952, at Chancellor Konrad Adenauer's request, President Theodor Heuss accepted the song as the national anthem, with only the third stanza sung on official occasions; the other stanzas were not outlawed. East Germany had its own anthem, "Auferstanden aus Ruinen". In 1990 the Federal Constitutional Court ruled that only the third stanza is protected by criminal law as the anthem, and in November 1991 President Richard von Weizsäcker and Chancellor Helmut Kohl agreed in an exchange of letters that the third stanza alone is the anthem of reunified Germany. The opening words of that stanza are an unofficial national motto, found on Bundeswehr belt buckles and German 2 euro coins.

The first stanza is not illegal, but singing it is taboo in Germany, and its list of rivers and a strait as borders, none of which lies in Germany today, has been criticised as irredentist. Friedrich Nietzsche called its claim the most idiotic slogan in the world, and Kurt Tucholsky named a satirical photo book of 1929 after it. It has more than once been played or sung by mistake abroad: German athletes were appalled when it was played at the canoe sprint world championships in Hungary in 2011, and at a Fed Cup match in Hawaii in 2017 German players and fans sang the third stanza to drown out the soloist.
== meaning
A patriotic poem in three stanzas: the first places Germany above loyalty to its many separate states, the second is a toast in the manner of a drinking song, and the third, the anthem of today, asks for a united and free Germany under the rule of law. The mood is earnest and stately, carried by Haydn's hymn tune.
