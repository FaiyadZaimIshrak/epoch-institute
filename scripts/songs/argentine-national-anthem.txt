== story
After the May Revolution of 1810, poets and musicians in Buenos Aires wrote a number of patriotic songs. The earliest, a "Patriotic March" published anonymously on 15 November 1810 in the Gazeta de Buenos Ayres, is generally believed to have words by Esteban de Luca and music by Blas Parera; it did not mention Argentina or any independent state. In mid-1812 the ruling triumvirate had the Buenos Aires Cabildo commission a national song: a text by the Franciscan friar Cayetano Rodríguez was approved on 4 August, and Parera, the music director of the local theatre, set it and conducted its premiere on 1 November.

Within a year the Assembly of Year XIII judged that song not effective enough, and the army's recent victories had made its words out of date. On 6 March 1813 several poets were invited to submit texts, and the poem by the lawyer Vicente López y Planes was chosen unanimously. On 11 May 1813 it was designated the sole national march, and it was published as a broadside three days later. Parera wrote new music within a few days. An unconfirmed tradition places the first performance on 14 May 1813 in the home of Mariquita Sánchez de Thompson; Parera conducted the official premiere in the theatre on 28 May and was paid 200 pesos. He may have shown his dislike of the new nation by quoting, in a minor key and with dissonant chords, a gesture from an anti-Napoleonic war song.

The text was fiercely pro-independence and anti-Spanish, and its outlook reached beyond Argentina to the struggles in Mexico, Central America, northern South America and Charcas, while its allegory cast not only Spanish absolutism but Spain itself as the enemy. A disputed word in one line, changed in a corrected song-sheet bearing the same date, reverses whether Buenos Aires sets itself against the other provinces or at their head; the first reading has been seen as a sign of the city's centralist views and also as a misprint. The song spread fast: within ten years it was performed throughout Argentina and also in Chile, Peru and Colombia, where it was used until those countries had anthems of their own. It was known as the "Canción Patriótica Nacional" and then the "Canción Patriótica"; the title "Himno Nacional Argentino" first appears on Juan Pedro Esnaola's arrangement of about 1848.

So many versions grew up that singing together became difficult. In 1860 Esnaola was commissioned to prepare an official version, with a slower tempo, a fuller texture and richer harmony. A committee's revision of 1927 that undid some of his changes was rejected after a public debate led by the newspaper La Prensa, and Esnaola's edition was confirmed as the official anthem in 1944; Decree 10302/1944 still governs it. Once hostility to Spain had faded and many Spanish immigrants had settled in the country, President Julio Argentino Roca decreed on 30 March 1900 that at official and public festivals and in schools only the first and last verses and the chorus should be sung, so no anti-Spanish text is sung today.

The anthem is performed at all official events, where those present stand and sing; radio stations play it at midnight, and on national holidays it must be played then. At international sporting events usually only the instrumental introduction, or a shortened form, is played. The rock musician Charly García caused controversy in 1990 with an idiosyncratic version on his album Filosofía barata y zapatos de goma, which broke the regulations on reproducing the anthem. Argentina celebrates National Anthem Day on 11 May.
== meaning
A patriotic hymn that celebrates the rise of an independent nation and calls its people to hold on to their freedom. The full 1813 text is also a fierce song of the war against Spain, with victories across the continent; the parts sung today keep only the solemn, proud opening and close.
