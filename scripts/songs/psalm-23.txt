== story
Psalm 23 is the 23rd psalm of the Book of Psalms, part of the third section of the Hebrew Bible and a book of the Christian Old Testament; in the numbering of the Greek Septuagint and the Latin Vulgate it is Psalm 22, known in Latin as "Dominus regit me". Its heading ascribes it to King David, who in the Hebrew Scriptures was a shepherd in his youth. Some scholars doubt this and propose other possibilities, commonly dating the psalm to the post-exilic period. The image of a god or king as shepherd was common in ancient Israel and Mesopotamia: Hammurabi called himself a shepherd who brought well-being to his people in the conclusion to his law code.

In Judaism the psalm is traditionally sung at the third Shabbat meal and before the first and second, and in some communities during the Kiddush. It is also recited beside a person who has died, by those keeping watch over the body before burial, and at funerals. Christians connect its shepherd with Jesus, called the Good Shepherd in the Gospel of John. The Orthodox include it in the prayers before receiving the Eucharist; the Catholic Church assigns it to the Liturgy of the Hours and sings it as a responsorial at Masses for the dead; the Coptic Agpeya prays it at Terce; and the Church of England's Book of Common Prayer appoints it for the evening of the fourth day of the month.

The psalm reached many languages and versions. Jerome is associated with three Latin psalters, and in England the first Book of Common Prayer used Myles Coverdale's prose rendering. The Reformation spread vernacular Bibles, among them the Geneva Bible (1557) and the King James Bible (1611), whose wording became the best known English form of the psalm. Several of its phrases became popular sayings in English.

After the Reformation the psalm was recast as metrical verse to be sung to hymn tunes: The Bay Psalm Book (1640), the Sidney Psalms, and settings by George Herbert and Isaac Watts. The best known is "The Lord's My Shepherd", first published in the 1650 Scottish Psalter. It is widely attributed to the Parliamentarian Francis Rous, but a translating committee edited it heavily in the 1640s. It is usually sung to the tune Crimond, generally attributed to Jessie Seymour Irvine, and it remains one of the most popular hymns among English-speaking congregations. Henry Baker's "The King of Love My Shepherd Is" (1868) is another hymn drawn from the psalm.

In the 20th century Psalm 23 became closely tied to funerals in the English-speaking world, and films often show it recited at a graveside. Official liturgies were slower: the Book of Common Prayer's burial service uses Psalms 39 and 90, and the Episcopal Church in the United States first used Psalm 23 at funerals in its 1928 prayer book.
== meaning
A short prayer of trust in which the speaker pictures God as a shepherd who feeds, leads and protects his flock, and as a host who welcomes a guest. Its mood is calm confidence that God stays near even in danger and through the whole of life.
