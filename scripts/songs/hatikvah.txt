== story
Naftali Herz Imber, a Jewish poet from Złoczów (Zolochiv) in Austrian Galicia, wrote the first version of his poem in 1877 while staying with a Jewish scholar in Iași, and its text is dated 1878. The nine-stanza poem, "Tikvatenu" ("Our Hope"), set out his thoughts after the founding of the settlement of Petah Tikva and expressed the wish of Jews in the diaspora to return to the Land of Israel. Imber emigrated to Ottoman-ruled Palestine in 1882 and read the poem to the pioneers of the early Jewish villages, among them Rishon LeZion, Rehovot, Gedera and Yesud Hama'ala. It was published in Jerusalem in 1886 in his first book, Barkai.

In 1887 Shmuel Cohen, a resident of Rishon LeZion aged about 17 or 18, saw how the farmers responded to the poem and sang it to a melody he knew from Romania; he later recalled it as "Carul cu boi" ("The Ox Cart"). The tune belongs to a family that goes back to "La Mantovana", an Italian song of about 1600, which circulated across Renaissance Europe under many names and which Bedřich Smetana used in "Vltava" from Má vlast. Cohen's setting, in a minor key unusual for an anthem, carried the poem quickly through the Zionist communities of Palestine. It was taken up as an anthem by Hovevei Zion and then by the Zionist movement.

The Zionist Organization held anthem competitions in 1898 and 1900 and chose none of the entries, but Imber's poem was already popular: a session of the Fifth Zionist Congress in Basel in 1901 closed with it, and at the Sixth Congress in 1903 it was sung by delegates opposed to the proposal of a Jewish state in Uganda, who wanted a homeland in Palestine. The Eighteenth Zionist Congress in Prague formally adopted "Hatikvah" as the movement's anthem in 1933. The British Mandate government briefly banned its public performance and broadcast from 1919, in response to a rise in Arab anti-Zionist political activity. A former member of the Sonderkommando reported that Czech Jews sang it at the entrance to the gas chamber of Auschwitz-Birkenau in 1944, and were beaten by Waffen-SS guards as they sang.

When the State of Israel was established in 1948, "Hatikvah" was proclaimed its national anthem unofficially. It became the official anthem in November 2004, when the Knesset sanctioned an abbreviated and edited version in an amendment to the Flag and Coat-of-Arms Law, since renamed the Flag, Coat-of-Arms, and National Anthem Law. The official text keeps only the first stanza and an amended refrain of Imber's poem.

The anthem has also met objections. Some religious Jews criticised it for naming neither God nor the Torah; Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook wrote an alternative, "HaEmunah" ("The Faith"), while still endorsing the original. Some non-Jewish citizens of Israel, particularly Arab Israelis, object that its words speak only of Jewish aspirations, and several public figures, including the Druze minister Salah Tarif, the Muslim minister Raleb Majadale and Supreme Court justice Salim Joubran, have declined to sing it; proposals to change the text have been raised from time to time. Later performances and arrangements include Barbra Streisand's in 1978 and John Williams's adaptation for the film Munich in 2005.
== meaning
A poem of longing written for Jews living in exile: as long as the hope of return survives in Jewish hearts, the hope of becoming a free people in their own land is not lost. Its mood is hopeful and determined, though the tune it is sung to is in a minor key. The official anthem text is a single long sentence that sets out a condition and then its outcome.
