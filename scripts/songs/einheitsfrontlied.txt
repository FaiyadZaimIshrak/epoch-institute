== story
The "Einheitsfrontlied" (United Front Song) is one of the best-known songs of the German labour movement, written by Bertolt Brecht and set to music by Hanns Eisler. It grew out of the long rift in the German left: since the split of the workers' parties in 1917 the Social Democratic Party (SPD), which upheld the Weimar Republic, and the Communist Party (KPD), which rejected it, had been bitter opponents, and following the Comintern's line the communists denounced the social democrats as "social fascists". After Adolf Hitler came to power in January 1933 the Nazis banned both parties and the trade unions, and many, Brecht among them, came to believe that only a united front of social democrats and communists could still resist.

In December 1934, at the request of the theatre director Erwin Piscator, Brecht wrote the song for the First International Workers' Music Olympiad, held in Strasbourg in 1935, where a choir of 3,000 workers gave its first performance. The olympiad itself was marked by quarrels between communists and social democrats, despite the will to unity the song expressed. It was first printed and recorded in 1937, during the Spanish Civil War, in Madrid, as "Lied von der Einheitsfront", sung and published by the communist actor and singer Ernst Busch, and it appeared in Brecht's collection Svendborger Gedichte in 1939.

Eisler, who later composed the East German anthem "Auferstanden aus Ruinen", kept the setting deliberately simple so that workers without musical training could sing it; the result is close to a march. In 1935 he wrote to Marcel Rubin that it should be sung very simply, at a moderate pace and without false militant shouting. In 1948 he made a symphonic version, meant in part to distance the song from the march, a form fascism had tainted; Busch sang it for his Aurora project.

In the Vilna Ghetto in Lithuania the song's text was the basis of a Yiddish song: one account credits Shmerke Kaczerginski's "Partizaner-Marsh" of 1943, which refers to the Jewish resistance group FPO (Fareynikte Partizaner Organizatsye), another names a Yiddish version by Leyb Rozental. Later recordings include an instrumental "Song of the United Front" on Charlie Haden's Liberation Music Orchestra (1969), Ton Steine Scherben (1971), a free-jazz version by Brötzmann, Van Hove and Bennink (1973), Hannes Wader (1977) and several punk bands.
== meaning
A call to workers of every party to unite in a single front against fascism, grounded in their shared condition as workers; plain and march-like.
