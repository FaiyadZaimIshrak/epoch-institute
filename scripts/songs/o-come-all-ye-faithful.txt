== story
"O Come, All Ye Faithful", first known by its Latin title "Adeste Fideles", is a Christmas carol of uncertain origin. Its words have been credited at various times to St. Bonaventure in the 13th century, to King John IV of Portugal in the 17th, and most often to Cistercian monks of the German, Portuguese or Spanish provinces of the order. A parody of its chorus was already current before 1542, when it appears in chapter 41 of Rabelais's Gargantua.

Modern English hymnals usually credit the text to John Francis Wade (1711–1786), whose name appears on the earliest printed versions. Wade, an English Catholic layman, lived in exile in France, where he had fled after the Jacobite rising of 1745 was crushed, and made his living copying musical manuscripts he found in libraries. In 1751 he published a printed collection of his copies, Cantus Diversi pro Dominicis et Festis per annum, the first printed source of the hymn: four Latin verses, set in the square notation of medieval liturgical music and in triple time, unlike modern versions. A manuscript by Wade dating to 1751 is held by Stonyhurst College in Lancashire. Whether Wade wrote the hymn, copied it or reworked an older one is not known. The tune has likewise been credited to Wade, to John Reading and his son, to Handel, Gluck, Marcos Portugal, John IV and Thomas Arne, whom Wade knew.

Wade's liturgical books were often decorated with Jacobite imagery, and some English Catholic books of the time placed the hymn close to prayers for the exiled Stuart claimant. This has led to speculation, put forward most recently by Bennett Zon of Durham University, that the Latin hymn was a coded birth ode and rallying cry for Charles Edward Stuart. The theory rests on claims that are unproven, and the hymn draws almost all of its content from the Bible and the Nicene Creed; the Irish musicologist William H. Grattan Flood concluded that its words and music came from a Catholic source for Catholic worship.

The hymn was printed again in the 1760 edition of Evening Offices of the Church and in Samuel Webbe's An Essay on the Church Plain Chant (1782). Later in the 18th century the French priest Jean-François-Étienne Borderies added three Latin verses, and another anonymous Latin verse is rarely printed. In 1795 the Duke of Leeds heard it sung at the chapel of the Portuguese embassy in London, now Our Lady of the Assumption and St Gregory, Warwick Street, and it was sometimes called the "Portuguese Hymn". Friedrich Heinrich Ranke published a German translation in 1823.

The English Catholic priest Frederick Oakeley translated it into English in 1841; at his Margaret Chapel in Marylebone it was first sung under another title, before it took its present form. The version most sung today joins one of Oakeley's translations of the four original verses with William Thomas Brooke's translation of three of the added ones, and was first published in Murray's Hymnal in 1852. It became widespread in English-speaking countries, and it has been translated into many other languages. "Adeste Fideles" is traditionally the final anthem of Midnight Mass at St. Peter's Basilica in the Vatican.
== meaning
A Christmas hymn that calls the faithful to come joyfully to Bethlehem and worship the newborn Christ. Its verses restate the Church's teaching on the person of Jesus, drawn from the Bible and the Nicene Creed, and each ends in a refrain of adoration. The mood is festive and triumphant.
