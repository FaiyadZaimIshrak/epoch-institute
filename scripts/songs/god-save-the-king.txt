== story
The author of "God Save the King" is unknown. The words of the title are much older than the song: a text built on 1 Kings 1:38–40 has been sung at every coronation since King Edgar's in 973, and by 1545 the phrase was a watchword of the Royal Navy. The music writer Percy Scholes found likenesses to an early plainsong melody, to galliard dance tunes, to a keyboard piece by John Bull (1619) and to pieces by Henry Purcell, and recommended crediting the tune as "traditional". Claims for Henry Carey and James Oswald he judged untenable or doubtful.

The first published version close to the present song appeared in 1744 under the heading "For two voices" in the anthology Thesaurus Musicus. When Charles Edward Stuart led the Jacobite rising of 1745, the song spread among supporters of King George II; it was sung at the London playhouses of Drury Lane and Covent Garden, Thomas Arne wrote a setting for Drury Lane, and The Gentleman's Magazine printed it in October 1745. Tradition dates the first performance to after the government's defeat at Prestonpans. It is sometimes claimed that the words were first Jacobite, and drinking glasses of the early 18th century carry a version of them toasting James II and VII; Scholes thought both sides used the same words for their own kings. Verses were added and dropped for passing events: a prayer for Marshal Wade's army in 1745, a verse by Richard Sheridan after an attempt on George III's life in 1800, anti-French verses, and a republican version praising the guillotine by Joel Barlow in 1794.

The song became accepted as the British national anthem in the 1780s and 1790s. Its status rests on custom, not on any proclamation or act of Parliament. A more aggressive second verse was debated in the 19th century, and alternative versions were written by William Edward Hickson (1836), Samuel Reynolds Hole for Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee, and a "peace version" approved by the Privy Council in 1919; none replaced the standard words. An Army Order of 1933, following George V's preference, set its tempo and scoring. It was played at the BBC's close-down until 1997.

The song travelled with the British Empire. It was Australia's national anthem until 1984, when it became the royal anthem; it remains one of New Zealand's two national anthems and the royal anthem of Canada, where a joint committee found in 1967 that its words and music were in the public domain. Rhodesia kept it after declaring independence in 1965, until it became a republic in 1970. Outside the empire the melody served Prussia (1795–1918), the German Emperor (1871–1918), the Russian Empire (1816–1833) and Switzerland (until 1961), and it is still used for Liechtenstein's national anthem, Norway's royal anthem and the American song "My Country, 'Tis of Thee".

About 140 composers have used the tune, among them Beethoven, who wrote piano variations on it and quoted it in Wellington's Victory. Haydn heard it in London in 1794 and was moved to write an anthem for the Habsburg emperor. In 1951 it became the first music played on a computer and recorded, on the Manchester Mark II. Today usually only the first verse is sung; since 2003 the Commonwealth Games have not used it, and there are calls in the UK for a new anthem on secular, republican and English grounds.
== meaning
A prayer that God protect the reigning monarch and grant a long, victorious and happy reign. The often-sung second verse asks God to defeat the monarch's enemies, which is why it was debated and rewritten. The mood is solemn and loyal, close to a hymn.
