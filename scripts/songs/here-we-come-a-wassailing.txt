== story
"Here We Come A-wassailing", also known as "Here We Come A-Caroling", "Here We Come A-Christmasing", "Wassail Song" and by many other names, is a traditional English Christmas carol and New Year song. It was typically sung while wassailing: going from door to door singing carols, wishing good health and exchanging gifts. It is number 209 in the Roud Folk Song Index. The song dates from at least the mid 19th century but is probably much older; the "a-" of its title is an archaic intensifying prefix.

Reader's Digest describes the custom behind it: at Christmas the rich were often a little more generous than usual, and bands of beggars and orphans danced through the snowy streets of England, offering to sing good cheer and tell good fortune if the householder would give them a drink from his wassail bowl, a penny, a pork pie, or a few minutes beside the warmth of his hearth. The wassail bowl held a mixture of hot ale or beer, apples, spices and mead.

Hundreds of versions of wassailing songs have been collected, among them dozens of variants noted by Cecil Sharp from the 1900s to the 1920s, mostly in the south of England. Many are not of this type; "Gower Wassail" and "Gloucestershire Wassail" are similar songs. Versions close to the one now famous were recorded from Emily Bishop of Bromsberrow Heath, Gloucestershire, in 1952, Dorothy Davey of Hull in 1969, George Dunn of Quarry Bank, Staffordshire, in 1971 and Frank Hinchliffe of Sheffield in 1976. The song appears to have travelled to the United States with English settlers: it has been found several times in the Appalachian region and was recorded twice there, from Edith Fitzpatrick James of Ashland, Kentucky, in 1934 and from Jean Ritchie of Viper, Kentucky, in 1949.

The song has many variants. In 1949 the Welsh folk singer Phil Tanner sang a minor-key variant called "Wassail Song", generally known as "Gower Wassail", which folk revival groups made popular. "Here We Come A-Christmasing" replaces the word wassail with Christmas. Other variants, often though not always sung by Americans, begin and are titled "Here We Come A-caroling"; they often leave out a verse and change the second line of the chorus. Further variants are called "We've Been a While-A-Wandering" and "Yorkshire Wassail Song", and satirical ones were used in the 1987 television special A Claymation Christmas Celebration.

As with most carols there are several related versions of the words; a standard one is given in The New Oxford Book of Carols. The verses are sung in 6/8 time and the chorus changes to common time.
== meaning
The singers go from house to house, greet the master and mistress, ask for a share of food, drink and a little money, and wish the household good health and joy for the season. It is a cheerful song of asking and blessing.
