== story
Eugène Pottier, a member of the First International who had attended its congress of 1864 and a member of the Paris Commune, wrote the French words in June 1871, after the French army crushed the Commune on 28 May and before he fled to Britain and then, from 1873 to 1881, to the United States. He had written an earlier version in September 1870 to greet the Third Republic declared after the fall of the Second Empire, and meant the song to be sung to the tune of "La Marseillaise". The text that endures is the one Pottier authorised for his Chants révolutionnaires, published by his fellow Communard Jean Allemane in April 1887; Pottier died in November that year, and the song was reputedly sung to the Marseillaise at his burial.

The melody now sung was composed the following year by Pierre De Geyter, a woodcarver and member of the French Workers' Party in the industrial city of Lille, for the party choir La Lyre des Travailleurs. He had been commissioned by Gustave Delory, later mayor of Lille, who had the text from a young socialist teacher, Charles Gros, and it was first performed in July 1888. To protect De Geyter's job, the first leaflets printed only the French form of his surname; a later edition named his brother Adolphe as composer. Pierre lost a lawsuit over the authorship in 1914 and was recognised only in 1922, aged 74; Adolphe, who died by suicide in 1916, left a note saying that Delory had manipulated him into the claim. The song's success went with that of the Second International, which adopted it as its official anthem, and by the 1910 International Socialist Congress in Copenhagen it had been sung in 18 languages.

Translations carried it across the world and often changed it. Charles Hope Kerr published the first American version in 1894, after hearing the song in Lille; Emil Luckhardt wrote the best-known German text in 1896 for Wilhelm Liebknecht; Arkady Kots made the Russian version in 1902, which was reworded after the Bolshevik Revolution. The Soviet Union used the song as its national anthem until it was replaced by the "State Anthem of the USSR". In China Qu Qiubai retranslated it from the French in 1923, and from that year it has closed every National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party; accounts of the deaths of Li Dazhao in 1927 and of later Communist martyrs tell that they sang it, and it became a leitmotif of Chinese revolutionary cinema. Kazi Nazrul Islam translated it into Bengali around 1927, Ho Chi Minh into Vietnamese, Abolqasem Lahouti into Persian, Yeghishe Charents into Armenian; it was sung by workers in South Africa until the Communist Party was suppressed from 1950, and returned on South African radio in Zulu in 1990.

In the Second World War the song became a matter of diplomacy. After Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, Winston Churchill forbade the BBC to play it in its weekly programme of Allied national anthems; the BBC dropped the programme rather than go on leaving it out, and Churchill lifted the ban on 22 January 1942. In 1943 the BBC's Salute to the Red Army brought a mass performance to the Royal Albert Hall, and Arturo Toscanini insisted on including his orchestration of the song in the 1944 film Hymn of the Nations; during the McCarthy era it was cut from re-released copies until a 1988 Library of Congress release restored it.

The song has served both governments and those who opposed them: students sang it in their final retreat from Tiananmen Square in 1989. It is still sung by communist, socialist and labour movements, and new translations continue to appear, among them a Swahili version adopted by a Kenyan party in November 2024.
== meaning
A call to the poor and exploited workers of every country to rise, unite and take the world into their own hands. It rejects waiting for any saviour from above and holds that working people must free themselves. Its mood is defiant and confident of a coming victory.
