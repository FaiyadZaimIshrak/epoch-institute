== story
The tune came from a folk hymn of the revivalist camp meetings of the late 1700s, "Say, Brothers will you Meet Us", first published in the early 1800s; its first known printed form was "Canaan's Happy Shore". By the 1850s it carried the "Glory, glory, hallelujah" chorus, and the tune spread across the United States. In the first months of the American Civil War, soldiers of the 2nd Infantry Battalion of the Massachusetts militia, the "Tiger" Battalion, made up new words to it. According to George Kimball's account of 1890, they began by teasing a Scottish comrade named John Brown, who shared his name with the abolitionist executed after the raid on Harpers Ferry, and the jokes grew into verses about Brown's body lying in the grave while his soul marched on. "John Brown's Body" was publicly played, perhaps for the first time, at a flag-raising at Fort Warren near Boston on 12 May 1861, and the battalion soon prepared the words for publication with the publisher C. S. Hall.

Julia Ward Howe, an abolitionist writer, heard troops singing the song at a review outside Washington, D.C., on Munson's Hill near Bailey's Crossroads, Virginia. Her husband, Samuel Gridley Howe, was an anti-slavery leader and one of the Secret Six who had funded John Brown. Her companion at the review, the Reverend James Freeman Clarke, suggested that she write new words for the soldiers' song. Staying at the Willard Hotel, she woke before dawn on 19 November 1861 with the lines forming in her mind and wrote them down in the half-dark with an old pencil stub. She sold the poem to The Atlantic Monthly for $4, and it appeared on the front page of the February 1862 issue; a sixth verse in her manuscript was not printed. Where the soldiers' words were rough and comic, Howe's tied the Union cause to God's judgment, drawing on Isaiah 63 and the Book of Revelation. In 1863 the Supervisory Committee for Recruiting Colored Regiments in Philadelphia printed it as a broadside.

After the war it became one of the most used American patriotic songs. Marian Anderson sang it in 1953 to a television audience of 60 million. The Mormon Tabernacle Choir's recording reached number 13 on the Billboard Hot 100 and won a Grammy in 1960; the choir sang it on national television on 22 November 1963, the day John F. Kennedy was killed, and Judy Garland sang it on her show that December when CBS would not let her dedicate a programme to him. Andy Williams recorded it at Robert Kennedy's funeral in 1968, and it was sung at the burial of Lyndon B. Johnson in 1973 and at Jimmy Carter's funeral ceremony in 2025.

Martin Luther King Jr. used its words in his speeches, among them "How Long, Not Long" at the Alabama State Capitol on 25 March 1965, at the end of the Selma to Montgomery march, and his last sermon in Memphis on 3 April 1968, the night before he was assassinated. The tune also carried many other words: the Civil War "Marching Song of the First Arkansas" of Black soldiers, Mark Twain's satire on the Philippine–American War in 1901, the labour song "Solidarity Forever", college fight songs, children's parodies, and football chants that began at Tottenham Hotspur in 1961 and spread through British and Australian sport. It has also been turned to hateful ends, as in the racist "The White Man Marches On".
== meaning
A hymn of apocalyptic faith: the singer describes the coming of God's judgment as if seen among the camps and armies of the war, and calls on the listener to answer it. It joins the Union's fight to a religious duty to end slavery, with a solemn, marching tone that rises to the repeated "Glory, hallelujah" chorus.
