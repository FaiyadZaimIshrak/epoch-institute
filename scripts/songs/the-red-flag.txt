== story
The Irishman Jim Connell wrote "The Red Flag" in 1889 in Nicholas Donovan's house: six stanzas, each followed by a chorus, praising the sacrifices and solidarity of the international labour movement. Connell wanted it sung to "The White Cockade", a pro-Jacobite song by Robert Burns, but in the 1890s the British socialist writer Adolphe Smith Headingley popularised it to "Lauriger Horatius", the tune better known as the German carol "O Tannenbaum". Connell disliked that tune, which he called church music and conservative by nature; it is the one usually sung today.

The song spread with the workers' movement. It was the first song in the first edition of the Industrial Workers of the World's Little Red Songbook in 1909, which printed five of its six stanzas. In 1913 the IWW songwriter Joe Hill, drawing on his time in the Magonista rebellion of 1911 in Lower California, argued in the Industrial Worker that the song should speak of the workers rather than "the people", whom he saw as middle class. In New Zealand the Runanga Band played it at the burial of Henry John Morris at Greymouth on 11 January 1920, as he had asked: he had left Wales in 1908 to work in New Zealand's government mines and was prominent in the local socialist and labour movement.

It became the anthem of the British Labour Party from its founding, and its annual conference closes with it, along with "Jerusalem"; it is also the anthem of the Irish Labour Party and of Northern Ireland's Social Democratic and Labour Party, and the New Zealand Labour Party used it until the late 1940s. It was first sung in the House of Commons on 1 August 1945, after Clement Attlee's Labour Party defeated Winston Churchill's Conservatives, and again on 27 May 1976, on the night of 28 March 1979 when a vote of no confidence brought down the Labour government, in February 2006 for the party's centenary and in September 2019 in protest at the prorogation of Parliament. In the 1950s London dockers sang it at the end of meetings with management while the managers in the gallery sang "God Save the Queen". Under Tony Blair the leadership was said to play it down; Jeremy Corbyn's supporters sang it when he was elected leader in 2015.

Translated versions are sung by the Japanese Communist Party and the Korean People's Army. The song has also been much reworked: parodied by Leon Rosselson, widely known from the 1960s, to satirise a perceived lack of socialist principles in the Labour Party, turned by football supporters into club chants, and recorded by Shakin Stevens and Robert Wyatt in 1982 and by Billy Bragg and Dick Gaughan in 1990, sung to Connell's own choice of tune.
== meaning
A song in honour of the red flag as the banner of the workers' movement and of the sacrifices made for it. It pledges loyalty to the cause through every hardship. Its mood is solemn and defiant.
