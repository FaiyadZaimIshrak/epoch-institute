== story
The melody of "Lillibullero" is attributed to Henry Purcell, though it is unclear whether he composed it or arranged it; it was first published in The Delightful Companion, John Playford's method book for the recorder, in 1686, and no original manuscript survives. In the same year Thomas, Lord Wharton, wrote words to it satirising James II's appointment of the Catholic Richard Talbot, 1st Earl of Tyrconnell, as Lord Deputy of Ireland. The song is a mocking dialogue between two Irishmen about Talbot's arrival and what it would mean for the Protestants, who feared a massacre like that of the Irish Rebellion of 1641, when "Lilliburlero" had been a watchword of the Irish Catholics. Its nonsense refrain has been read as garbled Irish, perhaps alluding to a prophecy of the astrologer William Lilly; the song mocks both Irish faith in prophecy and English trust in Providence.

At first it made little impression. When James II began moving Irish regiments to England in 1688, the words were printed as broadsides, and by October the song was immensely popular, sung in the streets and especially in English barracks to mock the arriving Irish soldiers. The Whigs all but adopted it as their anthem in the Glorious Revolution. When William of Orange invaded the next month it was everywhere, said to be sung even by James's own palace guards, and a second part, rougher still, followed as William advanced. Wharton boasted that he had "sung a deluded Prince out of three kingdoms". Parodies answered it, among them an anti-Dutch song in circulation by 17 November.

The tune long outlived the quarrel. John Gay used it in The Beggar's Opera in 1728; Laurence Sterne printed its score in Tristram Shandy, where Uncle Toby whistles it; it carried the English drinking song "Nottingham Ale", sung at the launch of the East Indiaman Nottingham in 1787, and a nursery rhyme. In Ulster it became the tune of "Protestant Boys", played by flute bands at Orange Order parades, which were a subject of controversy during the Troubles. In the Second World War the BBC used it for the programme Into Battle, and it became the unofficial march of the British Army's Commandos; the BBC World Service then used it for decades as its interval signal before the news, chosen by engineers who found it carried well through short-wave noise, until a new theme by David Lowe was introduced in 2008; a shortened version is still sometimes played before the hourly news. It is the regimental march of the Royal Electrical and Mechanical Engineers and of their Canadian and Australian counterparts.
== meaning
A satire in the voices of two Irish Catholics who welcome Talbot's appointment and expect their nation's power and lands to be restored. It mocks them, and by playing on Protestant fears turned a political crisis into a marching joke. Its mood is jeering and lively.
