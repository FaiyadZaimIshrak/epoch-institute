== story
"Men of Harlech", or "The March of the Men of Harlech" (in Welsh "Rhyfelgyrch Gwŷr Harlech"), is a song and military march traditionally said to tell of the siege of Harlech Castle from 1461 to 1468, when the castle was held for the Lancastrians against the Yorkists in the Wars of the Roses. Its garrison, commanded by the constable Dafydd ap Ieuan, son of the Baron of Hendwr, withstood the longest known siege in the history of the British Isles; "Through Seven Years" is another name for the song. It has also been linked with the earlier, shorter siege of about 1407 to 1409, between the forces of Owain Glyndŵr and the future Henry V of England. The song holds an important place in Welsh national culture.

The tune was first published, without words, in 1794 as "Gorhoffedd Gwŷr Harlech: March of the Men of Harlech" in the second edition of The Musical and Poetical Relicks of the Welsh Bards, though it is said to be a much older folk song. The earliest version found with words is a broadside printed around 1830. Welsh words first appeared with it in Y Canigydd, edited by the poet John Owen (Owain Alaw) and published in Wrexham in 1860, and a second edition, Gems of Welsh Melody, gave Welsh and English words in Ruthin in 1862. In the same year the collection Welsh Melodies printed it with Welsh words by the poet John Jones (Talhaiarn) and English words by Thomas Oliphant, President of the Madrigal Society, plainly tying it to Glyndŵr's siege. The Welsh words of John Ceiriog Hughes are found in Brinley Richards's The Songs of Wales (1873), and John Oxenford's English words were published the same year. There have been many more English versions since, and none is accepted as the standard one.

It is widely used as a regimental march, above all by British and Commonwealth regiments with historic ties to Wales. It is the slow march of the Welsh Guards and the quick march of the Royal Welsh and of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, and a march of several Canadian regiments. Haydn arranged it, Edward German used it in the final movement of his Welsh Rhapsody (1904), and William Thomas Best wrote an organ fantasia on it. Fans of Welsh football clubs and schools and colleges around the world sing their own words to it, and a comic parody, "Woad" or "National Anthem of the Ancient Britons", was written by William Hope-Jones some time before 1914.

The cinema made it known worldwide. It was first heard in films over the titles of How Green Was My Valley (1941), and it is best known from Zulu (1964), where it is sung with words written for the film, in counterpoint to the Zulu war chants and the pounding of their shields; the film's editor, John Jympson, cut the scene so that the song keeps its place on either side of the cuts. It is also heard in Zulu Dawn, about the Battle of Isandlwana, and in The Englishman Who Went up a Hill but Came down a Mountain (1995). The Royal Regiment of Wales' band recorded it in the church at Rorke's Drift, South Africa, on the 120th anniversary of the battle there. On 11 September 2001 Rick Rescorla, head of security for Morgan Stanley's offices in the World Trade Center, sang a Cornish adaptation of it through a bullhorn, among other songs, to keep up the spirits of the staff as he led their evacuation; after helping to save more than 2,700 of them he went back into the towers to bring out others, and was killed when they collapsed.
== meaning
A song of defiance in arms. In its many sets of words the men of Harlech, or of Wales, are called to stand and fight for their land against an invader, and to keep its freedom whatever the cost; the march tune gives the words their swing and pride. No one text is the song: the versions differ in their images, but they share the call to arms and the spirit of resistance.
