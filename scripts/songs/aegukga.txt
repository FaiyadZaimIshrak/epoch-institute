== story
"Aegukga" means "patriotic song". The word can name any song of love for one's country, and South Korea still has more than ten other songs called "aegukga", but in general use it means the national anthem of South Korea. Its words date back to the 1890s. Who wrote them is not settled: theories name Yun Ch'iho, Ahn Chang-ho or Min Young-hwan, but the National Institute of Korean History holds that the author remains unknown.

The words were first sung to the tune of the Scottish song "Auld Lang Syne", known in Korea as "The Song of Farewell". In that form it was sung before the founding of South Korea, including by dissidents while Korea was under Japanese rule. The composer Ahn Eak-tai (1906–1965) first met the words during the March 1st Movement of 1919. Regretting that they were sung to a Scottish folk tune, he decided that a Korean should compose their melody, and he did so in 1935. His version was adopted as the anthem of the Korean exile government during the Japanese occupation.

It is distinct from Korea's first official anthem. After the Gabo Reform of 1894 and the founding of the Independence association in 1896, about ten patriotic songs were written privately and their words printed in the Independence Newspaper. Emperor Gojong then wanted an anthem as a symbol of the state: the German musician Franz von Eckert, who had led the band of the German Navy and worked for many years in Japan, came to Korea in 1901 and completed the Korean Empire's national anthem, based on traditional Korean scales, on 1 July 1902; it was proclaimed on 15 August 1902. After the annexation of Korea by Japan in 1910, Japan's "Kimigayo" became the official anthem and the Korean one was banned. After liberation it was not taken up again, because Eckert had taken part in composing "Kimigayo".

Ahn Eak-tai's "Aegukga" was customarily called the national anthem, and it became the anthem of the Republic of Korea after the government was founded on 15 August 1948, by custom more than by a formal act of designation. It has four verses; on most public occasions, such as baseball and football matches, only the first verse and the chorus are sung, and every KBO baseball game begins with it. In 2018 the music director Park In-young made a new arrangement with added brass and woodwind, keeping close to Ahn's original. Performing the song is prohibited in North Korea, where it is treated as a grave political crime.

Because Ahn Eak-tai died in 1965, the copyright in the music was to last until at least 2036, and in December 2003 a copyright holders' group sued two South Korean professional football clubs for playing it. On 16 March 2005 the composer's widow, Lolita Ahn, and her family gave up all rights to the song to the South Korean government, and it has since been in the public domain.
== meaning
A patriotic song: the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture describes an aegukga as a song meant to awaken love of one's country. It is solemn and devoted rather than warlike.
