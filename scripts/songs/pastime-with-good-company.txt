== story
"Pastime with Good Company", also known as "The King's Ballad" ("The Kynges Balade"), is an English song written by King Henry VIII in the early 16th century. It is regarded as the most famous of the king's creative works, and it became a popular song in England and other European countries during the Renaissance. Like every man of noble birth in his age, Henry was expected to master fencing, hunting, dancing, writing poetry, singing, and playing and composing music, and he was educated in all of these as a prince; his contemporaries thought him a talented composer and poet.

The oldest known version is in the Henry VIII Manuscript of about 1513, now in the British Library, a collection of works of his authorship signed "By the King's Hand", which also holds two masses, a motet, an anthem and other songs and ballads, for voices and for instruments. It is thought that the song may have been written for Catherine of Aragon. It was presumably played at court with the king's other compositions, but its simple and catchy melody made it a popular tune beyond the court, soon heard at fairs, in taverns and at other gatherings. It is believed to have been one of the favourite pieces of Queen Elizabeth I. Contemporary documents and publications refer to it, and musicians made many variants and instrumental arrangements of it in the following years; The Complaynt of Scotland, written in 1548, lists "Passetyme with gude companye" among the songs popular in Scotland in the early part of the century.

The song belongs to the first years of Henry's reign, a time of exuberance and extravagance at the English court, made possible by the kingdom's political stability and the wealth of the royal finances. Banquets and feasts were held continually, as were outdoor sports and pastimes: hunting, hawking, jousting and archery tournaments. The young king was himself a skilled sportsman, excelling at riding, archery, wrestling and real tennis. The song praises these entertainments and the carefree mood of the court, and gives them a moral defence: good company is better than idleness, for idleness breeds vice.

It remains a favourite of choirs and has been recorded with lute, recorder, trombone, percussion and flute, among other instruments; its early Renaissance melody is often heard in films and documentaries about Henry VIII and the Tudors. Rock and folk groups have taken it up too: Jethro Tull as "King Henry's Madrigal", Gryphon on their self-titled album of 1973, Blackmore's Night as "Past Time with Good Company", and the Swingle Singers in 1989.
== meaning
A young king's defence of pleasure. The singer means to love good company, to hunt, sing and dance, as long as he lives, and will let no one stop him. Its later verses give the reason: company keeps a man from idleness, from which every vice comes, and so the best course is to choose good company and shun the bad. The mood is brisk, confident and cheerful.
