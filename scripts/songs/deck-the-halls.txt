== story
"Deck the Halls" is a Christmas carol with a Welsh tune and English words. The melody belongs to "Nos Galan" ("New Year's Eve"), a traditional Welsh winter carol. The tune is said to date back to the sixteenth century; it is first found in a musical manuscript of the 1700s by the Welsh harpist John Parry, who published it in British Harmony, Being a Collection of Antient Welsh Airs, and "Nos Galan" was published in 1794, though it is much older. In the eighteenth century the tune spread widely: Mozart is said to have used it in his 18th violin sonata (1778), and Haydn later arranged it under its Welsh title (1803). The poet John Ceiriog Hughes wrote Welsh lyrics of his own for it, and folk singers later added a middle verse.

The English words were written by the Scottish musician Thomas Oliphant. They first appeared in 1862 in the second volume of Welsh Melodies, a set of four volumes by John Thomas, with Welsh words by John Jones, known as Talhaiarn, and English words by Oliphant. The two texts were printed side by side. Some early sources say that Oliphant translated Talhaiarn's Welsh, but this is not so in any strict or literal sense: the Welsh verse speaks of the pleasures of New Year's Eve, and Oliphant wrote a new song of holly, feasting and the passing of the old year.

The words soon changed. A version in the Pennsylvania School Journal of December 1877, printed again four years later in The Franklin Square Song Collection, removed every reference to drinking and dropped the third "fa la la" line, which in the Welsh original answers an instrumental flourish. The title was "Deck the Hall", in the singular, until about 1892, when the plural "Deck the Halls" is first found. Other common changes put "Yuletide" in place of Oliphant's "Christmas"; "yule" comes from the Old English "geol", a word often used for Christmas and its season.

The older tune lived on in Wales beside the carol. In 1912 Ruth Herbert Lewis made a wax cylinder recording of a Welshman, Benjamin Davies, singing "Can y Coach faier" to the melody now tied to "Deck the Halls"; it can be heard on the website of the British Library Sound Archive. The melody as sung to the English words differs in its third and fourth "fa la la" lines from the one sung and played in Wales. A phrase from Oliphant's words, "'Tis the season", has become a byword for the Christmas and holiday season.
== meaning
A song of midwinter merrymaking: decorating the house with holly, dressing up, singing to the harp, drinking and dancing together while the old year goes out and the new one comes in. A nonsense refrain follows every line. Oliphant's original verses include drinking, which later school versions took out. The mood is fast and cheerful.
