== story
The Epic of Gilgamesh, from ancient Mesopotamia, is the world's oldest surviving example of narrative literature. Its history begins with five Sumerian poems about Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, some of which may date to the Third Dynasty of Ur (c. 2100 BCE). These independent stories were later used as material for a combined epic in Akkadian. The first surviving version of it, the "Old Babylonian" version, dates to the 18th century BCE and is named after its opening words, Shūtur eli sharrī, "Surpassing All Other Kings"; only a few of its tablets survive. The later Standard Babylonian version, compiled by Sîn-lēqi-unninni between the 13th and 10th centuries BCE, opens Sha naqba īmuru, "He Who Saw the Deep". About two thirds of its twelve tablets have been recovered, some of the best copies from the library of the 7th-century BCE Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh.

In the first half of the story Gilgamesh, two-thirds god and one-third man, oppresses his people, and the gods create the wild man Enkidu as his equal. Tamed by Shamhat, Enkidu comes to Uruk and fights Gilgamesh; the two become friends, travel to the Cedar Forest, kill its guardian Humbaba and, when the goddess Ishtar sends the Bull of Heaven against them, kill the Bull too. The gods condemn Enkidu to die of an illness. In the second half, Gilgamesh, stricken by his friend's death and afraid of his own, journeys to the end of the world to find Utnapishtim, the only man who survived the great flood with his wife and was given eternal life. He learns that immortality is not for him; a plant that restores youth is stolen by a serpent, and he returns to Uruk, whose great walls he praises.

About 15,000 fragments of cuneiform tablets were found in the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh by Austen Henry Layard, Hormuzd Rassam and W. K. Loftus in the early 1850s. In 1872 George Smith of the British Museum read translated fragments, among them the flood story, before the Society of Biblical Archaeology, and he published fuller translations in 1875 and 1876. Andrew George's critical edition of 2003 is now the standard one. The performance on this page is sung in Sumerian, the language of the older poems: its opening, "ud re-a ud su₃-ra₂ re-a" ("In those days, in those distant days"), is the first line of Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld, a Sumerian poem of 330 lines that the twelfth tablet of the epic mainly translates into Akkadian. The words printed here are its prologue of 26 lines, on the beginnings of the world and Enki's voyage by boat to the netherworld; the story then turns to the huluppu tree that Gilgamesh fells for Inanna.
== meaning
The epic asks what a life is worth if it must end. Its hero begins as a tyrant, is changed by friendship, is broken by grief, and searches for a way to escape death, only to learn that the gods kept life for themselves and gave death to humankind. What remains to him are the things people make and share: a friend, a well-ruled city, the walls of Uruk and the story itself.
