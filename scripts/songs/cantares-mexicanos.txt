== story
The Cantares Mexicanos is a manuscript collection of Nahuatl songs or poems written down in the 16th century. Its 91 songs form the largest collection of Nahuatl song and hold more than half of all the traditional Nahuatl songs known. The manuscript is kept in the National Library of Mexico in Mexico City, and it is described in the census of prose manuscripts in the native tradition in the Handbook of Middle American Indians.

The songs are made up of short stanzas of about thirty words each, set out in the manuscript as hanging paragraphs, about 1,700 of them. Many songs have eight stanzas, most have more, and the longest has 114. The text itself and the accounts of Bernardino de Sahagún and other observers of the time show how they were performed: to the upright skin drum (huehuetl) and the horizontal log drum (teponaztli), each able to sound two tones a fifth or a major third apart. Gongs, horns and other instruments could join them, and a full performance might include costumed dancing, often with mimicry.

The collection reached modern readers in stages. The Mexican scholar Ángel María Garibay Kintana published a Spanish edition and translation of much of it in the second and third volumes of his Poesía náhuatl (1965, 1968), and Miguel León-Portilla later edited a two-volume Spanish translation of the whole codex, published by the National Autonomous University of Mexico. In 1985 John Bierhorst published a complete transcription and English translation, Cantares Mexicanos: Songs of the Aztecs, with a dictionary and concordance.

Scholars praised Bierhorst's transcription for its accuracy but criticised his translations as misleading, shaped by his view that the Cantares are "ghost songs", part of a colonial revival movement like the ghost dances of the Plains peoples. Garibay and León-Portilla, and later the translator David Bowles, read the songs instead as part of a long artistic and philosophical tradition that began before the Spanish conquest.
== meaning
The collection gathers short songs in many stanzas, made to be sung to drums and often danced. Scholars disagree on what they were: Bierhorst read them as songs of a colonial revival movement, while Garibay, León-Portilla and Bowles see in them the poetry of a tradition older than the conquest.
