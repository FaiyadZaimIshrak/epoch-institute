== story
"The Sacred War" is one of the best-known Soviet songs of the Second World War, known in Russia as the Great Patriotic War. Two days after the German invasion, on 24 June 1941, its words appeared at the same time in the newspapers Izvestia and Krasnaya Zvezda, signed by the poet Vasily Lebedev-Kumach. Alexander Alexandrov, founder of the Red Army's song and dance ensemble and later the composer of the State Anthem of the Soviet Union, set them to music at once. There was no time to print words and notes, so he wrote them in chalk on a blackboard and the singers and players copied them into their notebooks; one more day went to rehearsal.

On 26 June 1941 a group of the ensemble not yet sent to the front sang it for the first time at Moscow's Belorussky Railway Station; eyewitnesses recalled that it was sung five times in a row. A memorial plaque was placed on the station building in May 2005. Yet until 15 October 1941 the song was not widely performed, as its tone was thought too tragic beside the hope of a quick victory at little cost. Only from that day, when the Wehrmacht had already taken Kaluga, Rzhev and Kalinin, was it played every morning on all-Union radio after the chimes of the Kremlin clock. It became hugely popular at the front, especially in the hard defensive battles, and was recorded twice during the war, in 1941 and 1942.

After the war the Alexandrov Ensemble sang it often, at home and on tours abroad. On 22 May 2007 the ensemble performed it at NATO headquarters in Brussels to loud applause; some Russian media read this as an ambiguous answer to Western attitudes to Russia. The song was translated into Ukrainian by Mykola Bazhan, into German as "Der Heilige Krieg" by Stephan Hermlin, and into Polish, best known in Krzysztof Gruszczyński's version; a Polish version that leaves out the Soviet Union and a Hungarian version are also known.

In the 1990s, after Soviet censorship ended, Russian media claimed that the words were really written during the First World War by Aleksandr Bode (1865–1939), an amateur poet and teacher of literature from Rybinsk, and that Lebedev-Kumach had plagiarised them. A Moscow court ruled on 21 December 1999 that the claim was untrue and defamatory and that Lebedev-Kumach was the author, and in June 2000 the newspaper Nezavisimaya Gazeta was made to print a retraction. A draft in Lebedev-Kumach's hand, with many corrections, is reported to be in the Russian State Archive of Literature and Art. The musicologist Evgeniy Levashev nonetheless defended Bode's authorship, questioned the court's decision (it had refused the defendants an expert examination), and argued that the first melody was also Bode's, with Alexandrov as arranger. Against this, Alexandrov's own "Song of Kliment Voroshilov" (words by Osip Kolychev, 1938) has a melody in which the musicologist A. N. Sokhor heard a forerunner of "The Sacred War".

In 2018–2019 the Ukrainian kobzar Taras Sylenko found among the songs of insurgents who fought the Bolsheviks for the Ukrainian People's Republic around 1920 a song whose melody is almost identical to Alexandrov's. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 his account gained new attention and was supported by the writer Yuri Andrukhovych. The music critic Lyubov Morozova suggested that Bode's authorship could explain it, the song reaching the people through his wife, a singer, and noted that its 3/4 time is most unusual for a Soviet march.
== meaning
A call to the whole country to rise and fight the German invasion as a people's war; grim, stern and solemn.
