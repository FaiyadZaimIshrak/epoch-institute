== story
Phos Hilaron is an ancient Christian hymn written in Koine Greek, known in the Western Church by its Latin title Lumen Hilare and in English as "O Gladsome Light". It is one of the earliest known Christian hymns recorded outside the Bible that is still in use today. Its author is unknown. It was first written down in the Apostolic Constitutions, a work of the late 3rd or early 4th century, in a collection of songs for the morning, the evening, before meals and at the lighting of candles. It was to be sung when the lamps were lit in the evening, which gave it a second name, the "Lamplighting Hymn". Unlike the other songs of that collection, whose words partly come from Scripture, it is considered the first hymn in the modern sense: rhythmic in structure, in twelve verses of five to eleven syllables.

By the time of Basil of Caesarea (329–379) it was already old: he spoke of its singing as a cherished tradition of the church, though some attribute the hymn to Basil himself. Others credit Athenogenes of Pedachtoë, a saint whose day is 16 July and who, by the account in the Roman Martyrology, sang a hymn of joy on his way to martyrdom by fire in Pontus and left it in writing to his disciples. His story closely resembles that of a bishop martyred with ten disciples at Sebaste in Armenia under the emperor Diocletian, most probably about 305. Sophronius of Jerusalem (560–638), a poet, is believed to have revised the hymn, and Orthodox liturgical books often name him as its author. A tradition links the hymn's early use to Jerusalem, where a lamp was kept burning in the empty tomb of Christ and a candle lit from it was carried out while the hymn was sung.

The hymn is a fixed part of Orthodox vespers, sung or recited every day after the lamp-lighting psalms. The original melody used by the Greek Orthodox Church spans almost two octaves and is considered taxing on the voice. In the Armenian Rite, where it is called Luys Zvart, it is sung only on Saturday evening and on the eve of certain feasts.

In 1834 John Keble, one of the leaders of the Oxford Movement in the Church of England, translated it into English as "Hail, Gladdening Light", keeping the irregular metre of the original. No existing hymn tune fitted it, so John Stainer, organist of St Paul's Cathedral in London, wrote the tune Sebaste for it, published in Hymns Ancient and Modern in 1875; Charles Wood set Keble's version as an anthem for eight voices in 1912. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow and Robert Bridges made further translations. The traditional Anglican Evening Prayer did not call for it, but the American Book of Common Prayer of 1979 prescribes it as an optional canticle before the psalms, and the Lutheran Service Book of the Lutheran Church – Missouri Synod includes it in early evening prayer. Composers who have set it include Tchaikovsky and Rachmaninoff, in their All-Night Vigils, and Arthur Sullivan.
== meaning
An evening hymn for the moment the lamps are lit at sunset. It praises Christ as light and, as the sun goes down, gives praise to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. The mood is calm and joyful.
