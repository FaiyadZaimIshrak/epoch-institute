== story
"La Borinqueña" is the regional anthem of Puerto Rico. Its title comes from Borinquén, the Taíno name for the island. The music was first credited to Félix Astol Artés in 1867, as a habanera danza with romantic words, but there is some evidence that Francisco Ramírez, a native of San Germán, wrote it in 1860 and called it "La Almojábana". In 1868 Lola Rodríguez de Tió wrote a poem in support of the Puerto Rican revolution, which was set to the Ramírez–Astol music. For fear of an investigation by the Spanish government, Ramírez asked Astol, a native of Catalonia who would raise no suspicion, to claim the music as his own.

After the cession of the island to the United States, Rodríguez de Tió's popular revolutionary words were thought too subversive to be adopted officially, and in 1903 the Asturias-born Manuel Fernández Juncos wrote a set of words that avoided confrontation. Luis Miranda, the musical director of the band of Puerto Rico's 65th Infantry Regiment, arranged the tune as a march in 1922. After Puerto Rico became the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico in 1952, its first elected governor, Luis Muñoz Marín, signed Law No. 2 of 24 July 1952, which made a changed version of the music the island's anthem; at the new government's request Ramón Collado rearranged it in a more military style. Fernández Juncos's words were approved by Governor Carlos Romero Barceló on 27 July 1977 by Law No. 123, which required only that the anthem be played as a march "in a martial manner" and set no official arrangement. An official revision of 2003 keeps it a march. It is usually performed today as a slow march, without the opening paseo of the original tune.

Under the 1952 law, "La Borinqueña" and "The Star-Spangled Banner" are both played at official events, while at international sports competitions such as the Olympics only "La Borinqueña" is played. A short version of the revolutionary words, the first half of the first verse and the second half of the third, is still sometimes sung. In 2012 Yajaira Sierra Sastre, an aspiring astronaut, took part in projects with the National Nanotechnology Infrastructure Network and the Cornell Nanoscale Facility that included writing the smallest "national" anthem ever, "La Borinqueña Más Pequeña". The anthem with its revolutionary words is sung by the Sharks, the Puerto Rican gang, early in Steven Spielberg's film of West Side Story (2021); neither the stage musical nor its 1961 film had used it.
== meaning
The official words of 1903 praise the island as a garden in flower under an always clear sky, with the waves murmuring at its feet, the land Columbus admired when he reached its shores, an island born of the sea and the sun. The revolutionary words of 1868 are a call to arms: they ask the people of Borinquén to follow Cuba, which is rising, to answer the drums of war and repeat the cry of Lares, to fear neither the cannon nor death, and to win their freedom from tyranny with the machete, women fighting beside men. The first are serene and admiring, the second urgent and defiant.
