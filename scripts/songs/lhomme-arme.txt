== story
L'homme armé ("the armed man") is a secular song of the Late Middle Ages, from the Burgundian School. The tune circulated in both the Mixolydian and the Dorian mode. Its first appearance coincides exactly with the fall of Constantinople to the Ottoman Turks in 1453, an event that shook Europe. One of the earliest datable uses of the melody is the combinative chanson Il sera pour vous conbatu / L'homme armé, ascribed to Robert Morton and now thought to date from around 1463; an anonymous three-voice setting in the Mellon Chansonnier may be earlier but cannot be dated precisely. In 1523 Pietro Aron named Antoine Busnois as the composer of the tune, but he wrote some 70 years after its first appearance and no other source supports him.

Why the armed man mattered so much is debated. He may stand for Saint Michael the Archangel, an allusion Johannes Regis (c. 1425 – c. 1496) seems to have meant in his Dum sacrum mysterium / Missa L'homme armé. He may be the name of a tavern, the Maison L'Homme Armé, near Guillaume Du Fay's rooms in Cambrai. Or the song may refer to arming for a new crusade against the Ottomans. There is ample evidence that it had special meaning for the Order of the Golden Fleece. Another recent theory sees it as a stylised mix of a street cry and a trumpet call, possibly from the late 14th or early 15th century. Richard Taruskin noted that it was a favourite of Charles the Bold and suggested it may have been written for him, but researchers have shown that it was in use before he became Duke of Burgundy.

The song is remembered because Renaissance composers used it as a cantus firmus, the borrowed melody on which a setting of the Latin Mass is built. More than 40 Masses titled Missa L'homme armé survive, more than for any other secular song, about 30 of them from 1450 to 1510. Taruskin argued that Busnois wrote the earliest, while other scholars favour Du Fay; Guillaume Faugues, Regis and Johannes Ockeghem may also have written settings in the 1450s. Josquin's two Masses, the Missa L'homme armé super voces musicales and the Missa L'homme armé sexti toni, are among the best known, and Pipelare, Pierre de la Rue, Cristóbal de Morales, Du Fay and Palestrina each wrote more than one. Six anonymous settings, probably by one composer, survive in a Neapolitan manuscript said to have been a gift to Beatrice of Aragon of music Charles the Bold favoured. The practice lasted into the 17th century, with a late setting by Carissimi. The tune's clearly divided phrases made it well suited to counterpoint and canon and easy to hear within many voices.

Composers still return to it. Peter Maxwell Davies wrote a parody Mass on it (1968, revised 1971), and Karl Jenkins drew heavily on it for The Armed Man – A Mass for Peace (1999), dedicated to the victims of the Kosovo War of 1998–1999. It also appears in works by Joel Thompson, Mark Alburger, Christopher Marshall, Beverly Grigsby, David Earl and Marc-André Hamelin, in folk and neofolk recordings, and in the video games Darklands and Pentiment.
== meaning
By its title the song is about an armed man, a figure read as Saint Michael, as a call to arms for a crusade against the Ottomans, and as a figure tied to the Burgundian court and the Order of the Golden Fleece.
