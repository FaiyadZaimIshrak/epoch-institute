== story
At the time the anthem was written, Mexico was still feeling the effects of its bitter defeat in the Mexican–American War, in which it had ceded more than half of its territory to the United States, and the country was demoralised and divided. On 12 November 1853 President Antonio López de Santa Anna announced a competition for a national anthem, with a prize for the best poem of patriotic ideals. The poet Francisco González Bocanegra at first refused to enter, saying that writing love poems needed very different skills. According to the story told of it, his fiancée, Guadalupe González del Pino, lured him into a bedroom of her parents' house, locked him in, and would not let him out until he had written an entry. Pictures of events in Mexican history in the room helped to inspire him, and after four hours he slid all ten stanzas under the door. He won the competition by unanimous vote, and his victory was announced in the Official Journal of the Federation on 3 February 1854.

A musical setting was chosen at the same time. The winner, Juan Bottesini, was rejected because his entry was disliked, and a second contest was held. It was won on 12 August 1854 by Jaime Nunó, a Catalan-born band leader whom Santa Anna had met in Cuba and invited to Mexico to direct its military bands; his music was titled "Dios y libertad" ("God and Freedom"). The song was adopted as the national anthem on Independence Day, 16 September 1854. Its first performance, at the Santa Anna Theatre, was directed by Bottesini and sung by the soprano Claudia Florenti and the tenor Lorenzo Salvi.

The anthem has ten stanzas and a chorus. Since 1943, by a decree of President Manuel Ávila Camacho, the official anthem consists of the chorus and the first, fifth, sixth and tenth stanzas; at sporting events such as the Olympic Games and the FIFA World Cup only the chorus and first stanza are sung. Mexican law regulates its use closely: it may not be altered or used for commercial purposes, it must be performed with solemnity, those present stand at attention, and it is taught in schools and played at the start and close of radio and television broadcasting. Performers who have got it wrong in public have been fined. A long-told story that its music was privately owned by a German publisher and later by Nunó and two Americans is not correct: the anthem is in the public domain in the United States, and under Mexican law the state holds moral rights, but not property rights, to the national symbols.

The historian Javier Garciadiego, speaking at the ceremony for the anthem's 150th anniversary in 2004, said that it set aside divisions and strife and encouraged national unity; on that day Mexico City and other parts of the country stopped to sing it together, as did Mexicans abroad. It has been described as one of the symbols of Mexican identity. An archaic line of the first stanza has often been misheard as the name of an enemy, and the misheard word has been used as a personal name and as a nickname for the anthem itself. Since a change to the law on 8 December 2005, the words may be translated into Mexico's Indigenous languages; official translations exist in Chinanteco, Hña Hñu, Mixtec, Maya, Nahuatl and Tenek.
== meaning
A call to the people of Mexico to defend the homeland against any foreign enemy, recalling the country's victories in battle and praising those who fight for it. Its mood is martial and solemn, and its theme is a united nation ready to defend itself.
