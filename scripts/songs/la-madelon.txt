== story
"Quand Madelon...", usually called "La Madelon", has words by Louis Bousquet (1870–1941) and music by Camille Robert (1872–1957). In 1913 the singer Bach (Charles-Joseph Pasquier) asked them for a new patriotic song; the music began as a march for a brass band, to which Bousquet wrote cheerful words. Bach first sang it on 19 March 1914 at the café-concert l'Eldorado in Paris, where it had little success.

The war made it famous. In August 1914 the songwriter Sioul, who had heard the first performance and was now an artillery soldier billeted in the Jules-Ferry school at Fontenay-sous-Bois, sang it to his comrades. Bach sang it to soldiers on leave in the army theatre, and on 16 June 1916 he sang it at the inn of mother Misquette at Étival-Clairefontaine behind the front in the Vosges, where it was a great success; from there it spread along the front, carried by the gunners and by the comic soldier-singers popular in the war. Its words were clean at a time when most soldiers' songs were bawdy, which may partly explain its long popularity, and as the war went on it became a patriotic song. Marcelly made the first recording in 1917; in the United States the Victor Military Band recorded it in 1919 with the French singer Marcel Journet, and Arthur Fields sang an English version in 1918. It also became popular among Spanish soldiers. In 1921 plaques were placed on the school at Fontenay-sous-Bois and at Étival-Clairefontaine to mark the song's two first successes at the front.

In Lyon soldiers sang it in thanks to Clotilde Bizolon (1871–1940), "the mother of the poilus", who kept a refreshment stall for soldiers at Perrache station and was given the Legion of Honour in 1925; she became the city's own Madelon. Lucien Boyer wrote a sequel, "La Madelon de la Victoire", in 1919.

The song was revived in the Second World War: Marlene Dietrich sang it in Paris at the celebration of 14 July 1939. Films drew on it, from Madelon's Daughter (1937) to Jean Boyer's comedy La Madelon (1955), in which Line Renaud sang it surrounded by soldiers, and the Spanish singer Sara Montiel sang it in El Último Cuplé (1957). Louis-Ferdinand Céline questioned the easy reputation of the song's Madelon in Voyage au bout de la nuit (1932). It remains a well-known patriotic song in France, though the scholar François Genton holds that its words and tune now interest people mainly as historical documents.
== meaning
Soldiers flirt with a lovely young waitress in a country tavern. Light, cheerful and clean, it was sung as a soldiers' song and became a patriotic one.

== notes
Status pd for the words: Bousquet died in 1941, and the song was performed in 1914 (published before 1930). Camille Robert's music (d. 1957) is still protected in France until the end of 2027; only the words matter for import, but the editor may want to note it.
The meaning rests on the English article's summary (soldiers flirting with a young waitress in a country tavern; clean words) and the French one's "texte gai".
The English article dates the words 1914; the French one says Bach commissioned the song in 1913 and first sang it on 19 March 1914.
The French article says Bach sang it on 16 June 1916 at Étival-Clairefontaine; it calls it the song's second, decisive success at the front after Sioul's of August 1914. Bach's army-theatre performances are given as the reason for its success in the lede.
The English article titles the 1937 film "Madelon's Daughter"; it may be the same as "Victoire, la fille de Madelon" (1939) in the French list, or not: kept apart.
Left out: the Swiss parallel Gilberte de Courgenay.
