== story
"The Skye Boat Song" (Roud 3772) is a late 19th-century Scottish song made from a Gaelic song composed around 1782 by William Ross, Cuachag nan Craobh ("The Cuckoo in the Grove"), in which the poet laments to a cuckoo that Lady Marion Ross, whom he loves, is rejecting him. The English words instead tell of the flight of Prince Charles Edward Stuart, "Bonnie Prince Charlie", from Benbecula to the Isle of Skye as he evaded government soldiers after his defeat at the Battle of Culloden in 1746: disguised as a serving maid, he escaped in a small boat with the help of Flora MacDonald.

Anne Campbell MacLeod (1855–1921) heard the air in the 1870s. According to the folk-music collector Andrew Kuntz, she was being rowed over Loch Coruisk on Skye when the rowers broke into the Gaelic rowing song, and she set down what she remembered of it for a book she was writing with Sir Harold Boulton, who added the words about the prince. Set in the style of an iorram, a Gaelic rowing song, it was first published by Boulton and MacLeod in London in 1884, in Songs of the North, which went into at least twenty editions; later editions dropped MacLeod's name for "Old Highland rowing measure arranged by Malcolm Lawson". Other compilers soon took it up. Kuntz observed that it succeeded perhaps too well as modern romantic literature, for people soon began "remembering" that they had learned it as children and that its words were old Gaelic lines. It appears in no older book of Scottish songs.

Robert Louis Stevenson, after hearing the Jacobite song sung by a visitor, probably in 1885, judged its words "unworthy" and wrote new verses "more in harmony with the plaintive tune", published in 1892. Both texts draw on Jacobite themes nearly a century and a half after the events; Stevenson's version, which gives the boat's course past Mull, Rum and Eigg, seems to describe a flight from the mainland, which is unhistorical, for the prince's only time on Skye followed his crossing from Benbecula.

From its first recording, by Tom Bryce on 29 April 1899, the song became a standard of Scottish folk and dance musicians, often played as a slow lullaby or waltz in a rocking 6/8 time. Michael Tippett arranged it in 1957 as "Over the Sea to Skye" among his Four Songs from the British Isles; Alfred Deller recorded it in 1958 and Paul Robeson in 1959 and 1960, and Tom Jones, Roger Whittaker with Des O'Connor (a UK top-ten duet in 1986), Rod Stewart and Tori Amos followed. Patrick Troughton played it on his recorder as the Second Doctor in Doctor Who in 1968. Bear McCreary adapted it, with Stevenson's poem, as the opening titles of the television series Outlander (2014). Pipers played it as the coffin of Queen Elizabeth II travelled up the Long Walk to Windsor Castle on 19 September 2022. The line "Over the Sea to Skye" has become a mainstay of the island's tourist trade.
== meaning
A rowing song sung as if by the boatmen carrying the defeated prince over the sea from Benbecula to Skye, out of reach of the government soldiers searching for him. It is a song of defeat and loyalty, wistful rather than warlike, and its slow swaying rhythm made it a lullaby.
