== story
Lekha Dodi is a Hebrew liturgical song sung in the synagogue on Friday at dusk, usually at sundown, to welcome the Sabbath before the evening service. It is part of Kabbalat Shabbat, the service of welcoming the Sabbath. It was composed in the 16th century by Solomon Alkabetz, who was born in the Ottoman city of Thessaloniki and became a leading Kabbalist of Safed. As was common at the time, the poem is an acrostic: the first letters of its first eight stanzas spell the author's name. It is one of the latest Hebrew poems to be regularly accepted into the traditional liturgy.

Its refrain takes a phrase from the Song of Songs (7:12), which the Talmudic sage Rava (Abba ben Joseph bar Ḥama) read, in Tractate Eruvin 21b, as the people of Israel speaking to God. Alkabetz drew on the rabbinic reading of the Song of Songs, in which the maiden stands for the people of Israel and the lover for God, and on the books of the Prophets, which use the same image. The poem asks God to bring the great Sabbath of messianic deliverance.

The song carries its own customs. During the last verse the whole congregation rises and turns to the west, or to the door, and some leave the sanctuary; at the words greeting the bride they bow and turn back to the front, and traditional congregations face Jerusalem for the rest of the service. Practice varies: many Reform congregations leave out the verses about messianic redemption, Sephardic congregations of the Jerusalem and Aleppo rites leave out the verses that speak of suffering, and the Sephardic rite and Hasidic tradition use a different last section.

Few liturgical texts have been sung to so many tunes. Early in the 20th century Abraham Zevi Idelsohn recorded hundreds. Some Sephardic congregations chant it to an old Moorish melody far older than the text: old prayer books direct it to be sung to the tune of a composition of Judah Halevi, who died nearly five centuries before Alkabetz, a tune brought to the Land of Israel by refugees from Spain. In some old-style Ashkenazi synagogues the cantor chants the verses at length and the congregation answers with the refrain; in most Ashkenazi Orthodox synagogues everyone sings it together. Northern European congregations kept two older tunes for parts of the year: one for the Omer weeks between Passover and Shavuot, which has been compared to "Lillibullero" and to Mozart's Marriage of Figaro and resembles late 17th-century German folk song, and one for the Three Weeks before Tisha b'Av. A variant of the second was used by Benedetto Marcello in his setting of Psalm 19 (Venice, 1724) as an air of the German Jews, and the cantor Eduard Birnbaum traced it to a Polish folk song printed by Oskar Kolberg in 1857. Another old melody, sung in the City of London's synagogues, was used by Isaac Nathan for "She Walks in Beauty" in his setting of Byron's Hebrew Melodies (1815).
== meaning
A mystical poem that welcomes the Sabbath as a bride, with the congregation and God going out together to meet her. Its stanzas move from the holiness of the Sabbath to a call for the restoration of Jerusalem and the people of Israel and the coming of messianic deliverance. The mood is joyful and expectant.
