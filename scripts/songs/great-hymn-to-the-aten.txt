== story
The Great Hymn to the Aten is the longest of several hymn-poems addressed to the Aten, the sun-disk worshipped as the supreme god under the 18th Dynasty pharaoh Akhenaten. It was composed in the middle of the 14th century BC and is attributed, depending on the version, to Akhenaten himself or to his courtiers. Akhenaten broke with centuries of Egyptian religious practice by forbidding the worship of other gods; scholars disagree whether his Atenism was monotheistic or henotheistic. His reforms were later regarded as heretical and reversed under his successor Tutankhamun.

The hymn belongs to the art of the Amarna period, which included new temples and a whole new capital at Akhet-Aten, present-day Amarna. The rock tombs of courtiers there carry prayers and hymns to the Aten, or to the Aten and the king together; a shorter hymn appears in almost identical form in five tombs. The long version was found in the tomb of the courtier Ay, who later became pharaoh. The beginning of the text is damaged and cannot be read. The Egyptologist John Darnell holds that the hymn was sung, and Toby Wilkinson quotes a description of it as one of the most splendid poems to survive from the world before Homer.

Modern readers soon compared it with the Bible. James Henry Breasted noted its likeness to Psalm 104 and believed the hymn inspired the psalm; Arthur Weigall set the two texts side by side and judged a direct connection likely, either through a common Syrian source or by descent from the Egyptian poem. Miriam Lichtheim thought the resemblances came from the general likeness of Egyptian hymns and biblical psalms. Mark S. Smith notes that enthusiasm even for indirect influence has cooled, though Jan Assmann and Donald Redford argue for Egyptian influence, and Aaron Koller suggests the themes may have passed on while Egypt still ruled Canaan. C. S. Lewis discussed the hymn in Reflections on the Psalms (1958).

Readings of the hymn have changed with the evidence. Breasted called Akhenaten the first monotheist, Flinders Petrie in 1899 praised his worship as free of superstition, and Henry Hall in 1913 called him the first example of the scientific mind. Later scholars were more cautious. Dominic Montserrat reads the Amarna hymns as formal royal eulogies centred on the king, and quotes John Baines that Amarna religion was a religion of god and king, or even of king first. Redford points out that earlier kings had claimed the same role as son and mediator of the sun, and that the evidence from Thebes and Amarna gives little support to the idea of Akhenaten as the forerunner of biblical monotheism.

Philip Glass set the hymn to music in his 1984 opera Akhnaten.
== meaning
A formal hymn of praise to the sun-disk Aten as the one god, honouring the god together with the king and queen. It presents Akhenaten as the god's son, the one who alone truly knows him and speaks for him.
