== story
"Lift Every Voice and Sing" is a hymn written by James Weldon Johnson (1871–1938) and set to music by his brother J. Rosamond Johnson (1873–1954). After graduating from Atlanta University in 1894, James Weldon Johnson returned to his hometown of Jacksonville, Florida, as principal of Stanton High School, the first public high school for African Americans in the state. He first meant to write a poem for President Abraham Lincoln's birthday, but chose instead to write about the struggles of African Americans after the Reconstruction era, as Jim Crow laws were being passed across the South. The poem was first recited by 500 students in 1900; his brother later set it to music, by one account in 1905.

After the Great Fire of 1901 in Jacksonville the brothers moved to New York City to work on Broadway, but the song stayed with the school. Johnson later wrote that the children of Jacksonville kept singing it, took it to other schools, became teachers and taught it in turn, so that within twenty years it was sung across the South and beyond. The male gospel group Manhattan Harmony Four recorded it in 1923 as "Lift Every Voice and Sing (National Negro Anthem)". In 1939 Augusta Savage, the only Black woman commissioned for the New York World's Fair, exhibited a sculpture named after the song, a choir of children shaped into a harp, which the organizers retitled "The Harp" and which was destroyed when the fair closed.

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) called the hymn the "Negro national anthem" in 1919 (its promotion by the association had begun by 1917), and James Weldon Johnson became the association's first executive secretary the following year. It is now often called "the Black national anthem" and has appeared in 54 Christian hymnals. The name has been criticized: Timothy Askew of Clark Atlanta University argued that it could suggest a wish for separatism, though the words name no race, and other commentators have called it a slight to "The Star-Spangled Banner". The NAACP's Hilary O. Shelton answered that the hymn had been welcomed by an interracial group and spoke of hope for full citizenship. In January 2021 Representative Jim Clyburn sponsored HR 301, a bill to make it the national hymn of the United States, with "The Star-Spangled Banner" remaining the national anthem.

The hymn has marked many public moments. Maya Angelou's 1969 autobiography I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings has it sung at her eighth-grade graduation; Kim Weston opened the Wattstax festival with it in 1972; Melba Moore's 1990 recording with many R&B and gospel singers was entered into the Congressional Record. The Rev. Joseph Lowery began his benediction at Barack Obama's inauguration on January 20, 2009, with its third stanza, and Denyce Graves sang it at the opening of the National Museum of African American History and Culture in 2016. In 2017 a music professor, Laura Ellis, played it on the University of Florida's carillon when the white supremacist Richard Spencer spoke there.

During the protests that followed the police murder of George Floyd in 2020 the hymn was sung at demonstrations, and the National Football League announced that it would be played or performed before every Week 1 game of the 2020 season; Alicia Keys sang it for the NFL Kickoff Game, and as of February 2026 it had been performed at six consecutive Super Bowl pregame ceremonies. The 1923 and 1990 recordings were added to the National Recording Registry in 2016.
== meaning
A hymn of thanksgiving and a prayer for faithfulness and freedom, written from the experience of African Americans after slavery. It draws on the biblical Exodus, the journey out of bondage, and is solemn and hopeful at once.
