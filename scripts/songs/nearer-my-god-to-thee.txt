== story
"Nearer, My God, to Thee" is a Christian hymn written in 1841 by the English poet and Unitarian hymn writer Sarah Flower Adams at her home, "Sunnybank", in Loughton, Essex. It retells the story of Jacob's dream in the Book of Genesis (28:11–12), in which Jacob, sleeping with a stone for a pillow, sees a ladder reaching from earth to heaven with the angels of God going up and down on it. Adams's sister, the composer Eliza Flower, gave it its first music, for William Johnson Fox's collection Hymns and Anthems. Edward Henry Bickersteth Jr. later added a sixth verse.

The hymn has been sung to many tunes. In the United Kingdom it is usually sung to "Horbury" (1861) by John Bacchus Dykes, named after a village near Wakefield where Dykes had found "peace and comfort"; elsewhere it is usually sung to "Bethany" (1856) by Lowell Mason, which has also made its way into British use; and British Methodists prefer "Propior Deo" ("Nearer to God"), written by Arthur Sullivan in 1872. The hymn books of the British churches have printed these and other tunes, from the Methodist Hymn Book of 1933 to Hymns Ancient and Modern, and many other 19th-century composers set it.

It became a hymn of mourning in the United States. The dying words of President William McKinley in September 1901 were said to be its first lines, and at 3:30 pm on 14 September 1901, after five minutes of silence across the nation, bands across the country played it, his favourite hymn, in his memory; it was also played as President James Garfield was interred at Lakeview Cemetery in Cleveland, and at the funerals of Warren G. Harding and Gerald Ford. A Confederate army band is said to have played it as the survivors of Pickett's Charge at the Battle of Gettysburg came back from their failed assault, and the Rough Riders sang it over their dead after the Battle of Las Guasimas.

The hymn is best known through the sinking of the RMS Titanic in April 1912. Some survivors reported that the ship's string ensemble played it as the ship went down: Violet Jessop said so in her account of 1934, and Eva Hart claimed it was the last tune the band played, while Archibald Gracie IV firmly denied it and the wireless operator Harold Bride remembered a piece called "Autumn". The bandleader Wallace Hartley, who died with all the other musicians, liked the hymn and had wished it played at his funeral; his family were sure he would have used the "Propior Deo" tune, whose opening notes are on his memorial. The hymn had already been sung by the crew and passengers of the SS Valencia as it sank off the Canadian coast in 1906, which may be where the Titanic story came from. Films of the disaster, from Atlantic (1929) to Titanic (1997), have the band play it, and composers from Carl Nielsen and Sigfrid Karg-Elert to Joseph Bonnet wrote works on it after the sinking. Charles Ives quoted the "Bethany" tune in his Symphony No. 4.
== meaning
The singer asks to be brought closer to God, whatever the way: even through suffering and hardship, as Jacob slept alone on a stone in the dark and dreamed of a stairway to heaven. Each verse ends by returning to that wish. The mood is quiet, trusting and devout, and it has often been sung in grief.
