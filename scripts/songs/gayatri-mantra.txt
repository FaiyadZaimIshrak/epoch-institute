== story
The Gāyatrī Mantra, also called the Sāvitrī Mantra, is a verse of the Rigveda (Mandala 3, hymn 62, verse 10) addressed to Savitr, a Vedic solar deity. It is attributed to the sage Viśvāmitra, who is also regarded as the author of the whole third book of the Rigveda. Its name comes from its metre, the gāyatrī, three lines of eight syllables; as preserved, its first line is one syllable short, and reciters either lengthen a sound or use a reconstructed form to fill it. In recitation the verse is preceded by the syllable oṃ and the three "great utterances", the mahāvyāhṛti, a practice set out in the Taittiriya Aranyaka. Gāyatrī is also the name of the goddess of the mantra and the metre.

The verse is cited throughout Hindu literature: in the Brahmanas and Srauta-sutras of the Vedic liturgy, in Upanishads such as the Brihadaranyaka, Shvetashvatara and Maitrayaniya, which treat it esoterically, and in the Mahabharata, the Harivamsa and the Manusmṛti. It served as the model for later "gāyatrīs" addressed to other gods and goddesses, built on the same pattern. The Buddha knew it: in the Pali canon he calls the Sāvittī the foremost of metres, and uses it as a sign of Brahmanic learning.

For centuries the mantra was bound to the upanayana, the ceremony that begins a young man's study of the Vedas; Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan called its imparting the essence of the rite, which is sometimes named "Gayatri diksha". Traditionally this verse was given only to Brahmins, with other verses for Kshatriyas and Vaishyas. Its repetition (japa) has been used as a form of atonement, and practitioners hold that it brings wisdom through the Sun.

Modern reform movements opened it to all. In 1827 Ram Mohan Roy published a dissertation on the mantra; from 1830 members of the Brahmo Samaj used it in private devotion, and the First Covenant of 1843 required it for worship. In 1898 Swami Vivekananda, arguing that one became a Brahmin by learning rather than by birth, gave the mantra to non-Brahmins in the Ramakrishna Mission, and Dayananda Saraswati, founder of the Arya Samaj, gave it a rendering of his own. In the later twentieth century the All World Gayatri Pariwar spread it further through gatherings large and small, and it has reached the wider public on pendants and recordings. In Indonesia it opens the Trisandhyā Puja, the prayer that Balinese and many other Indonesian Hindus say three times a day, at 6 a.m., noon and 6 p.m.

Western scholars translated it from the early nineteenth century, among them William Jones (1807), Monier Monier-Williams (1882) and Ralph T.H. Griffith (1896). It has since appeared in popular culture: engraved on the belt of the statue of George Harrison unveiled in Liverpool in 2015, in the opening theme of the television series Battlestar Galactica (2004), and sung by Cher on her tours from 2002.
== meaning
A Vedic prayer addressed to Savitr, the god who stirs and quickens all things through the sun's light. The worshippers ask to share in his radiance and that he awaken their minds and understanding. It is said as a daily act of devotion and as a seal of learning, in a mood of quiet reverence.
