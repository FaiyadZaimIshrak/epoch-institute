== story
"Misirlou" is a folk song of the Eastern Mediterranean, from the lands of the Ottoman Empire. Its original author is not known, but Arabic, Greek rebetiko and Jewish klezmer musicians were all playing it by the 1920s. The title is the Greek feminine form of Misirlis, from the Turkish Mısırlı, "Egyptian", so the song is about an Egyptian woman. The claim that the tune comes from "Bint Misr" by the Egyptian musician Sayed Darwish is thought to be incorrect. Because the melody runs up and down the double harmonic (Hijaz Kar) scale, it is easy to learn, and people from Morocco to Iraq claim it as a folk song of their own country.

The earliest known recording was made in 1927 for Columbia by Theodotos ("Tetos") Demetriades, an Ottoman Greek born in Istanbul in 1897 who had moved to the United States in 1921, when most of the Greek-speaking population fled the emerging Turkish state. He probably knew the song as a folk song before he left. As with almost all early rebetika, a style begun by Greek refugees from Asia Minor, the composer was never identified and ownership rested with the band leader. This rebetiko version was meant for the Greek tsifteteli dance, slower and in a different key than the "Oriental" versions known today. Michalis Patrinos recorded it in that style in Greece around 1930 and in New York City in 1931.

In 1941 the Greek-American music instructor Nick Roubanis released a jazz instrumental arrangement and credited himself as composer. The claim was never legally challenged, so he is still the official composer everywhere except Greece, where credit goes to Roubanis or Patrinos. Chaim Tauber, Fred Wise and Milton Leeds then wrote English lyrics, and the tune became an "exotica" standard of the light swing and lounge bands. Harry James's recording reached No. 22 in the United States in 1941, the pianist Jan August's No. 7 on the Billboard Jockey chart in 1946, and the Turkish-Jewish singer Darío Moreno recorded it in French in 1951.

In 1945 a dance group at Duquesne University in Pittsburgh, formed to honour America's allies in the Second World War, set the Cretan Syrtos dance to a slowed "Misirlou". First performed at Stephen Foster Memorial Hall on 6 March 1945, the new "Misirlou" dance spread through Greek-American communities and among American folk-dance enthusiasts, became a staple kolo at Serbian Orthodox church dances, and passed to Armenian Americans; Reuben Sarkisian recorded the first Armenian version, with his own Armenian words, in Fresno in the early 1950s.

In 1962 Dick Dale, whose father and uncles were Lebanese-American musicians, recast it as a fast solo rock guitar piece, remembering an uncle playing it on one string of the oud. Dale's surf version brought the tune to a wide audience and was followed by the Beach Boys, the Ventures, the Trashmen and exotica players such as Martin Denny and Arthur Lyman. It played over the opening titles of the film Pulp Fiction (1994) and was heavily used in the Black Eyed Peas' "Pump It" (2006). The Athens 2004 Olympic organisers chose it as one of the most influential Greek songs of all time, and Anna Vissi sang it at the closing ceremony.
== meaning
The title means "the Egyptian woman", and the song is about such a woman. Its best-known versions are instrumental, carried by a melody in the double harmonic scale that listeners across the Mediterranean and the Middle East hear as their own.
