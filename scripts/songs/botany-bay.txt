== story
"Botany Bay" can be traced back to Little Jack Sheppard, a musical burlesque staged at the Gaiety Theatre in London in 1885 and in Melbourne in 1886. The show was written by Henry Pottinger Stephens and William Yardley, with music composed and arranged by Wilhelm Meyer Lutz. Its programme credits "Botany Bay" as an "Old Air arr. Lutz". Sheet music from Allan & Co. in Australia instead credits Florian Pascal, the pseudonym of Joseph Williams Jr. (1847–1923), the music publisher and composer who published the show's music; Pascal wrote other numbers in the score but was not credited for this one in the programme.

The song's earlier history is less clear. Its verses share lines with "Farewell to Judges and Juries", performed in 1820. In 1890 the London paper The Era described the melody as written over a hundred years before, and it appears to have been adapted from the folk song "Mush, Mush". An older song called "Botany Bay", catalogued by the British Library as from the 1780s, has no obvious connection: it is about Cook's landing, not the convicts.

Botany Bay was the place chosen for the settlement of the First Fleet, which brought convicts to Australia in the 18th century. The song looks back on the late 18th and 19th centuries, when the British government transported convicts to the Australian penal colonies for seven-year terms as an alternative to prison in Britain. By the time it was written, transportation to New South Wales had already ended, in 1840, 45 years earlier.

After Little Jack Sheppard the song became a popular folk song, sung and recorded by Burl Ives and many others. It has no official words, and singers vary them slightly. In Australia it appears on compilations of children's songs, and documentaries on the transportation of convicts use it. It is sung in the film The Sundowners (1960) and by shearers in the television miniseries The Thorn Birds (1983).
== meaning
A song of a convict transported from England to the penal colony at Botany Bay. It tells of life on the convict ships and ends by warning young people at home not to steal. The mood is rueful, carried by a singalong refrain.
