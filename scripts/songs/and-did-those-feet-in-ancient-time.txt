== story
The words are a short poem by William Blake in the preface to his epic Milton: A Poem in Two Books. The title page bears the date 1804, probably when Blake began the plates, but the poem was printed around 1808. Blake wrote much of Milton while living in a cottage at Felpham in Sussex; local tradition at Lavant, near Chichester, holds instead that he wrote this poem in a public house there. The poem draws on an old story, repeated in Milton's History of Britain, that Joseph of Arimathea came to preach to the ancient Britons; the idea that Jesus himself visited England, often assumed to lie behind it, is according to the folklorist A. W. Smith not attested as an oral tradition before the early 20th century. The poem asks questions rather than asserting such a visit, and its last part is usually read as a call to build a just society in England. Its mills have been read as the factories of the early Industrial Revolution, perhaps the steam-powered Albion Flour Mills near Blake's home, burnt in 1791; as the established Church; or as part of Blake's own mythology. Blake supported the French Revolution and in 1803 was tried at Chichester for sedition over words spoken in conversation, and acquitted.

The poem was little known for a century. In 1916, as casualties of the First World War mounted, the Poet Laureate Robert Bridges included it in his anthology The Spirit of Man and asked Hubert Parry to set it for a meeting of the Fight for Right campaign at the Queen's Hall in London, as simple music an audience could join in. Parry, uneasy about the campaign's patriotism, agreed for the sake of Bridges and his former student Walford Davies, who was to conduct; he wrote the tune on 10 March 1916 and the first performance followed on 28 March. In May 1917 Parry withdrew his support from Fight for Right.

The song was saved for wider use by the women's suffrage movement. The National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies had taken it up in 1917, and Millicent Fawcett asked to use it at a Suffrage Demonstration Concert on 13 March 1918; Parry orchestrated it for the occasion and agreed that it should become the Women Voters' Hymn, assigning the copyright to the union. When the union was wound up in 1928, the copyright passed to the Women's Institutes, which still close meetings with it; it entered the public domain in 1968. The title "Jerusalem" appears to date from about 1918, though Parry always used the poem's first line. Edward Elgar re-scored it for very large orchestra for the Leeds Festival in 1922, and his version is the one usually heard at the Last Night of the Proms.

The song has been claimed across the political spectrum. Labour used it in the 1945 general election, when Clement Attlee promised to build a new Jerusalem, and it is sung at the close of Labour Party conferences as well as at Conservative and Liberal Democrat gatherings. King George V is said to have preferred it to "God Save the King". Some Church of England clergy hold that it is not strictly a hymn, since it is not a prayer to God, yet it is sung in many churches, on Saint George's Day, at the 2011 royal wedding in Westminster Abbey and in schools across Britain and beyond. It opened the 2012 London Olympics, topped a 2019 poll of the United Kingdom's favourite hymns, and is used as an English anthem by the England and Wales Cricket Board and, at times, by rugby league and the Commonwealth Games team; England has no official anthem. In 2020 a new arrangement by Errollyn Wallen, sung at the Last Night of the Proms, caused much controversy.
== meaning
The poem asks whether a holy presence once walked in ancient England and whether heaven was briefly built there, set against the grim industry of the poet's own day. It ends in a vow of spiritual struggle: not to rest until a just and holy city is built in England. Its mood moves from wonder to resolve.
