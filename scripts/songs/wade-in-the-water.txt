== story
"Wade in the Water" is an African American spiritual. Its words were first printed in 1901 in New Jubilee Songs as Sung by the Fisk Jubilee Singers, published by John Wesley Work Jr. and his brother Frederick J. Work. Work, also known as John Work II (1871–1925), spent three decades at Fisk University, the historically black college in Nashville, Tennessee, collecting the songs of the original Fisk Jubilee Singers. That student chorus, formed in 1871, had first toured to raise money for the university on 6 October 1871, now called Jubilee Day; by 1872 it sang at Boston's World Peace Festival and the White House, and in 1873 it toured Europe, bringing spirituals to a wide audience. Ella Sheppard, one of its singers, recalled that at first they would not sing the slave songs, which were sacred to their parents, who had worshipped and shouted over them, and only slowly came to value them in public.

The spirituals were the songs of people held in slavery. Frederick Douglass wrote that the songs he heard in slavery were "a testimony against slavery, and a prayer to God for deliverance from chains", and W. E. B. Du Bois, in The Souls of Black Folk (1903), called these "Sorrow Songs" the "singular spiritual heritage of the nation". After the original singers disbanded in 1878, Ella Sheppard Moore returned to Fisk in 1890 to train new singers, among them Work; his male quartet won wide acclaim and recorded for Victor, Edison, Columbia and Starr between 1909 and 1916. In 1925, the year Work died, Paramount released the first commercial recording of the song, by the Sunset Four Jubilee Singers. Later collections printed many versions, among them James W. Johnson's Book of American Negro Spirituals (1926) and Alan Lomax and Peggy Seeger's Folk Songs of North America (1960).

The song's water has been read in more than one way. Writers at George Washington University in 2002 connected it to the Israelites' escape from Egypt in Exodus. Others link its image of God stirring the water to the Gospel of John, chapter 5, where a sick man waits beside the pool of Bethesda to be healed when its water is troubled. The minister and theologian Howard Thurman took that story as the song's source: within the troubled waters of life, he wrote, there are healing waters, because God is in the midst of the turmoil.

The song is also linked with the Underground Railroad, the network of secret routes and safe houses by which enslaved people escaped to freedom. The PBS Newshour put it this way: it "has not been proven", but "it is believed" to have been one of its songs. It is believed that Harriet Tubman, who made thirteen trips to the South and helped free more than 70 people, used it to warn people escaping to leave the trail and go into the water, so that the slave catchers' dogs could not follow their scent; a 2002 Maryland Public Television teaching guide repeats this account. The musicologist Arthur C. Jones, who founded the Spirituals Project at the University of Denver in 1998, described Tubman and others improvising on existing spirituals and using them in secret in the struggle for freedom.

In the 20th century the song was sung again in a new struggle. It was included in the 1968 compilation Freedom Is a Constant Struggle: Songs of the Freedom Movement, and the Staple Singers' version became part of the civil rights movement; Fannie Lou Hamer also sang it. Alvin Ailey set part of his 1960 ballet Revelations to it, and the Ramsey Lewis Trio's 1966 instrumental reached number 19 on the Hot 100. Sweet Honey in the Rock, Eva Cassidy and many others have recorded it since.
== meaning
A spiritual that calls on the listener to wade into the water, because God is stirring it. It draws on the crossing out of Egypt and on the healing pool of Bethesda, and has been read as a call to trust God through the ups and downs of life and as a hope of coming freedom. The mood is steady, urgent and hopeful.
