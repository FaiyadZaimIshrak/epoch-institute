== story
"The Wearing of the Green" is an Irish street ballad lamenting the repression of the supporters of the Irish Rebellion of 1798. The revolutionary Society of United Irishmen had taken green as its colour, and its supporters wore green garments, ribbons or cockades; in some versions the green is shamrock rather than cloth. The song proclaims that men and women are being hanged for wearing it.

Many versions exist, sung to an old Irish air. In the usual form the narrator is a rebel who has gone into exile and meets a public figure, most often Napper Tandy, who asks for news of Ireland and is told that those who wear green are persecuted. Henry Halliday Sparling's Irish Minstrelsy (1888) prints the anonymous "Green upon the Cape", dated to 1798, a longer poem in which a croppy from Belfast travels into exile and is questioned in Paris by "Boney", Napoleon Bonaparte. In "Green on my Cape", published in Dundalk in 1802, it is Robert Emmet who meets the narrator, at Brest, and versions of the 1840s and 1850s bring in Napoleon. Henry Grattan Curran (1800–76), son of John Philpot Curran, wrote a version of his own and claimed that the original came from County Tipperary.

The best-known words are Dion Boucicault's, written for his play Arragh na Pogue, or the Wicklow Wedding (1864), set in County Wicklow during the rebellion. Boucicault said he had based them on a half-remembered Dublin street ballad. His second verse tells of the singer's meeting with Napper Tandy, the rebel leader exiled in France, and the third and last verses he added, in contrast to the defiance of the middle verse, advise emigration to America; Boucicault himself fled to New York after leaving his wife for a young actress. The tune was first printed in The Citizen, or Dublin Monthly Magazine in 1841, and a variant appeared as "Up! For the Green" in James Duffy's The Spirit of the Nation (Dublin, 1845); others are in Alfred Moffat's Minstrelsy of Ireland (1897) and Francis O'Neill's Music of Ireland (Chicago, 1903).

The air carried other words. "The Rising of the Moon" tells of the same rising of 1798, "The Orange and the Green" of a marriage between Protestant and Catholic, and in 1865, at the end of the American Civil War, "The Wearing of the Grey" lamented the Confederate army, while "The Army of the Free" was published to the same tune. Wellington Guernsey made a version for voice and piano in 1866, Charles Villiers Stanford set new words by Alfred Perceval Graves in 1900, and Joseph O'Kelly wrote piano variations on it, his Air irlandais of 1877. Gerald O'Hara sings it in Margaret Mitchell's Gone with the Wind, and John McCormack (1904 and 1912) and Judy Garland (1940) recorded it; in 1962 Allan Sherman turned it into the comic "Shake Hands With Your Uncle Max".
== meaning
An exile asks for news of Ireland and hears that those who wear the green of the rebels of 1798 are persecuted, and that men and women are hanged for it. A lament for a crushed rising and a song of defiance, it is mournful and bitter; in Boucicault's version it ends by turning to emigration across the sea.
