== story
Peter Dodds McCormick (1833–1916), a Scottish-born composer living in Sydney, wrote both words and music. In a letter of 1 August 1913 to R. B. Fuller he recalled that, after a concert at Sydney's Exhibition Building at which the anthems of many nations were played, he was annoyed that there was nothing for Australia; he made up the first verse on the bus home and set it to music when he got home. The song was first sung by Andrew Fairfax, with a concert band conducted by McCormick, at a function of the Highland Society of New South Wales in Sydney on Saint Andrew's Day, 30 November 1878, and was published in early December 1878 under the pen-name "Amicus". The original had four verses. An amended version was sung by a choir of about 10,000 at the inauguration of the Commonwealth of Australia on 1 January 1901, and in 1907 the Australian Government paid McCormick £100 for it.

Before it became the anthem the song had wide use. The earliest known recording is in The Landing of the Australian Troops in Egypt (c. 1916), a dramatised record of Australian troops on their way to Gallipoli. The Australian Broadcasting Commission used it to introduce its radio news from 1942 to 1952, and towards the end of the Second World War some cinemas played it alongside "God Save the King" and "The Star-Spangled Banner".

As Australia looked for a national identity of its own after the Second World War, support for a distinct anthem grew: a Gallup poll found 72% in favour in 1972, against 38% in 1968. The Whitlam government held a competition in 1973 that produced no music and a shortlist of lyrics that critics derided, and then an opinion poll of about 60,000 people that offered only "Advance Australia Fair", "Waltzing Matilda" and "Song of Australia". "Advance Australia Fair" won 51.4%, and on 9 April 1974 Whitlam told parliament it was the national anthem for all but regal occasions. Officials in four states refused to use it. On 22 January 1976 the Fraser government restored "God Save the Queen" as the anthem and allowed any of four songs at civilian functions. In a plebiscite held with the 1977 referendum, "Advance Australia Fair" won 43.29% as the "national song", ahead of "Waltzing Matilda" (28.28%), "God Save the Queen" (18.78%) and "Song of Australia" (9.65%).

The Hawke government proclaimed "Advance Australia Fair" the national anthem with effect from 19 April 1984; "God Save the Queen" became the royal anthem, played when the monarch or members of the royal family attend. The official words, modified by the National Australia Day Council, cut the song to two verses: the first verse of 1878, made gender-neutral in its first line, and a reworked version of a verse first sung at Federation in 1901. The orchestral arrangement most often heard, at medal ceremonies and major events, is by the Hungarian-born composer Thomas Tycho, commissioned by ABC Music in 1984 and first televised on Australia Day 1986. McCormick's own copyright has expired, but the Commonwealth claims copyright on the official lyrics and particular arrangements and requires permission for commercial use.

The anthem has been criticised as dull and archaic in its wording, and, with growing debate since about 2015, for leaving out Australia's Indigenous peoples and celebrating British colonisation. The boxer Anthony Mundine said in 2013, 2017 and 2018 that he would not stand for it; rugby league players declined to sing it in 2019; the soprano Deborah Cheetham refused to sing it at the 2015 AFL Grand Final when her request to change a line was turned down. Alternative words were proposed by Judith Durham and Kutcha Edwards (2009) and by Peter Vickery's Recognition in Anthem Project (2017), which Bob Hawke endorsed in 2018. In November 2020 the New South Wales premier Gladys Berejiklian proposed changing one word; Prime Minister Scott Morrison announced the change in December 2020, and from 1 January 2021 the word "young" in the second line became "one", in recognition of the long presence of Indigenous Australians on the continent. Versions in the Dharug language have also been written and sung, by Jacqueline Troy (2003), Richard Green (2010) and others.
== meaning
A patriotic song that praises Australia's land, its freedom and its prospects and calls on its people to rejoice together. The tone is formal and celebratory.
