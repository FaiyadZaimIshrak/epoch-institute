== story
"Shosholoza" is a traditional Nguni work song of the mine workers of southern Africa, in a mix of Ndebele and Zulu words into which singers bring other South African languages. It was first sung by groups of Ndebele men who travelled by steam train from their homes in Zimbabwe, then Rhodesia, to work in South Africa's diamond and gold mines; the Ndebele live mainly in Zimbabwe, near its border with South Africa, and the song is Zimbabwean in origin. Some hold that it describes the journey to the mines, others the journey home.

The song belonged to men doing hard, rhythmical manual labour. It was sung in call and response, one man singing a line and the group answering by copying him, and miners sang it in time with the swing of their axes as they dug. According to the researchers Booth and Nauright, Zulu workers later took it up to keep the rhythm of group tasks and to ease boredom and strain. It expressed the hardship and heartache of work in the mines, and its title word, meaning "go forward" or "make way for the next man" in Ndebele, served as encouragement and a sign of solidarity among the workers. The repeated "sho sho" imitates the sound of the steam train, the stimela.

Prisoners also sang it, dividing the parts between rows. Nelson Mandela described singing "Shosholoza" while he worked during his imprisonment on Robben Island, calling it a song that compares the struggle against apartheid to the motion of an oncoming train, and saying that the singing made the work lighter.

The song was featured in the musical King Kong (1959), whose London production followed in 1961, and Pete Seeger recorded it on his album We Shall Overcome. It became a standard of gumboot dance bands and a campfire song of South African scouts. After South Africa won the 1995 Rugby World Cup it became a favourite at sporting events; a recording sung by the radio host Dan Moyane, produced by Stuart Lee, was made and released in five days for the tournament's first game and went gold. The South African football team sang it as they came onto the field to open the 2010 FIFA World Cup.

The song is now so widely sung that it is often called South Africa's second national anthem, used at sporting and national events to tell players they are not alone. Its meaning has widened to support for any struggle: climate activists made it the centrepiece of their Occupy COP17 rally on 9 December 2011, the last day of the United Nations climate negotiations, calling on negotiators to "Stand With Africa". The first African challengers for the America's Cup, Team Shosholoza, and the long-distance passenger train service Shosholoza Meyl took their names from it.
== meaning
A song of men at hard labour far from home, carried by the image of the steam train that brought them to the mines. Sung in call and response, it urges the group forward together and sets the rhythm of the work, so that it voices both the weariness of the labour and the strength of solidarity. Later singers heard in it a call to keep going through any struggle.
