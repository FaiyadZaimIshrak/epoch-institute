== story
In 1959 the Belgian government called a Belgo-Congolese Round Table Conference in Brussels to discuss the political future of the Belgian Congo. Thomas and Philippe Kanza, sons of the Congolese delegate Daniel Kanza, proposed bringing Congolese musicians to entertain the delegates, and late in 1959 Thomas invited Joseph Kabasele, known on stage as Le Grand Kallé and leader of African Jazz, and Victor Longomba, leader of the rival band OK Jazz. Franco Luambo of OK Jazz could not come because of earlier engagements. Longomba and the OK Jazz guitarist Armando Brazzos joined Kabasele and his musicians Nico Kasanda, Déchaud Mwamba and Roger Izeidi, with the conga drummer Pierre Yatula, all playing as African Jazz. Kabasele also took part in the conference's discussions.

Kabasele wrote "Indépendance Cha Cha" on 20 January 1960, in the Congolese rumba style, drawing on instruments and rhythms of the music of the African diaspora, particularly Cuba. It was first played at the Hotel Plaza in Brussels on 1 February 1960, sung by Longomba with Kasanda on guitar. The group recorded it during the conference, together with Kabasele's "Table Ronde" and Longomba's "Vive Patrice Lumumba", in a studio linked to His Master's Voice. That label was not interested, and Kabasele took the masters to other companies until Fonior agreed to release them.

The song called for unity in the independent Congo among the factions and leaders of the nationalist movement, and gave the credit for independence to the Congolese people. It was sung in Lingala with French loanwords, so it was also a hit in the neighbouring French Congo, and Radio Congo Belge carried it across Africa. In 1960, the "Year of Africa", when many countries, most of them French-speaking, became independent or were about to, its tune and optimism matched the popular mood, and it has been called the first Pan-African hit. When Rwanda became independent on 1 July 1962, a youth group marched through Kigali singing it.

Its longest success has been in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The musician Gilles Sala said the three Brussels recordings "sent a musical shock wave". The historian David Van Reybrouck wrote that although "Debout Congolais" and "La Zaïroise" each served officially as the state's anthem, since independence the country has had only one true national anthem: this song. It remains the best-known example of Congolese rumba abroad.
== meaning
A song celebrating independence and calling the new nation's leaders and parties to stand together; joyful and light, made for dancing.
