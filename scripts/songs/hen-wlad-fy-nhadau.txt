== story
"Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau" ("The Old Land of My Fathers", usually rendered "Land of My Fathers") was written in January 1856 by Evan James, with a tune by his son James James, both of Pontypridd in Glamorgan; the earliest written copy survives in the National Library of Wales. Known at first as "Glan Rhondda" ("Banks of the Rhondda"), it was first sung by Elizabeth John of Pontypridd in the vestry of the original Capel Tabor in Maesteg in January or February 1856, and it soon became popular in the district. James James was a harpist who played in the public house he ran, for dancing; the song was first meant to go in 6/8 time and had to be slowed to its present 3/4 when large crowds began to sing it.

Its fame grew after the Llangollen Eisteddfod of 1858, where Thomas Llewelyn of Aberdare won a competition for an unpublished collection of Welsh airs with a collection that included "Glan Rhondda". The adjudicator, John Owen ("Owain Alaw", 1821–1883), asked to include it in his Gems of Welsh Melody (1860–1864), which gave it the title "Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau" and sold in large numbers across Wales. At the Bangor Eisteddfod of 1874 it was sung by Robert Rees ("Eos Morlais"), one of the leading Welsh soloists of the day; it was sung more and more at patriotic gatherings and gradually became a national anthem. On 11 March 1899 Madge Breese recorded it for the Gramophone Company, in the first recording made in the Welsh language.

It was the first national anthem sung at the start of a sporting event. In 1905 Wales hosted New Zealand's first touring rugby team, who began every match with a haka; in reply, the Wales player Teddy Morgan led the crowd in singing the anthem. For the next decades it was sung with "God Bless the Prince of Wales" and "God Save the Queen" before matches, until sports officials decided in 1975 that it should be sung alone. Like the other British anthems it has no status in law, but since the 1970s it has been used at official ceremonies, including the opening of the Welsh Parliament (Senedd Cymru), and at receptions of the British monarch; a petition to make it the official anthem was debated in 2014 and judged "not currently a possible development". Usually only the first stanza and the chorus are sung, in Welsh, and "God Save the King" is sometimes played beside it at events with a royal connection.

Not knowing the words has been held against politicians: in 1993 the new Secretary of State for Wales, John Redwood, was filmed failing to mime them, and according to John Major's autobiography his successor William Hague at once looked for someone to teach him; he found Ffion Jenkins, whom he later married. In 1978 Geraint Jarman a'r Cynganeddwyr recorded a version with electric guitars, played by Tich Gwilym and inspired by Jimi Hendrix's "The Star-Spangled Banner". The words "Pleidiol wyf i'm gwlad" ("I am devoted to my country"), from the refrain, were inscribed on the edge of the Welsh £1 coins of 1985, 1990, 1995 and 2000 and form the motto of the Royal Badge of Wales adopted in 2008. The tune was adapted for the anthems of Cornwall ("Bro Goth agan Tasow") and Brittany ("Bro Gozh ma Zadoù"), and for "Gwlad Newydd y Cymry" ("The New Country of the Welsh"), written by Lewis Evans, who emigrated to Y Wladfa, the Welsh-speaking settlement in Patagonia.
== meaning
A song of love for Wales, the old land of the singer's fathers: it honours the country's poets and singers and the patriots who gave their lives for it, praises its mountains and streams, and in the chorus pledges devotion to the land and the hope that its ancient language will endure. The mood is proud and tender.
