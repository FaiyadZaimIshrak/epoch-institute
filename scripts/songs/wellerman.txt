== story
"Soon May the Wellerman Come", also known as "Wellerman" or "The Wellerman", is a New Zealand folk song in ballad style. Its title refers to the "wellermen", the supply ships of the Weller brothers and the men who worked for them. Edward, George and Joseph Weller were British-born merchants who had emigrated to Sydney in 1829. In 1831 they founded a whaling station at Otago Heads, near present-day Dunedin in the South Island, seventeen years before Dunedin was established, and from 1833 they sold provisions to whalers from their base at Otakou, which they named "Otago".

New Zealand whalers worked from the shore, processing the carcasses on land rather than at sea. At its peak in 1834 the Otakou station produced 310 tons of whale oil a year and stood at the centre of seven stations, employing as many as 85 people at Otago alone; from it the Wellers also traded in timber, spars, flax, potatoes, dried fish and Māori artefacts, among them tattooed Māori heads, which were in demand in Sydney. Their stations stretched from Akaroa on Banks Peninsula to Stewart Island and drew competitors such as Johnny Jones. The boom was short: overhunting emptied the local whale grounds, and whaling ships from Sydney are thought to have brought an epidemic to the region. Joseph Weller died of tuberculosis in Otago in 1835, the brothers were declared bankrupt in 1840, the Otakou station closed in 1841, and in the same year a Court of Claims in New South Wales ruled their land purchases in New Zealand invalid. Industrial whaling in New Zealand went on until the 1960s, with the southern right whale (tohorā) and the humpback (paikea) among the species most reduced.

The origin of the song is unknown. In 1966 the collector Neil Colquhoun took it down from a man then in his eighties, who said his uncle had taught it to him, and in 1973 Colquhoun printed it in his book New Zealand Folksongs: Songs of a Young Country. More than ten recordings followed between 1971 and 2005, among them one by the New England folk trio of Gordon Bok, Ann Mayo Muir and Ed Trickett in 1990, and one by the Wellington Sea Shanty Society in 2013. The Bristol a cappella group the Longest Johns recorded a well-known version on their album Between Wind and Water in 2018.

In early 2021 a cover by the Scottish singer Nathan Evans became a viral hit on TikTok and set off a craze for sea shanties and sea songs, nicknamed "ShantyTok", during the lockdowns of the COVID-19 pandemic; commentators saw in its stoic patience something that young people marking time at home shared with the whalers. A remix by Evans with 220 Kid and Billen Ted reached number one on the UK Singles Chart. Though popularised as a shanty, the song is more exactly a ballad, a forebitter or sea song, sung for its own sake rather than to time a task. It was covered and parodied many times; Evans performed it on Blue Peter and on Saturday Night Takeaway, with a guitar solo by Brian May. The Weller brothers' whale lookout, Weller's Rock (Te Umukuri), is now a protected historic reserve.
== meaning
A whaling crew's song: a ship's company chases a right whale and waits for the supply boat to bring them comforts, and the hunt drags on through lost boats and passing time without ending. The mood is steady and patient, a song of endurance through a long, hard job.
