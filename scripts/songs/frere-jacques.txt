== story
"Frère Jacques", known in English as "Brother John", is a nursery rhyme of French origin, traditionally sung as a round. It is about a friar who has overslept and is urged to wake and ring the bell for matins, the night or early-morning prayers. The common English version keeps the rhythm but changes the meaning, so that the bells wake Brother John. The French frère, "brother", is the root of the English word friar.

The oldest known version of the tune is in a French manuscript of vaudeville tunes in the Bibliothèque nationale in Paris, dated to between 1775 and 1785, where it is called "Frère Blaise". According to the sheet-music collector James Fuld, the tune was first published in 1811; an 1825 publication gave the words with the melody described in solfège, and words and music were printed together in Paris in 1869. They also appear together in Charles Lebouc's popular children's book Récréations de l'enfance, first published in 1860. The musicologist Sylvie Bouissou found a manuscript in the French National Library that lists "Frère Jacques" among 86 canons under the name of Jean-Philippe Rameau, some evidence that he wrote the music. The tune resembles Girolamo Frescobaldi's Capriccio Fra Jacopino, published around 1615, as well as a French song of 1650 and a Hungarian folk tune.

Who the friar was is not known. A link to the 17th-century lithotomist Frère Jacques Beaulieu has often been claimed, but a study found no evidence for it. Others have suggested that the song mocked the Dominican friars, called Jacobins in France, for idleness and comfort. The satirical Russian pseudonym Kozma Prutkov claimed it came from a Russian seminary song.

The round is one of the simplest repeating canons, and that simplicity helped it travel. In China its tune is so familiar that it is widely taken for a Chinese folk song, "Two Tigers", and children in China, Vietnam and elsewhere in Asia sing their own words to it. In 1926 officers of the Whampoa Military Academy set to it the patriotic song "Revolution of the Citizens", associated with the First United Front between the Kuomintang and the Chinese Communist Party, and demonstrators in Tiananmen Square chanted political slogans to it. Gustav Mahler used it, in a minor key and under its German name "Bruder Martin", in the third movement of his Symphony No. 1; Leonard Bernstein used it to teach counterpoint on television; and the Beatles sang its title under the last verse of "Paperback Writer" (1966).
== meaning
A short, teasing wake-up song: a friar who should be calling his house to early prayers has overslept, and the singers rouse him. Sung as a round, the overlapping voices sound like a peal of bells.
