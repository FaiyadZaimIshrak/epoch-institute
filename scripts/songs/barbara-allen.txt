== story
"Barbara Allen" (Child 84, Roud 54) is a traditional ballad that began in the 17th century or earlier. The earliest surviving mention is in the diary of Samuel Pepys for 2 January 1666, who at a New Year's party delighted in hearing the actress and singer Elizabeth Knepp sing "her little Scotch song of Barbary Allen". The folklorists Steve Roud and Julia Bishop inferred that it was already popular and perhaps written for the stage; Phillips Barry and Fannie Hardy Eckstorm thought it was instead a libel on Barbara Villiers, mistress of Charles II, and Charles Seeger noted that Pepys would have relished such a song.

It spread quickly, both by word of mouth and in print, through Britain and Ireland and later North America. A London broadside of about 1690, "Barbara Allen's cruelty: or, the young-man's tragedy", is the earliest text. Printings were common through the 18th century: Allan Ramsay published "Bonny Barbara Allen" in his Tea-Table Miscellany (1740), and Thomas Percy two versions in his Reliques of Ancient English Poetry (1765). In the 19th century Francis James Child combined these and versions from the Roxburghe Ballads into the standard texts used by later scholars. In 1792 Joseph Haydn arranged it, one of more than 400 folk-song arrangements commissioned by George Thomson and the publishers William Napier and William Whyte, probably taking the melody from James Oswald's Caledonian Pocket Companion of about 1750. The ballad was first printed in the United States in 1836 and went on appearing on broadsides, in songbooks and in newspaper columns there.

Because it was so often printed, its words vary less than those of most folk songs: broadsides kept "correcting" the oral versions. Some details still change. It may be set in "Scarlet Town" (perhaps a pun on Reading), London or Dublin, and may open at Martinmas, Lammas or in May; the Martinmas versions, most common in Scotland, are thought to be older. The dying man is Sir John Graeme in the earliest prints and often William, James or Jimmy in America. The folklorist Harry Smith used to ask people to sing a verse and then tell them which county they were born in. The tunes, by contrast, were rarely printed and passed from singer to singer: many American tunes are pentatonic, English ones mostly major, and the minor Scottish tune in Oswald's collection seems to be the oldest.

Roud and Bishop called it "far and away the most widely collected song in the English language", and the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library counts about 500 traditional recordings. The earliest is probably Percy Grainger's wax cylinder of the Lincolnshire singer Joseph Taylor in 1907. Others include Hule "Queen" Hines of Florida (1939), Phil Tanner (1949), Elizabeth Cronin (early 1950s), Sam Larner (1958) and Jean Ritchie (1961). Charles Seeger edited a Library of Congress record of 30 American versions recorded between 1933 and 1954.

Roger Quilter arranged it in 1921, and in the folk revival of the 1950s and 1960s it was recorded by Pete Seeger, Eddy Arnold, the Everly Brothers, Joan Baez, Bob Dylan, and Simon and Garfunkel, among others. Dylan wrote that without "Barbara Allen" there would be no "Girl from the North Country". It has also been retold in a ghost story by Robert E. Howard, the 1942 play Dark of the Moon and radio dramas of 1952 and 2009.
== meaning
A servant fetches Barbara Allen to the bedside of a young man dying for love of her; she turns him away, recalling a slight, and after his death she is overcome with grief and dies too. Buried in the same churchyard, they are joined at last by a rose and a briar that grow from their graves and twine into a lovers' knot. The mood is sorrowful and full of regret.
