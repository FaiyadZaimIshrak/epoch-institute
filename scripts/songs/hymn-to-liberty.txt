== story
Dionysios Solomos wrote the "Hymn to Liberty", also called the "Hymn to Freedom", on Zakynthos in 1823, inspired by the Greek War of Independence, to honour the Greeks' struggle for independence after centuries of Ottoman rule. The poem runs to 158 stanzas. It was printed in Messolonghi the following year, and in October 1824 the Philhellenic Committee published it in London, while an Italian translation appeared at about the same time in the Messolonghi newspaper Ellinika Chronika.

In 1828 the Corfiot operatic composer Nikolaos Mantzaros set it to music as a choral work in 24 parts and dedicated it to Otto, the first King of Greece. Otto awarded him the Silver Cross of the Order of the Redeemer, but during Otto's reign (1832–1862) the anthem in use was one based on "God Save the King", with words glorifying the king.

After Otto was ousted in 1862, the "Hymn to Liberty" was adopted in 1864 as the national and royal anthem of Greece, its first two stanzas forming the anthem. It was the royal anthem from 1864 to 1924 and again from 1935 to 1973, ending each time with the abolition of the monarchy. An adapted version served as the anthem of the short-lived Cretan State.

In Cyprus, an anthem written for the Republic by the Cypriot composer Solon Michaelides and shared with the Turkish Cypriots was abandoned in 1963 during intercommunal conflicts, and in 1966 the Council of Ministers adopted the "Hymn to Liberty" as the national anthem of Cyprus. The anthem is performed at every closing ceremony of the Olympic Games in honour of Greece as the birthplace of the Games, usually without words; it was sung at Sydney, Athens and Vancouver. Military bands play an arrangement by Lieutenant Colonel Margaritis Kastellis (1907–1979), a former director of the Greek Music Corps.
== meaning
The poem tells of the Greeks' suffering under Ottoman rule and their hope of freedom, and follows events of the War of Independence: the execution of Patriarch Gregory V, the response of the Great Powers, at length the Siege of Tripolitsa, and the Christian character of the struggle. The mood is exalted and martial.
