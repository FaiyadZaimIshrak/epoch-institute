== story
The Nāsadīya Sūkta, also called the Hymn of Creation, is the 129th hymn of the 10th book (mandala) of the Rigveda (10.129). Its name comes from its opening words. It is concerned with cosmology and the origin of the universe. Like the 10th book as a whole, it is late within the Rigveda, and it expresses thought more typical of later Vedantic philosophy. Although it is unlike most Vedic hymns, it is one of the most widely received parts of the Rigveda, and it has drawn a large literature of commentary both in the Indian schools of philosophy (darśanas) and in Western philology.

The hymn asks when, why and through whom the universe came into being, and gives no definite answer. It suggests that the gods themselves may not know, since they came after creation, and that even the one who watches over creation from the highest heaven may or may not know. For this reason the usual English title is perhaps misleading: the hymn does not tell a creation myth, but leads the listener to ask whether anyone can know how the universe began. As it unfolds, it moves from a state before both being and non-being towards being, naming heat (tapas) and desire (kāma) among the first causes, and the first poet-seers (kavayas) as those who sought the origin in their own thought.

It is composed in seven stanzas in the trishtubh metre, but one line of the last stanza is two syllables short, and the final line ends in a clause left without a main clause. The scholar Brereton (1999) argued that both are deliberate, a way for the seer to express puzzlement at the thought that the world may not have been created, and that the hymn's slow passage from non-being to being re-enacts creation within the listener, equating poetic speech with creation. Karel Werner described its source as not reasoning but "visionary, mystical or Yogic experience put into words".

An atheist reading sees the hymn as one of the earliest accounts of sceptical inquiry and agnosticism. The astronomer Carl Sagan quoted it when he discussed India's "tradition of skeptical questioning and unselfconscious humility before the great cosmic mysteries."
== meaning
A meditation on the beginning of the universe. It pictures the state before anything existed, traces how being may have arisen, and ends by doubting whether even the gods, or the highest overseer, know how it happened. The mood is quiet, questioning and humble.
