== story
"Farewell of Slavianka" is a Russian patriotic march written by the composer Vasily Agapkin in honour of Slavic women seeing their husbands off to the First Balkan War. Slavianka means "Slavic woman". It was written and first played in Tambov in late 1912, published by Zimmerman around that year, and issued as a gramophone record in Kiev in the summer of 1915.

The march became popular in Russia and its neighbours during the First World War, when Russian soldiers left home to its music. In the Russian Civil War it served as an unofficial anthem of Admiral Kolchak's White Army, while its earliest known performance by communist troops dates from 1918. It appeared in a Soviet publication in 1929 and in an official collection of music for Red Army bands, and in the early 1940s a military orchestra under Ivan Petrov recorded it. On 7 November 1941 it was one of four marches played at the October Revolution Parade on Red Square, from which soldiers went straight to the Battle of Moscow. Claims that it was banned before it was heard in the 1957 film The Cranes Are Flying are contradicted by the many earlier performances, several conducted by Agapkin himself.

Many sets of words have been written for the tune. In the Finnish Civil War the Red Guards adapted it as the workers' marching song "Vapaa Venäjä". In German-occupied Poland during the Second World War a version with words by Roman Ślęzak, "Rozszumiały się wierzby płaczące" (Weeping Willows Began to Hum), spread in the Polish resistance. In 1945 Haim Hefer wrote the Hebrew "Ben Gvulot" (Between Borders) for the Palmach; a phrase he coined in it was used by the Israel Defense Forces in 2002 to name Operation Defensive Shield. The Soviet Union first gave the march official words fitted to its politics, removing references to Russian culture and religion; a version of 1941 was followed by one of 1967 by A. Fedotov, which mentions the Battle of Berlin. Vladimir Lazarev's words of 1984, slower and more fragile in tone, became popular after the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991; other versions came with the constitutional crisis of 1993 and, after 1991, a White Army version by Andrei Mingalyov. In 2002 the melody became the regional anthem of Tambov Oblast, with words by A. Mitrofanov, and in 2026, during Russia's war in Ukraine, which Russia calls a "special military operation", Andrey Kandaurov wrote new hymn-like words for it.

In the 1990s the liberal party Yabloko proposed it, without success, as Russia's national anthem. It is heard in films including The Cranes Are Flying (1957), At Home Among Strangers (1974), the Ukrainian Raspad (1990, in its scene of the evacuation of Pripyat), Prisoner of the Mountains, 72 Meters and Charlie Wilson's War. The Red Army Choir sings it today with later words.
== meaning
A march of soldiers departing for war and the women who see them off; stirring and sad at once.
