== story
The "Wilhelmus" was written at the start of the Eighty Years' War, the Dutch Revolt against the Spanish Empire, at some time between April 1568 and the capture of Brielle on 1 April 1572, an event it does not mention. It is first attested in 1573, sung during the siege of Haarlem, and in a German translation of the same year; the oldest complete Dutch text is in a Geuzenliedboek (Beggars' songbook) printed in 1577–1578. Its author is not known. Philips of Marnix and Dirck Coornhert were named early on, but neither ever claimed it, and its awkward rhymes make some doubt that either experienced poet wrote it; recent stylometric research has pointed to Pieter Datheen. The tune was borrowed from a Catholic French song mocking the Huguenot prince Louis of Condé's failed siege of Chartres in 1568, so a satire of one Protestant prince became a tribute to another.

The song is unusual among anthems in being a first-person monologue of William of Orange, the leader of the revolt. It sets out his struggle to stay loyal to Philip II of Spain without betraying his conscience, God and the people, placing the blame on the king's emissaries such as the Duke of Alba. The first letters of its fifteen stanzas spell "Willem van Nassov", and the stanzas mirror one another around the eighth, which compares William to David fleeing Saul. In 1581 the Netherlands rejected the Spanish king's rule in the Act of Abjuration.

For two centuries the song served as a military and political signal. Spanish musicians played it mockingly at the siege of Alkmaar in 1573; it was played at William's entry into Brussels in 1577 and after the liberation of Groningen in 1594. Ships of the Dutch East India Company sounded it before engagements in colonial wars, and in the Great Ambon War of 1655 a trumpeter played it to signal the assault. In the disaster year 1672 crowds sang it to press the regents to make William III stadholder. Adriaen Valerius published a slow, devotional version in 1626; in the 18th century it became a fast instrumental "Princes' March" of the Orangists, which the ten-year-old Mozart set in seven variations at The Hague in 1766. After the Batavian Revolution it was banned as a symbol of the House of Orange.

The new kingdom chose another anthem, "Wien Neêrlands Bloed", adopted in 1817. But the "Wilhelmus" came back, helped by the republication of Valerius's melody in 1871 and Eduard Kremser's solemn arrangement of 1877; Queen Wilhelmina had it played at her inauguration in 1898, and on 10 May 1932 it became the official national anthem. Under the German occupation crowds sang it in demonstrations in June 1940, the authorities banned royal symbols, and the whole resistance took it up, while the collaborating NSB dropped it. In the Dutch East Indies it was also a song of colonial rule: political prisoners who refused to sing it were sent to the Boven-Digoel camp, where a protest version was made to its tune, and Indonesian nationalists chose "Indonesia Raya" as their anthem as early as 1928.

Today the first stanza, sometimes followed by the sixth, is played at state ceremonies, Remembrance of the Dead, Liberation Day, King's Day, inaugurations and sporting events. Flemish nationalist rallies such as the IJzerbedevaart close with its sixth stanza.
== meaning
William of Orange speaks in his own voice: he declares his descent, his faith in God and his loyalty to the king, and explains that conscience has forced him into revolt against the king's harsh governors. He compares himself with the biblical David, comforts his followers in exile and defeat, and asks God for the strength to drive out tyranny. Its tone is that of a psalm as much as a war song.
