== story
The Lament for Ur, or Lamentation over the city of Ur, is a Sumerian lament composed around the time when Ur fell to the Elamites and its Third Dynasty came to an end, about 2000 BC. The dynasty had ruled for around a hundred years. Invading Elamites raided the temple treasury, the centre of power in Sumer moved to Isin, and control of Ur's trade passed to several leading families of the city. The lament is one of five known Mesopotamian "city laments", dirges for ruined cities sung in the voice of the city's protective goddess; the others mourn Sumer and Ur together, Nippur, Eridu and Uruk.

The text runs to 438 lines in eleven sections, called kirugu, in stanzas of six lines. At its heart the goddess Ningal, wife of the moon god Nanna, weeps for her city and recalls how she pleaded with the great gods An and Enlil, in private and before the assembly of the gods, not to let Ur be destroyed. Other sections, perhaps of different origin, describe the ghost town Ur has become, the wrath of Enlil's storm, and the gods leaving their temples across the land, and ask the god Nanna to protect the city from future disasters. The invasion is pictured as a rising flood and a raging storm, fitting Enlil's title as god of the winds. The text names the shrines destroyed and describes in detail the ruin of the E-kiš-nu-ĝal, which the people of Šimaški and Elam broke up with axes and pickaxes. It ends in praise of Enlil and his consort Ninlil.

According to Edward L. Greenstein, the speakers of such laments were generally male lamentation-priests, who took on the part of a traditional female mourner and asked that the gods be appeased so that the temples could be restored.

The text survives on clay tablets scattered among many collections. Its first lines were found among tablets from the temple library at Nippur in the University of Pennsylvania Museum; George Barton translated them in 1918 as "A prayer for the city of Ur", noting that it was plausible but unproven that the prayer was written in the last days of Ibbi-Sin, as Ur was falling. Edward Chiera published further tablets in 1924 and 1934, and others were found in the Louvre, the Ashmolean in Oxford, the Hilprecht collection at Jena, Geneva, Berlin, Yale, the Nippur collection in Istanbul, and at Ur itself in Charles Woolley's excavations. Samuel Noah Kramer joined twenty-two fragments into the first complete edition, published by the University of Chicago in 1940. Miguel Civil produced a composite text in 1989, Thorkild Jacobsen and Joachim Krecher published translations in 1987 and 1996, and three new fragments from Nippur were published in 2012.

Because it was well edited early, the Lament for Ur came to overshadow the Lament for Sumer and Ur, which Piotr Michalowski argues is the older version. Scholars compare it with the biblical Book of Lamentations on the fall of Jerusalem in the sixth century BC, which is similar in style and theme, and with laments in Jeremiah, Ezekiel and Psalm 137; Philip S. Alexander has set some of its lines beside Lamentations 2:17.
== meaning
A city lament in which the goddess Ningal mourns Ur, destroyed by a storm sent by the god Enlil, and recalls her vain pleas to the gods to spare it. Around her lament it describes the deserted temples and ruined streets, asks for protection against future disaster and ends by praising Enlil and Ninlil. Its mood is one of deep grief over a whole city.
