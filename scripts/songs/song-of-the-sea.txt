== story
The Song of the Sea, also known by its opening words as Az Yashir Moshe and by a later line as Mi Chamocha, is a poem in the Book of Exodus (15:1–18). In the biblical story the Israelites sang it after crossing the Red Sea in safety, celebrating their freedom after generations of slavery and oppression in Egypt; its first two verses are repeated in verses 20 and 21, where Miriam and other Israelite women sing them. Christians, following Revelation 15:3, call it the Song of Moses.

The poem is written in a form of Hebrew much older than the rest of Exodus. Some scholars consider it the oldest surviving text describing the Exodus, from before the Israelite monarchy; others think it was written in an archaic style on purpose, a known literary device. Proposed dates therefore range from the 13th to the 5th century BCE. Joshua Berman, comparing the texts closely, argues that the Exodus sea account appropriates the Poem of Pentaur, Ramesses II's account of the Battle of Kadesh, possibly with satirical intent as part of an ideological contest with the pharaoh.

The song is one of only two sections of the Torah scroll written in a layout other than plain columns. The Ashkar-Gilson Manuscript, a fragment of a 7th-century Torah scroll, already shows the "brickwork" pattern, which some scholars take as evidence that the Masoretes copied earlier manuscripts faithfully; the Dead Sea Scrolls do not use it. In cantillation the passage has its own festive tune, not bound to the ordinary trope marks.

The poem is recited daily in the morning prayers (Shaharit) of Jewish prayer books, and the Shabbat on which its Torah portion, Beshalach, is read is called Shabbat Shirah, the Sabbath of Song. Most Jewish communities sing a related Ladino poem, the Ketubá del Seten Diya de Pesah, on the seventh day of Passover, the day tradition gives for the drowning of Pharaoh's army and the singing of the song. In Christianity the poem is the first ode of the Eastern Orthodox canon and is used in the Catholic Easter Vigil.

Composers set the song from the Renaissance on, among them Étienne Moulinié, Fernando de las Infantas and Ascanio Trombetti, Handel in Part Three of his oratorio Israel in Egypt (1739) and Schubert in Mirjams Siegesgesang. Lines and paraphrases from it appear in "When You Believe" from the 1998 film The Prince of Egypt, and in the African-American gospel song "O Mary Don't You Weep".
== meaning
A hymn of thanksgiving and triumph that praises God for bringing the Israelites safely through the sea and for the defeat of Pharaoh's army. Its mood is the joy of a people newly freed from slavery.
