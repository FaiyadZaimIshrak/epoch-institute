== story
"The British Grenadiers" is a traditional marching song of the British and Commonwealth military units whose badge bears a grenade. Its tune is old and its origins are disputed, though they are generally put in the early 17th century: John Playford's collection of dance tunes of 1728 has it as "The New Bath", the Victorian musicologist William Chappell linked it to a work of 1622, "Sir Edward Nowell's Delight", and in 1907 the composer Ernest Walker called the melody "three centuries' evolution of an Elizabethan tune". It was brought into Great Britain as a military march in the reign of William III (1689–1702) and resembles one written for Prince John William of Friesland; Henry Grattan Flood proposed another ancestor, the Dutch march "Wilhelmus van Nassouwe" of 1672, itself reworked from a French version of 1568.

The song is about grenadiers in general, not the Grenadier Guards, and every fusilier unit was entitled to use it. It is said to commemorate the assault of 700 British grenadiers on the French-held fortress of Namur in August 1695, during the Nine Years' War. A "Granadeer's March" was mentioned in a London publication of 1706, though it is not certain that it was the same tune, and in 1786 Francis Grose, in his Military Antiquities, quoted two lines of a "grenadier song" he already thought old. The words as sung may go back to the War of the Spanish Succession (1702–1713): they speak of grenadiers throwing grenades, of the tall caps and the heavy pouches in which grenades were carried, and of the looped coats, with broad bands of lace across the chest, that marked the early grenadiers. Some of its words have grown obscure: a fusee is the short musket carried by grenadier officers, the glacis the open slope in front of a fortress's walls, a bumper a cup filled for a toast, and a run of nonsense syllables imitates the drums that kept soldiers in step.

It became the regimental quick march of the Royal Artillery and the Honourable Artillery Company (both since 1716), of the Grenadier Guards and the Royal Regiment of Fusiliers (since 1763) and of the Corps of Royal Engineers (since 1787), and an authorised march of artillery and grenadier regiments in Australia, Gibraltar and Canada; its standard orchestration for military band was approved in 1762. It was popular in Britain and North America throughout the 18th and 19th centuries and is most often heard today at the yearly Trooping the Colour, when the Colour Escort marches onto Horse Guards Parade.

Its tune has carried many other words. Before the American Revolution, Joseph Warren wrote "Free America" to it in 1774, and the Irish poet Edward Lysaght, a supporter of the Irish Volunteers, set his ballad "The Man Who Led the Van of the Irish Volunteers", dedicated to Henry Grattan, to the English melody. In 1871 women students of Girton College, Cambridge, sang "The Girton Pioneers" to it in honour of the first three women to pass the university's tripos examinations, and it has also been set to church hymns. Ignaz Moscheles made it the main theme of the finale of his Piano Concerto No. 4 (1823) and Charles Ives used it in "Putnam's Camp", the second movement of Three Places in New England. Like "Rule, Britannia!", it is often heard in films and on television to mark a British setting or character.
== meaning
A soldiers' boast. The song names the heroes of the ancient world and then claims that none of them can match the British grenadiers, who march up to the enemy's walls and throw their grenades under fire, and it ends with a toast to them. The mood is swaggering and cheerful, carried by the beat of the drum.
