== story
"In dulci jubilo" ("In sweet rejoicing") is a Christmas carol whose original words alternate between medieval German and Latin, a mixed form called macaronic. They are thought to have been written around 1328 by the German mystic Heinrich Seuse. According to folklore, Seuse heard angels sing the words and joined them in a dance of worship; his biography, perhaps written by himself, tells how an angel came to "the Servant", as it calls him, to bring him heavenly joys amid his sufferings, drew him into the dance, and began a joyous song about the infant Jesus.

The tune first appears in Codex 1305, a manuscript of about 1400 in Leipzig University Library, though it may be older. It was printed in Joseph Klug's Lutheran hymnal Geistliche Lieder in 1533 and in Michael Vehe's Gesangbuch of 1537. In 1545 a further verse, possibly by Martin Luther, appeared in Valentin Babst's Geistliche Lieder, printed in Leipzig. The melody spread across Europe and appears in a Swedish and Latin version in Piae Cantiones, the Finnish songbook of 1582. Michael Praetorius set it many times, from 1597 to 1618–19, from simple settings for two to four voices to a festive version for up to five choirs with trumpets and timpani, and his settings were widely adapted in Protestant continental Europe.

Its fame in English rests on two versions. Robert Lucas Pearsall's translation of 1837 keeps the Latin phrases and puts English in place of the German, and his arrangement for eight voices, adapted for four by William Joseph Westbrook and edited by Reginald Jacques in Carols for Choirs, is a mainstay of the Christmas service of Nine Lessons and Carols; a 2008 survey by BBC Music Magazine found it the second most popular choral carol among British cathedral organists and choirmasters. John Mason Neale's looser translation of 1853, "Good Christian Men, Rejoice", has often been criticised: Thomas Helmore misread the old notation of Piae Cantiones, which produced its repeated words, and in 1921 H. J. Massé called the result a mutilation of a grand tune, preferring John Wedderburn's translation of 1567. The broadcaster Jeremy Summerly has argued that the mistaken repeated note is what makes that version memorable. Arthur T. Russell's 19th-century translation, "Now Sing We, Now Rejoice", is sung in several Lutheran hymnals.

The tune has been set by many composers. Dieterich Buxtehude made a chorale cantata of it in 1683 and an organ chorale prelude around 1690. Johann Sebastian Bach set it as a chorale, as a double canon in his Orgelbüchlein and as the chorale prelude BWV 729, written to accompany congregational singing in Arnstadt; another prelude long credited to him, BWV 751, has been attributed to Johann Michael Bach since the rediscovery of the Neumeister Collection in 1984. BWV 729 has been played as the first organ voluntary at the end of the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols at King's College, Cambridge, since the organ scholar Douglas Guest introduced it in 1938. Franz Liszt, Gustav Holst and Norman Dello Joio used the carol too, Thomas Pynchon made it the choral centrepiece of an Advent scene in his novel Gravity's Rainbow (1973), and Mike Oldfield's instrumental arrangement of the Pearsall version reached number 4 in the UK singles chart in January 1976.
== meaning
A Christmas song of joy at the birth of Jesus, moving between everyday German and the Latin of the church. It tells of heavenly joy at the coming of the infant Jesus. The mood is bright and dancing.
