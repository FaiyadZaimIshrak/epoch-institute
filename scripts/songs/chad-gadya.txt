== story
Chad Gadya ("one little goat" or "one kid") is a playful cumulative song sung at the end of the Passover Seder, the ritual feast that opens the Jewish holiday of Passover. An early version appears in a prayer book of the Jewish community of Provence from about the 13th or 14th century, and the song was printed in Haggadahs in Prague in 1527 and 1590, which makes it a late addition to the Seder. Its melody may have roots in medieval German folk music. It is popular with children, like the Haggadah's other cumulative song, Echad Mi Yodea ("Who Knows One?").

Although it is often called Aramaic, the song mixes Aramaic and Hebrew, and its composer's grasp of Aramaic seems to have been limited: it opens with Aramaic words for the goat, the cat and the dog, but towards the end uses Hebrew for the slaughterer, the Angel of Death and God, and its grammar slips between masculine and feminine. Nor was Aramaic used to hide its meaning from non-Jews, since its first printing included a full German translation. It is also sung in Yiddish, Ladino, Judaeo-Italian and Judaeo-Arabic, and Haggadahs differ over whether the father bought or sold the goat.

Behind the light-hearted song commentators have found deeper meanings. A popular reading, apparently first widely published in a pamphlet by Philip Nicodemus Lebrecht in Leipzig in 1731, makes it an allegory of the nations that conquered the Land of Israel, from Assyria and Babylon to the Crusaders and the Ottomans, with the goat as the Jewish people and God bringing them home at the end; many variants assign the oppressors differently. Rabbi Jacob Emden read it in 1795 as the perils facing the soul in life; Rabbi Jonathan Eybeschuetz as a short history of Israel from the covenant with Abraham to the Roman conqueror Titus; Rabbi Moses Sofer as the Passover rite in the Temple of Jerusalem; and the Vilna Gaon as a history from the birthright of Abraham and Isaac to the Messiah. These readings were summarised by Rabbi Kenneth Brander in the Journal of Jewish Music & Liturgy in 1994.

In 1917 and 1919 the Russian avant-garde artist El Lissitzky made two illustrated books of Had Gadya, with the verses in Yiddish. Art historians have read the change between them, in which the Angel of Death is cast down in the first and plainly dead in the second, with his victims raised to life, as showing his sympathy for the October Revolution and the end of discrimination against the Jews of the former Russian Empire. In 1989 an anti-war version by the Israeli singer Chava Alberstein drew calls for a ban on Israeli radio; it became well known and is now often played at Passover. The song gave its title to Wolf Mankowitz's 1953 novel A Kid for Two Farthings, became a popular song in Italy as Angelo Branduardi's "Alla fiera dell'est" in the 1970s, and appears in Henry Roth's novel Call It Sleep (1934) and many films and television series. In Yiddish slang "chad gadya" also means jail.
== meaning
A cumulative chain: a father buys a little goat for two small coins, and each verse brings on something that overcomes the one before, from a cat and a dog through a stick, fire, water, an ox, a slaughterer and the Angel of Death, and at last God. Each verse repeats the whole chain back to the goat, so the song grows with every verse; it is cheerful in tone, though often read as an allegory of suffering and redemption.
