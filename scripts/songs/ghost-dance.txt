== story
The Ghost Dance songs belong to a ceremony that began among the Northern Paiute of Mason Valley, in what became the state of Nevada. A typhoid epidemic in 1867, and other diseases more common among Europeans, had killed about one-tenth of the people, and the disruption had kept many families from their nomadic way of life. In 1869 the healer Hawthorne Wodziwob organised community dances to announce a vision of the land of the dead, helped by the "weather doctor" Tavibo. Tavibo's son Wovoka, also called Jack Wilson, was believed to have had a vision during the solar eclipse of 1 January 1889. According to the anthropologist James Mooney, who interviewed him before 1892, Wovoka taught that the people must love one another and not fight, must work and not steal or lie, and must give up war and the old self-mutilation of mourning; if the dance was performed for five days at the proper intervals, the living and the dead would be reunited. The Paiute called it "Dance In A Circle"; the Lakota name, "Spirit Dance", was translated as "Ghost Dance".

The dance grew out of the circle or round dance, in which large groups move in a ring, usually around a leader and often with singers. At its height a Ghost Dance drew hundreds of people, many from nearby reservations, who fasted for one or two days beforehand and went into sweat lodges, men and women apart. For two to four days the dancers held hands, faces turned upwards, and shuffled slowly sideways in a clockwise circle, singing Ghost Dance songs, while shamans waved eagle-wing fans before them; the dance itself used no instruments. Exhausted dancers fell unconscious into the centre and the circle closed behind them. The aim was a trance in which the dancer travelled to the afterworld and saw the times before the Europeans came, when bison were plentiful; on waking, dancers told the shamans what they had seen. Ella Cara Deloria and Elaine and Dora Goodale left eyewitness accounts.

The message spread quickly across much of the western United States, to California and Oklahoma, as peoples sent delegates to see the prophet and many returned as believers; each people joined parts of the ritual to its own beliefs. Among the Lakota it took a more millenarian turn: a renewed earth from which the white settlers would be rolled away. In 1890 Kicking Bear is believed to have brought them the ghost shirts, said to turn bullets. That February the United States government broke a treaty and divided the Great Sioux Reservation into five smaller reservations to make room for homesteaders, and later that year, after a failed harvest and with the bison almost wiped out, it cut the Lakota's rations by half, leaving them at risk of starvation. The Lakota turned to the Ghost Dance. The agents of the Bureau of Indian Affairs took fright; James McLaughlin called for troops and named Sitting Bull as the movement's leader, while the former agent Valentine McGillycuddy saw nothing out of the ordinary in the dances and warned that the troops themselves would bring trouble. Thousands of soldiers were sent. On 15 December 1890 Sitting Bull was arrested for failing to stop his people's dancing, and was shot dead in the exchange of fire that followed.

On 29 December 1890, at Wounded Knee Creek near the Pine Ridge Agency, soldiers trying to disarm the Miniconjou band of Spotted Elk opened fire, with carbines and Hotchkiss guns on the hill above the camp. Between 200 and 300 Lakota were massacred by US soldiers, most of them women and children; 25 soldiers died, many by their own side's fire. Twenty soldiers received the Medal of Honor, and none of the medals has been revoked, although Congress apologised for the massacre in 1990. After Wounded Knee open dancing declined for fear of further violence, but the ceremony did not end: it went underground, and Wovoka, Kicking Bear and Short Bull went on teaching. Not every people took it up; Navajo leaders called it "worthless words" in 1890.

Soon after the gramophone came on the market, a small set of Ghost Dance songs, gathered by James Mooney, was recorded and pressed in 1894, and the masters are held by the Library of Congress; who sang them is not known, possibly Mooney himself. An Edison Kinetoscope film of a "Sioux Ghost Dance" from the 1890s showed a fast dance with a drum, unlike the slow ceremony. During the occupation of Wounded Knee in 1973, Lakota men and women, Mary Brave Bird among them, danced the Ghost Dance on the site where their ancestors had been killed, and Leonard Crow Dog later recalled the songs they sang there. The Caddo still practise the Ghost Dance, and the ceremonies continue in private, their details kept among the participants.
== meaning
The songs of the dance announce that a nation is coming, and the whole world and the buffalo with it, a message brought by the eagle and the crow from "the father". They carry the hope of a renewed world, sung over and over in a slow, solemn and expectant mood.
