== story
"The Bonnie Banks o' Loch Lomond", or simply "Loch Lomond", is a traditional Scottish folk song (Roud 9598), named after the largest loch in Scotland; in Scots "bonnie" means fair or beautiful. Its maker is unknown. It has often been dated to 1746, and its origins are thought to lie in the Jacobite rising of 1745, with which it has long been associated.

Two readings of the words are common. In one, it is a lament of the march of December 1745, when Bonnie Prince Charlie retreated into Scotland, sung by a soldier who rues his sacrifice for the king; in Celtic mythology the "low road" was a fairy route by which the soul of a soldier killed abroad returned home. In the other, the chorus gives the last words of a Jacobite rebel before his execution, perhaps at Carlisle, where dozens of rebels were executed: seeing his lover at the gallows, he tells her they will meet again in Scotland, though by different roads. In spite of these Jacobite roots, the song became one of a handful of folk songs that stood for British unity as the empire grew.

One of the earliest printings of the music was published in Edinburgh in 1840 by Paterson as "Bonnie Loch Loman", credited to "a lady", with arrangements by Finlay Dunn and John Thomson. Lady John Scott was often named as its composer, but she only wrote down the tune and words after hearing a boy sing them in the streets of Edinburgh; in William Black's novel Wild Eelin (1898) the heroine, Eelin MacDonald, dismisses as "spurious" the idea that an Edinburgh street urchin would ever sing it. Both the 1840 music and Lady John Scott's words differ a good deal from the form sung today. A broadside of 1849, "Flora's Lament for Her Charlie", largely parallels its words; Flora MacDonald, a supporter of the prince, was imprisoned for helping him escape his pursuers. There are local variants too, among them one that sets the lovers' parting in Luss glen. The song is often wrongly credited to the Scottish poet Andrew Lang, who wrote a poem titled "The Bonnie Banks o' Loch Lomond, 1746" in 1876; it names Sergeant Môr, John Du Cameron, a Jacobite who fought on as an outlaw until he was captured and hanged in 1753. The Irish song "Red Is the Rose", popularised by Tommy Makem, has the same melody and different words on a similar theme; it is traditional, not Makem's own.

The tune has precedents in other folk songs: the Danish and Faroese "Dankonning lod gribe en havfrue fin" ("The Dane-King Captured a Mermaid"), "Godesses" in John Playford's The Dancing Master (1651), which "The Oak and the Ash" also resembles, and fragments of "The Bonniest Lass in a' the World" and "The Bonnie House of Airlie". It has often been described as sharing a melody with "Kind Robin Loes Me" and its variants, such as "Robin Cushie".

Composers took it up early. Ferdinand Thieriot wrote a symphonic fantasy on the tune in 1868, Charles Ives quoted it in "The Alcotts" movement of his Concord Sonata, and Ralph Vaughan Williams arranged it for baritone and male choir in 1921. Marie Narelle recorded it for Edison in 1906 and Harry Lauder in 1926. Maxine Sullivan's swing version, arranged by Claude Thornhill and recorded on 6 August 1937, made her career and led many others to swing folk songs; she recorded it 14 times, the last at the Fujitsu-Concord Jazz Festival in Tokyo in 1986. Benny Goodman's orchestra played Thornhill's arrangement, sung by Martha Tilton, at its Carnegie Hall concert of 1938.

The Scottish band Runrig recorded it in 1979 and made it their first single in 1982; in 2007 they recorded it again with 50,000 members of the Tartan Army, the supporters of Scotland's football team, for the BBC's Children in Need, and the single went to number 1 in the Scottish chart. AC/DC released a rock version, "Fling Thing", in 1976 and played it at the Apollo Theatre in Glasgow. The song is also the anthem of FC Köln, whose fans sing it before each match. In films and television it often marks a Scottish setting or character, from Laurel and Hardy's Tit for Tat to the Scottish terrier Jock in Disney's Lady and the Tramp (1955).
== meaning
A song of parting by the loch: the singer remembers the bright shores of Loch Lomond where he and his true love walked, and knows that he will be in Scotland before her, she by the high road and he by the low, though they will never meet there again. Sung slowly it is a lament of loss and exile, with the loch's beauty set against the sorrow; its well-loved chorus has also carried it into marches, swing and the football terraces.
