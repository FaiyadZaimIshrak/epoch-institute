== story
Qasīdat al-Burda, the "Ode of the Mantle", is a thirteenth-century ode in praise of Muhammad by al-Busiri, a mystic of the Shadhili Sufi order in Egypt. Its own title is "The Celestial Lights in Praise of the Best of Creation". The poem's story is told of its author: al-Busiri, afflicted by illness, praised Muhammad without ceasing until the Prophet appeared to him in a dream and wrapped him in a mantle; in the morning he found that God had cured him. The name recalls an older poem, Bānat Suʿād by Ka'b ibn Zuhayr, which was first called al-Burda: Ka'b recited it before Muhammad after embracing Islam, and Muhammad, moved, put his own mantle over him. Al-Busiri's poem, in which the mantle comes in a dream, became by far the more famous of the two.

The Burda has ten chapters and 160 verses, all on one rhyme: every verse ends in the Arabic letter mīm, a form called mīmiyya. A refrain asking God's blessings and peace on the Prophet is sung between the verses. The chapters move from love poetry and warnings against the self's whims to the praise of the Prophet, his birth, his miracles, the Qur'an, his Ascension and his struggles, and end with a plea for his intercession and a personal prayer.

Sufis have long venerated the poem. It is learned by heart and recited in gatherings, and many Muslims recite it both day to day and on special occasions such as the Mawlid, the Prophet's birthday, which makes it one of the most recited poems in the world. Its verses decorate the walls of public buildings and mosques; for centuries they decorated the Prophet's Mosque in Medina, where all but two lines were later erased.

Mainstream Sufi scholars such as Ibn Hajar al-Haytami, Nazifi and Qastallani wrote commentaries on it, over 90 in all, and the Shafi'i hadith master Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani (d. 852 AH) studied it, reading it aloud to his teacher and receiving it in writing from a transmitter who had heard it from al-Busiri himself. Not everyone approved: the Najdi scholar Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab held the poem to be shirk, idolatry. It has been translated into Persian, Turkish, the Berber languages, Urdu, Punjabi, Sindhi, Saraiki, Hausa, Chinese, French, German, Norwegian and English, among others.

The Burda also became the model of a kind of poem. Ahmad Shawqi wrote Nahj al-Burda in its style, and Tamim al-Barghouti's Burdat Tamim, written in November and December 2010 and first published in 2013, is the latest major work in the line; in 2020 he presented it in a five-part video series that first explains the three earlier Burdas, Ka'b's, al-Busiri's and Shawqi's.
== meaning
A devotional poem of love and praise for the Prophet Muhammad. It begins with longing and with the poet's regret for his own failings, then recounts the Prophet's birth, miracles, the Qur'an and his night journey, and ends by asking for his intercession and God's mercy. Its mood is yearning, reverent and hopeful.
