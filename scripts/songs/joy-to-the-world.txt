== story
"Joy to the World" was written by the English minister and hymn writer Isaac Watts as a Christian reading of Psalm 98, with an allusion to Genesis 3. It was first published in 1719, under the title "The Messiah's Coming and Kingdom", in Watts's collection The Psalms of David: Imitated in the Language of the New Testament, and applied to the Christian State and Worship. Watts held that Psalms 96 to 98 referred to Christ's incarnation and his kingdom, and his paraphrase turns the psalm's praise into a celebration of Christ's coming and rule. The hymn has four stanzas. The third, which turns from the psalm to the curse of Genesis 3:17–19, is sometimes left out of hymnals.

Watts's preface said the verses fitted the tunes of the "Old Psalm-Book" and were to be sung to the common tunes in Common Metre; which psalm book he meant is not clear. In the late 18th century the hymn was printed with music several times, but to tunes unrelated to the one used today.

That tune comes from the American composer Lowell Mason, already known for hymn tunes such as "Bethany" and "Hamburg". He first published it with these words in his Occasional Psalm and Hymn Tunes (1836) under the name "Antioch", marked "From Handel", again in The Modern Psalmist (1839), and in the arrangement now usual in The National Psalmist (Boston, 1848). Its first four notes match the opening of the chorus "Lift up your heads" in Handel's Messiah (1742), and a later phrase the arioso "Comfort ye". Mason admired Handel and in 1827 had become president of the Boston Handel and Haydn Society, so the tune's link to Messiah has long been debated; Handel scholars today dismiss the resemblance as chance. Tunes very like "Antioch" were printed in the early 1830s, the earliest as "Comfort" in 1832, and Thomas Hawkes printed "Comfort" in 1833 as by an unknown author. A possible common source is Martin Luther's Christmas chorale "Vom Himmel hoch da komm ich her", of which "Antioch" appears to be an expanded paraphrase; that resemblance may also explain why the hymn came to be sung at Christmas.

A recording by the Trinity Choir was very popular in 1911. Since the 20th century it has been the most-published Christmas carol in North America: by December 2009 it appeared in 1,387 North American hymnals, according to the Dictionary of North American Hymnology.
== meaning
A hymn of praise in which heaven, earth and the nations are called to rejoice at the coming of a king, read as Christ. The third stanza turns to the overcoming of sin and its curse, and the last celebrates his rule over the nations. The mood is jubilant throughout.
