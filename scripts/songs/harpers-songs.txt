== story
The Harper's Songs are ancient Egyptian texts that first appear in tomb inscriptions of the Middle Kingdom and were still copied on papyrus in the New Kingdom, as in the Papyrus Harris 500. They are reflections on death and were often used in funerary settings. Egyptian texts do not always separate songs, hymns and poetry clearly; by convention, poetic texts shown beside musical instruments are treated as songs, and those linked with the cult of the dead are nearly always shown with harps, which gives these their name. The drawings of blind harpists that accompany them suggest that they were sung.

Most of the songs praise the tomb and the life after death, to reassure the tomb's owner of his fate. Because they were reflections rather than part of the burial rites, they allowed freer thought. On the funerary stela of Iki, the dead man sits at an offering table with his wife while the rotund harpist Neferhotep sits before them; on the stela of Nebankh from Abydos, the singer Tjeniaa, who names himself as the one who keeps his master's name alive, squats before the seated official and praises his eternal tomb, its offerings and the presence of his ka.

One song went further. Its opening line says it was copied from the tomb of a King Intef, a name borne by several kings of the 11th and 17th dynasties; it survives in the tomb of Paatenemheb from the reign of Akhenaten and in the Papyrus Harris 500, and scholars accept it as a copy of a genuine Middle Kingdom text. It doubts that there is an afterlife at all, laments death, and advises the listener to enjoy life while it lasts. Miriam Lichtheim saw it as opening a sceptical strand of thought that is also found in the Dispute between a Man and His Ba and in other Harper's Songs. It has been compared with Herodotus's much later account of Egyptian banquets of the rich, at which a wooden figure of a dead man was carried round as a reminder to drink and be merry, and with the text known as The Immortality of Writers.

The New Kingdom answered this scepticism, either rejecting it as impious or tempering it. The tomb of the priest Neferhotep holds three Harper's Songs that span the whole range: one blends the sceptical view with the usual hopes, a second rejects scepticism and defends the land of the dead as a peaceful place without terrors that all must reach, and a third affirms life after death in ritual terms. A Harper's Song has been sung in modern times in Spanish by Macarena Fajardo Vicente-Ortega.
== meaning
Songs sung for the dead in their tombs. Most praise the tomb, its offerings and the life to come, and assure the dead of a good welcome; one famous song doubts the afterlife and urges the living to enjoy their days, and later songs answer it. Their mood ranges from calm confidence to wistful scepticism.
