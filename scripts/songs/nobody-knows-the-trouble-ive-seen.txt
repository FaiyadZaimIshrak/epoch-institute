== story
"Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen" is an African-American spiritual that took shape during the period of slavery but was not published until after emancipation. It first appeared in print, as "Nobody Knows", with seven verses, in April 1866 in The Child at Home, a paper of the American Tract Society in Boston. The paper called it a specimen of the hymns sung by the freedmen of the South, sent in by a teacher who had been working for some time at Charleston, South Carolina, among the formerly enslaved. In May 1866 The Pennsylvania Freedmen's Bulletin reported it being sung at a school near Beaufort, South Carolina.

In 1867 it was printed with four verses, as "Nobody Knows The Trouble I've Had", in Slave Songs of the United States; Hugo Frey used this version in his collection Famous Negro Spirituals of 1924. The Jubilee Singers sang a song with a similar chorus but a different tune and words, first published in 1872. In the earliest known versions, and most often in American church hymnals, the second line of the chorus turns from the singer's private sorrow to Jesus as the one witness of it; later versions changed that line.

From the late 19th century African-American music entered the concert hall in arrangements by Black composers such as Samuel Coleridge-Taylor, Henry Thacker Burleigh and J. Rosamond Johnson. Johnson arranged this spiritual for voice and piano in 1917, while he directed the New York Music School Settlement for Colored People, and for violin and piano in 1919 at the suggestion of the violinist Maud Powell, the first white solo concert artist to perform classical arrangements of spirituals; she played it that autumn and died in November. Florence Price quoted it in her Mississippi River Suite of 1934, and Michael Tippett made it one of the five spirituals in his oratorio A Child of Our Time, first performed in 1944.

The contralto Marian Anderson had her first successful recording with the song, on the Victor label, in 1925. It has since been recorded by Mahalia Jackson, Louis Armstrong, Lena Horne, Paul Robeson, Harry James, Sam Cooke and many others; in 1962 Georgia Lee, the first Indigenous Australian artist to record blues songs, included it on her album. It has been sung in many films, among them the 1944 race film Go Down, Death! and Roberto Rossellini's Paisan (1946), where an African American soldier sings it to an Italian boy. In June 2026 CBS News named it among the 250 essential American songs of the past 250 years.
== meaning
A song of private grief and endurance: the singer has known troubles that no one around them sees or shares, and turns to God as the one who knows them. The mood is sorrowful but steady, with a turn towards praise in the earliest versions.
