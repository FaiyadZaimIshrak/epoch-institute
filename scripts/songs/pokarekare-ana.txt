== story
"Pōkarekare Ana" is a traditional New Zealand love song in the Māori language, probably composed communally about the time the First World War began in 1914. Its author is not known. The Eastern Māori politicians Paraire Tomoana and Āpirana Ngata published it in 1919, but neither claimed to have written it: they explained that it had come from north of Auckland and had been made popular by Māori soldiers training near Auckland before they sailed for the war in Europe.

The Māori words have hardly changed since, except that the waters named in the first line are made local. Some versions name Lake Rotorua in the North Island, which ties the song to the story of Hinemoa, who swam across the lake to her forbidden lover Tūtānekai on Mokoia Island. There have been many different English translations. The song was first written mostly in triple time, with the verse in duple time, but since the Second World War it has more often been heard in duple time.

It became one of the most popular songs in New Zealand and has been used in advertising and by sporting groups: in "Sailing Away" for the country's 1987 America's Cup challenge, in Air New Zealand advertisements of the 1990s and 2000 (with a recording by Kiri Te Kanawa) and again in 2020, sung by Hayley Westenra, for the airline's 80th birthday. In April 2013 members and spectators in the New Zealand Parliament sang it after the house passed the bill legalising same-sex marriage. Dozens of singers have recorded it, among them the opera singers Kiri Te Kanawa and Malvina Major.

The song travelled far beyond New Zealand. It is said to have reached South Korea with New Zealand soldiers in the Korean War, though there is no record of their spreading it; given Korean words and the title "Yeonga", it became popular across the country. Since the 1960s it has been sung in Timor-Leste as "Ha'u Hakerek Surat Ida", with words by Momô dos Mártires, and its melody carries an Irish hymn to the Virgin Mary.
== meaning
A love song of longing and separation, addressed to a loved one far away. The waters named in it change with the singers, so each community can make the song its own.
