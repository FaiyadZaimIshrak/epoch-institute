== story
Friedrich Schiller wrote "An die Freude" in the summer of 1785. From the beginning of May until mid-September that year he stayed with his publisher, Georg Joachim Göschen, at Gohlis near Leipzig, where he wrote the poem together with his play Don Carlos. The friendship of Christian Gottfried Körner, his longtime friend and patron, had inspired it. The ode was published the next year in the magazine Thalia. A slightly revised version, which changed two lines of the first stanza and left out the last stanza, was published after Schiller's death, in 1808. Schiller himself later judged the poem a failure: in an 1800 letter to Körner he called it "detached from reality".

Some writers have speculated that Schiller first wrote an "Ode to Freedom" and changed it to "To Joy"; Beethoven's biographer Alexander Wheelock Thayer suggested this. The musicologist Alexander Rehding notes that scholarly consensus finds no factual basis for the idea, and that even Leonard Bernstein, who used "Freiheit" in two performances in 1989, called it conjecture.

The poem is best known through Ludwig van Beethoven, who used the 1808 version in the final, fourth movement of his Ninth Symphony, completed in 1824. Beethoven's text does not follow Schiller's poem entirely and adds some new sections. Other composers had set the poem before him, among them Körner himself (1786), Carl Friedrich Zelter (1792) and Johann Friedrich Reichardt (1796), and later Franz Schubert (1815) and Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky (1865).

Beethoven's melody, without Schiller's words, was adopted as the anthem of the Council of Europe in 1972 and of the European Union in 1985, known as the Anthem of Europe. The same melody served as the national anthem of Ian Smith's Rhodesia from 1974 to 1979, as "Rise, O Voices of Rhodesia".

The setting has also been sung in protest and remembrance. In 1957 Paul Robeson, blacklisted and with his passport revoked, sang it to a festival of 5,000 miners in Wales over a transatlantic telephone line. Under the military dictatorship in Chile (1973–1990), women gathered outside prisons to sing a Spanish version, "Himno a la Alegría", to bring hope to those held inside. Chinese students broadcast it during the Tiananmen Square protests of 1989, and on Christmas Day 1989, after the fall of the Berlin Wall, Leonard Bernstein conducted it in Berlin with choir and orchestra members from East and West Germany. A Yiddish version by Isaac Leib Peretz from the 1890s is still used as a Jewish protest song.

Today it is performed at Daiku ("Number Nine") concerts in Japan every December, was played after Emmanuel Macron's victory in the French presidential election of 2017, and, as an instrumental, serves as the anthem of UEFA's European Qualifiers and of the Copa Libertadores.
== meaning
A hymn in which joy is pictured as a divine spark that draws all people together as brothers. It celebrates friendship and love and looks upward to a creator above the stars, in a mood of exalted, communal celebration.
