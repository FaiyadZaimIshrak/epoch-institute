== story
"Kâtibim", "my clerk", also called "Üsküdar'a Gider İken", "while going to Üsküdar", is a Turkish folk song in the form of an Istanbul türkü. It tells of someone's clerk (kâtip) on the way to Üsküdar, on the Asian shore of Istanbul. The same melody is sung, usually with entirely different words, in many countries around Turkey and beyond, above all in the Balkans, and many places claim the tune as their own; a documentary film, Whose is this song?, and an international youth project, Everybody's Song, recorded many of these versions.

The versions show how far the tune travelled across the former Ottoman lands. Anton Pann collected and published a Romanian one in the 1850s. In Bulgaria it is a love song and also a hymn of resistance to the Ottoman Empire from the Strandzha Mountains; in Bosnia it is a sevdalinka and a qasida; Albania, Serbia (a recording of 1910), North Macedonia and Hungary have their own, and Greece knows it under at least eight names, among them "Little trehantiri" and "From a foreign place". In the Arab world the same melody carries the folk song "Ya Banat Iskandaria" ("Oh girls of Alexandria"), recorded by Mohammed El-Bakkar around 1957, the Aleppine "Ghazali Ghazali" and a devotional qasida for Muhammad; Gloria Levy sang a multilingual Sephardic version, "Fel Shara". In Bengal the poet Kazi Nazrul Islam, who is thought to have learned the melody while fighting in the Middle East in World War I, set it in the 1930s to an Islamic song about Muhammad and to songs of youth and of the monsoon rain.

The tune reached North America in the 1920s: the klezmer clarinettist Naftule Brandwein, born in Galicia and in the United States since 1909, recorded it without words as "Der Terk in America" in 1924. In Turkey, the composer Saygun ended his choral work Bir Tutam Kekik, Op. 22 (1943), with variations on it as an "old Istanbul folk song". Safiye Ayla's recording of 1949 became the model for many later ones; in 1953 Eartha Kitt and Eydie Gormé recorded it in the United States as "Uska Dara – A Turkish Tale", with an English adaptation by Stella Lee. Zeki Müren's recording was also popular, and he acted in the 1968 film "Kâtip (Üsküdar'a Giderken)", directed by Ülkü Erakalın, in which it plays an important part.

The song is still arranged and mixed with others: in a Turkish-Urdu version for Pakistan's Coke Studio (2013), in Pink Martini's Get Happy (2013), in Fazil Say's violin concerto "1001 Nights in the Harem", and in 2026 with Nazrul's "Room Jhoom" for Coke Studio Bangla. Its tune has been compared to Boney M.'s "Rasputin" (1978), which the band denied, although its singer Liz Mitchell later said they had bought an old Turkish folk song from a Tunisian.
== meaning
A folk song about someone's clerk (kâtip) on the road to Üsküdar. The many other-language versions set new words to the same tune: love songs, a song of resistance, devotional poems for the Prophet, a song of the monsoon rain.
