== story
"Ma'oz Tzur" is a piyyut, a Hebrew liturgical poem, sung on the festival of Hanukkah after the festival lights are lit. It takes its name from its opening words, "Strong Rock (of my Salvation)", a name for God in Judaism. The first letters of its first five stanzas spell the poet's name, Mordechai. Who he was is uncertain: he may have been the Mordecai ben Isaac ha-Levi who wrote the Sabbath table hymn "Mah Yafit", or, judging from the appeal in the closing verse, the Mordecai whose father-in-law was martyred in the Mainz massacre of 1096, during the First Crusade. The poem is commonly dated to the 13th century, in the time of the Crusades, but recent research points to the 12th; Avraham Fränkel suggests it was written in Germany between 1160 and 1190.

Like much medieval Jewish liturgical poetry, the hymn is full of allusions to the Bible and to rabbinic interpretation. Its four middle stanzas retell four deliverances of the Jewish people: from Pharaoh in the Exodus, from Nebuchadnezzar at the end of the Babylonian captivity, from Haman in Persia (the miracle of Purim) and from Antiochus, in the revolt against Greek rule commemorated by Hanukkah. The first and last stanzas are in the present tense. The sixth stanza, whose opening words form a second acrostic, hazak ("be strong"), again calls for retribution against the enemies of the Jewish people; some read its "Admon", "the red one", as the emperor Frederick Barbarossa. The stanza was not found in print until the 18th century, and it has been suggested that it was passed on only by word of mouth, held back for its strong anti-Christian hints; others take it for a later addition.

The hymn was at first sung only at home, and has been used in the synagogue since at least the 19th century; often only the first stanza, or the first and fifth, is sung. Its bright and stirring tune is sung by Jews almost everywhere at Hanukkah and has become the festival's representative melody, though many other traditional tunes exist. Cantor Birnbaum of Königsberg identified it as an adaptation of the old German folk song "So weiss ich eins, dass mich erfreut", which was widespread among German Jews as early as 1450. The same folk melody was the first that Martin Luther used for his chorales, setting it to "Nun freut euch, lieben Christen g'mein"; harmonised by Bach (BWV 388), it appears in "Hymns Ancient and Modern" (London, 1875). The earliest transcription of the Jewish form is by Isaac Nathan, who set it to Byron's poem "On Jordan's Banks" in the "Hebrew Melodies" (London, 1815), and Julius Mombach shaped the version now favoured by British Jews. An older minor-key air once sung to the hymn appears in Benedetto Marcello's "Estro Poetico Armonico" (Venice, 1724) as a melody of the German Jews.

Before the Second World War, German Jews commonly sang the second and fourth verses to another melody, recorded by Cantor Israel Alter and revived in 2018 by the Jewish Amsterdam Chamber Ensemble at the Royal Concertgebouw. In English the hymn is best known as "Rock of Ages", a free rendering by Marcus Jastrow and Gustav Gottheil based on Leopold Stein's German version. In 1969 it inspired Naomi Shemer's "Shivchei Ma'oz", performed by the Israel Defense Forces' Southern Command Band, which tied the old hymn to the positions of the Bar Lev Line, then under attack in the War of Attrition.
== meaning
The singer praises God as the fortress of his salvation and hopes for the Temple to be rebuilt and its enemies silenced. The poem then walks through Jewish history, from slavery in Egypt to exile in Babylon, the threat of Haman and the victory of the Hasmoneans, giving thanks for each rescue. The last stanza asks for a final redemption. The mood is thankful and confident, with a fierce edge.
