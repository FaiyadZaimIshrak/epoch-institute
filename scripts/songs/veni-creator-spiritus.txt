== story
Veni Creator Spiritus ("Come, Creator Spirit") is a Christian hymn in Latin believed to have been written by Rabanus Maurus, a ninth-century Frankish Benedictine monk, teacher, archbishop and saint. When the Latin text is used, it is normally sung to a Gregorian chant tune first known from Kempten Abbey around the year 1000. Two forms of the words are in use: the original ninth-century text, kept by some Protestant churches and certain Catholic religious orders, and a classicized revision published in 1632 under Pope Urban VIII, used in most Catholic parishes.

As an invocation of the Holy Spirit, the hymn is sung in the Catholic Church at Pentecost, at Terce and Vespers. It also opens solemn occasions: the College of Cardinals sings it as it enters the Sistine Chapel for a papal conclave, and it is sung at the consecration of bishops, the ordination of priests, confirmation, the profession of members of religious institutes, the dedication of churches, the opening of synods and councils, the coronation of monarchs and the Red Mass that starts the judicial year. It is traditionally chanted on New Year's Day to obtain a plenary indulgence.

The Reformation carried it into other churches and languages. Martin Luther made a German paraphrase as a Pentecost chorale, first performed and published in 1524 with a melody derived from the chant; it is still in the Protestant hymnal Evangelisches Gesangbuch. In England the hymn has been part of Anglican liturgies since the ordinal of 1550, in a version attributed to Archbishop Thomas Cranmer, his only venture into English verse and the only metrical hymn of the Edwardian liturgy; Cranmer added a stanza on the Last Judgement and strife within Christendom that has no parallel in the Latin. From 1562 John Day printed Cranmer's version at the head of the metrical paraphrases in The Whole Booke of Psalmes. More than fifty English translations and paraphrases have been made since the English Reformation.

Bishop John Cosin wrote an English version for the coronation of Charles I in 1625, compressing the seven Latin verses into four. It entered the 1662 Book of Common Prayer and has been sung at every British coronation since, by the choir after the Creed and before the Anointing. At the coronation of Charles III and Camilla in 2023 it was sung for the first time in Irish Gaelic, Scots Gaelic and Welsh as well as English, in new translations by Iain Urchadan, Damian McManus and Grahame Davies. Another English version, by the poet John Dryden, was first published in his Examen Poeticum (1693). In German, Heinrich Bone published a Catholic paraphrase in 1845 and Friedrich Dörr another in 1972; both stand in the Catholic hymnal Gotteslob.

Composers have set the hymn or its tune for four centuries: Jehan Titelouze (1623), Marc-Antoine Charpentier (five settings), Johann Pachelbel, Nicolas de Grigny, Johann Sebastian Bach (the chorale BWV 370 and the organ preludes BWV 631 and 667), Berlioz, Franck, Bruckner, Gustav Mahler, who set the Latin text in Part I of his Symphony No. 8 (1906), Maurice Duruflé, Karol Szymanowski, Paul Hindemith, Krzysztof Penderecki, Karlheinz Stockhausen and Arvo Pärt, among others.
== meaning
The hymn is a prayer that calls on the Holy Spirit, as creator, to come to those who sing it. It is an invocation rather than a story: a request for the Spirit's presence and help. That is why it opens solemn acts in which a community asks for guidance, such as conclaves, ordinations and coronations, as well as the feast of Pentecost.

== notes
Authorship is only "believed" (attributed) to Rabanus Maurus; the article gives no year of composition beyond "ninth century" and no dates for Rabanus, so year 850 is a placeholder for sorting.
The article has a typo: "In 156, John Day included it after the psalms in his incomplete metrical psalter of that year" (presumably 1560 or 1561). I left that sentence out and kept only the 1562 Whole Booke of Psalmes.
The == inspired titles of the German paraphrases (Luther, Bone, Dörr) are also their first lines, which in turn render the Latin first line; I kept them as the works' titles but left out the first lines of the English versions (Cosin's, Dryden's), which the article also quotes. The editor may prefer the German titles out too.
The article's external links ("English Version Vatican News", "Gregorian Chant: free downloads") have no URLs in the saved file, hence "none named". The Latin words are public domain (9th century; 1632 revision); the 2023 Gaelic and Welsh translations are modern and in copyright.
