title: Social Hierarchy of Joseon Korea: From King to Enslaved
desc: Joseon Korea's social ladder: the king, yangban aristocracy, middle people, commoners, enslaved nobi and outcast butchers, shamans and entertainers.
intro: Below the king, Joseon Korea ranked its people by birth, recorded in household registers and justified by Neo-Confucian teaching. In law the state divided its subjects into the free "good people" (yangin) and the "base people" (cheonmin), but in practice four status groups formed. The yangban, officials and their descendants, studied the classics, sat the state examinations and owned land and slaves. The "middle people" (jungin) were hereditary technical specialists and clerks. The commoners (sangmin), mostly farmers, paid the taxes and owed military service. At the bottom were the lowborn: above all the nobi, enslaved people who made up at least 30 per cent of the population at the system's height, and outcast butchers, shamans and entertainers. Status decided dress, marriage and the examinations a man could sit. From the 1590s war, commerce and the sale of titles loosened the order; slavery was abolished in 1894.

text: The king (wang) stood at the head of the state and of its Confucian order. The general Yi Seong-gye founded the dynasty as King Taejo in 1392, and his descendants of the Jeonju Yi clan reigned until its end. The king appointed and promoted officials, promulgated the laws, among them the National Code (Gyeongguk daejeon) completed in 1485, and was the final judge; King Taejong set up the Sinmun Office to hear subjects wronged by officials or aristocrats. Joseon was a tributary of Ming China, whose recognition the kings sought. Royal power was hedged by Confucian institutions: the State Council deliberated on policy, and the censors reviewed the king's decrees and could demand that they be withdrawn. A king who defied the scholar-officials could be overthrown: Yeonsangun, who abolished the Office of Censors and purged scholars, was deposed in 1506, and Gwanghaegun in 1623. Sejong worked with the scholars of the Hall of Worthies, and under him the Korean alphabet, hangul, was created in 1443.
rise: The throne passed within the royal Yi family to a son or another kinsman, not always peacefully: Grand Prince Suyang seized it from his nephew Danjong and reigned as Sejo from 1455, and the officials who deposed Yeonsangun in 1506 chose his half-brother.
you: You rule with the advice of the State Council and must hear the censors, who can demand that you withdraw your decrees; if you defy the scholar-officials too far, they may depose you.
kid: Joseon kings ruled with the help of scholar-officials, and King Sejong gave Korea its own alphabet, hangul.

text: The yangban, the "two orders" of civil (munban) and military (muban) officials, were the ruling class. In law the title belonged to office holders, and office came through the state examinations (gwageo): the regular higher literary examination, held every three years, passed only 33 men, and 14,620 passed in the whole dynasty; candidates had to list their ancestors and the offices they held. In practice the status became hereditary. The descendants of officials were accepted as yangban, and local yangban kept registers of their lineages (hyangan), whose householders sat on the local council. Wealth was measured in land and slaves, and the yangban were their chief owners. A yangban was expected to study the Confucian classics rather than work with his hands, and many lived on country estates as teachers and local leaders, as Yi Hwang did at his academy near Andong. Officials were ranked in eighteen grades, marked by red, blue or green robes. From the 16th century a few great Seoul families monopolised the highest offices.
rise: In theory any free man could sit the examinations, and a family that produced no official for several generations lost its status, though the rule seems never to have been enforced, and yangban sons, who could study for years, won almost all the degrees. After the Japanese invasions of the 1590s the state sold titles and blank certificates of office for grain, and status was increasingly bought.
you: You study the Confucian classics for the examinations that open the road to office, your family's name stands in the local register of yangban lineages, and slaves work your land.
kid: Yangban were Korea's ruling families, whose sons studied for years to pass the government exams.

text: Between the yangban and the commoners stood the jungin, the "middle people": the hereditary technical specialists of the government and their families. Interpreters of Chinese, Mongolian, Jurchen and Japanese, physicians, astronomers, accountants and legal experts entered service through the miscellaneous examinations (japgwa), which the yangban looked down on: under their pressure the graduates' certificates were changed from crimson, the colour of the literary degree, to white, entitling them only to lower ranks. Court painters shared their standing, and in the provinces hereditary local clerks (hyangni) staffed the county offices under the magistrates sent from the capital. The sons of yangban by concubines (seoeol) were held to a similar station, barred from yangban family status, from the literary examinations and from high office. The jungin married mostly among themselves, lived in the centre of Seoul, and from the late 17th century wrote poetry and formed poetry societies of their own. One jungin woman, Jang Ok-jeong, became a consort of King Sukjong and was queen from 1689 to 1694.
rise: Status passed from father to son, and the miscellaneous examinations kept the technical posts within the same families. King Jeongjo opened a door in the late 18th century, appointing sons of concubines as scholars in his royal library, the Gyujanggak.
you: You serve the government as an interpreter or a physician, as your father did, after passing the miscellaneous examination that the yangban look down on, and it opens only the lower ranks to you.
kid: The "middle people" were Korea's interpreters, doctors and clerks, who passed their own special exams.

text: Most Koreans were commoners (sangmin), the free "good people" (yangin) of the law: farmers above all, with artisans, merchants and fishermen. They bore the state's burdens. Farmers paid the land tax and local tribute in kind and owed labour on public works, and every adult man owed military service or, increasingly, a tax in cotton cloth to support those who served; in 1750 King Yeongjo halved this military cloth tax from two bolts to one. Many farmed small fields of their own, while others rented land from yangban for half the crop. Each man carried a tag (hopae) recording his name and status. In law commoners could sit the examinations, but few could spare the years of study to pass them. Sumptuary laws kept them to plain white clothes of cotton, and they wore straw shoes or wooden clogs. In famine years some sold themselves into slavery to escape taxes and military service, while in the 18th century markets and commercial farming made other commoners rich.
rise: A rich commoner could buy a post, a title or a forged genealogy, and so many did that in the registers of Daegu nearly 70 per cent of the people were listed as yangban by 1858. Debt and famine pushed others into slavery.
you: You farm a few fields, pay the land tax and the local tribute, and owe the state military service or a tax in cotton cloth; the law lets you sit the examinations, but you cannot spare the years of study.
kid: Most Koreans were farmers, and by law ordinary people had to wear plain white clothes.

text: The nobi were the core of the lowborn (cheonmin), and in the 15th to 17th centuries, the system's height, they made up at least 30 per cent of the people. Status was hereditary, and the child of a mixed marriage generally took the lower status. State nobi (gongnobi) served government offices or paid a yearly levy; private nobi (sanobi), owned mostly by yangban, could be bought, sold, given away and inherited. Most private nobi lived apart from their owners, with houses, families, land and even nobi of their own, and owed their masters labour or the harvest of one of their plots, but their property reverted to the owner at death; others served in the master's household. Historians therefore still debate whether to call them slaves or serfs. Most had no surnames and many bore degrading names; runaways were severely punished, and an owner was not held responsible if a nobi died of a beating. Gisaeng, the female entertainers of the court and local offices, were government nobi.
rise: Nobi could buy their freedom, earn it by military service or receive it from the state, and many ran away. The system declined from the 18th century: most government nobi were freed in 1801, hereditary slavery was abolished in 1886 and all slavery with the Gabo Reforms of 1894.
you: You belong to a yangban family that can sell you or give you away; you farm a plot of your own apart from your master's house and hand him the harvest of another, but whatever you own goes back to him when you die.
kid: Enslaved people, called nobi, were owned by others and had to work for them; their children were born enslaved too.

text: Beside the nobi among the lowborn stood groups despised for their work rather than owned. The baekjeong descended from wandering groups of the Goryeo period, the yangsucheok and hwacheok, who made willow baskets, slaughtered animals and performed. King Sejong tried to settle them as ordinary farmers under the old name for commoners, baekjeong, putting them on the registers and giving them land, but neighbours and even officials went on shunning them, and the name itself became an insult. They lived apart as butchers, tanners, basket-makers and executioners, work that Buddhist ideas of pollution led others to avoid. Custom forbade them tiled roofs, silk clothes, leather shoes and the gat hat; outside their houses they wore bamboo hats, they had to humble themselves before commoners, and they were buried in separate graveyards. They were denied surnames and barred from the examinations. Shamans (mudang), who held rites for the villagers, and travelling entertainers shared the low status of the lowborn.
rise: There was almost no way out: outcast status was inherited, and the baekjeong could not sit the examinations. Their legal disabilities ended only with the Gabo Reforms of 1894, and discrimination lasted far longer.
you: You slaughter animals and tan their hides in a settlement set apart from your neighbours; outside your house you must wear a bamboo hat, you may not wear silk or live under a tiled roof, and you have no surname.
kid: Butchers and some other workers were treated as outcasts and had to live apart from other people.

== mobility
Joseon's ladder was steep and, in law, nearly closed. In theory the examinations were open to free men other than the baekjeong and sons of concubines, yet the yangban won almost every degree. A few rose by royal favour: Jang Yeong-sil, born a government nobi, became a court technician under King Sejong and built water clocks, and Sukbin Choe, a palace slave who carried water, became a royal consort and mother of King Yeongjo. War loosened the order. When the Japanese invaded in 1592, the Seoul offices holding the slave records were looted and burned, and the state, short of grain, sold rank and blank certificates of office and let nobi win freedom by paying or fighting. Status inflation followed: in the registers of Daegu the yangban rose to nearly 70 per cent of the people by 1858, while the nobi fell to about 1.5 per cent. Movement ran downwards too: commoners sold themselves into slavery in famines, yangban families without office sank into poverty, and some disaffected scholars turned to revolt, as Hong Gyeong-nae did in 1811.

== women
Women's lives narrowed under Neo-Confucian rule. In the early dynasty daughters still inherited a share of family property, but from the 17th century property and ancestral rites passed through the male line, and a married daughter was called "one who has left the family and become an outsider". Yangban women lived in the inner quarters and left the house by day only in a closed litter. A yangban widow could not remarry: the law barred the sons of remarried women from the literary examinations. A husband could divorce his wife on seven grounds, among them failing to bear a son; she could not divorce him. Commoner and enslaved women worked in the fields, and only a few callings were open to women: palace women, shamans, physicians and gisaeng. Some won lasting fame: the painter Sin Saimdang, mother of the philosopher Yi I; the gisaeng poet Hwang Jini; and the merchant Kim Man-deok, who gave her rice to the starving.
