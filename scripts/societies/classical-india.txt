title: Social Hierarchy of Classical India: From King to Enslaved
desc: Classical India's social ladder: the king, Brahmins, Buddhist and Jain monks, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Shudras, the Chandalas and the enslaved dasas.
intro: The Brahmanical law books of this age, the Dharmashastras such as the Manusmriti, ranked people in four varnas fixed by birth: Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas and Shudras, with groups such as the Chandalas placed outside them. This was a Brahmin ideal, and it was contested. Buddhist texts put the Kshatriyas first and described society as householders, and the Greek envoy Megasthenes, around 300 BCE, counted seven hereditary classes. Lived society was made of many jatis, groups born to an occupation that married among themselves. Birth and calling, more than wealth, set a person's rank, and the law books gave the three "twice-born" varnas rights that Shudras and outcastes lacked. Historians debate how rigid caste was, and how far British rule, much later, hardened it; genetic studies suggest that, at least in the north, marriage within one's group became the rule only in the first half of the first millennium CE.

text: The law books made kingship the duty of a Kshatriya: to protect his subjects, punish wrongdoers and uphold the order of the varnas, in return for a share of the harvest, traditionally a sixth. The Maurya kings ruled from Pataliputra with a council of ministers, sent princes to govern the provinces and kept a great standing army, by Megasthenes' count 600,000 infantry, 30,000 cavalry and 9,000 war elephants. Ashoka (r. c. 268–232 BCE) had edicts carved across his realm urging respect for Brahmins and ascetics and proper treatment of slaves and servants. The Gupta kings took the title maharajadhiraja, "great king of kings", and performed the Vedic horse sacrifice; the Allahabad Pillar Inscription praised Samudragupta as a conqueror and a poet. The Chinese pilgrim Faxian, in India around 400 under Chandragupta II, reported that the king ruled without executions: most criminals were only fined, and repeated rebels lost their right hand.
rise: Kingship was meant for Kshatriyas, but few dynasties of the age were Kshatriyas by birth: the Nandas were said to be Shudras, the Shungas and Kanvas were Brahmins, and the Gupta records never name their varna. From the 4th century Brahmins gave rising dynasties Kshatriya standing by tracing their descent to the solar and lunar lines of the epics.
you: You rule with a council of ministers and a standing army, take a share of every harvest and are expected to protect your subjects and the order of the varnas, while your sons govern provinces for you.
kid: Indian kings took part of every harvest as tax and were expected to protect their people.

text: The Brahmanical texts placed Brahmins first. With the Kshatriyas and Vaishyas they were "twice-born", initiated into the study of the Vedas, which were learned by heart; but only Brahmins could teach the Vedas, perform sacrifices for others and live on gifts. The Manusmriti told kings never to tax a learned Brahmin and never to put a Brahmin to death, whatever his crime. Megasthenes counted Brahmins among his "philosophers", few in number but the most honoured, exempt from public duties and hired to perform sacrifices and funeral rites. In practice many Brahmins lived as farmers or officials, and some as generals, and Buddhist texts show them in many trades. The Maurya kings patronised Buddhists, Jains and Ajivikas as well as Brahmins; the Guptas favoured Brahmins, performing Vedic sacrifices and granting them villages, with their revenue and free of tax, by charters engraved on copper plates. Brahmins wrote the law books, and Brahmin ministers served kings.
rise: In the law books Brahmin rank came by birth alone. A Brahmin in hard times was allowed to live by a Kshatriya's or a Vaishya's work, and one who committed a grievous sin could be cast out of his varna.
you: You were born a Brahmin: you study the Vedas, perform rites for others and live on their gifts, and a king may grant you the revenue of a village, free of tax.
kid: Brahmins were priests and teachers who learned the holy texts called the Vedas by heart.

text: Buddhist and Jain monks and nuns, and the ascetics of smaller orders such as the Ajivikas, left household life and stood outside the varna order: the Buddhist order took people of every varna, though not slaves or debtors. Ashoka's edicts paired "Brahmins and shramanas" as the holy men to be honoured, and Megasthenes counted ascetics with the Brahmins among his "philosophers". Monks and nuns shaved their heads, wore robes and owned little beyond them and an alms bowl; Buddhist monks lived by a code of more than two hundred rules, kept celibate and ate only before noon. Kings, merchants and guilds gave the orders caves, monasteries and land: Ashoka gave the Ajivikas caves in the Barabar Hills and, by Sri Lankan tradition, sent his son Mahinda and his daughter Sanghamitta to take Buddhism to Sri Lanka. Under the Guptas, who favoured Brahmins but tolerated other faiths, great monasteries such as Nalanda became centres of learning, though the Huna king Mihirakula destroyed many others in the early 6th century.
rise: Anyone free of debt and servitude could take the robe, whatever their varna, and a monk or nun could leave the order and return to household life.
you: You have left your family, shaved your head and taken the robe; you own little more than your robes and an alms bowl, eat only before noon and live by the rules of your order.
kid: Buddhist and Jain monks and nuns gave up their homes to live simply and study their faith.

text: The Kshatriyas were the warrior and ruling varna. The law books told them to protect the people, fight and govern, to study the Vedas, sacrifice and give gifts, but not to teach the Vedas or live on gifts. Kingship belonged to them in theory, and the clans that ruled the old republics of the Ganges plain, such as the Licchavis, called themselves Kshatriyas and were patrons of Buddhism and Jainism. Buddhist texts placed the Kshatriyas above the Brahmins. Kshatriya lineages held land, commanded armies and governed provinces, and after the Mauryas many clan states in Punjab and Rajasthan claimed Kshatriya status. Brahmin writers counted the foreign conquerors, the Greeks (Yavanas) and Shakas, as Kshatriyas who had fallen to the rank of Shudras by neglecting the rites. Megasthenes described a hereditary class of soldiers, the second most numerous, paid by the state and idle in peacetime, but the Arthashastra expected troops of every varna.
rise: Kshatriya rank came by birth, but success could claim it: from the 4th century Brahmins gave newly risen royal families Kshatriya genealogies, while a Kshatriya people that abandoned the rites could be counted as fallen.
you: You were born to a warrior lineage: the law books tell you to fight, govern and protect, to study the Vedas and give gifts, and you may command troops or govern a province.
kid: Kshatriyas were warriors and rulers whose duty was to protect the people.

text: The Vaishyas were the third varna and, like the two above them, twice-born, entitled to study the Vedas. The law books gave them farming, herding cattle, trade and lending money, though the Manusmriti spends only a handful of verses on them and the Shudras. Over these centuries the name came to mean above all merchants and moneylenders, who prospered on the trade of the Ganges cities and the ports. Traders and craftsmen formed guilds (shrenis) with their own laws, which the Manusmriti told kings to respect. Guilds took deposits, lent money and paid for monuments: an inscription credits the ivory carvers of Vidisha with work on the southern gateway of the great stupa at Sanchi. Under the Guptas a district governor was advised by a council of the chief merchant, the chief caravan trader, the chief artisan and the chief scribe. Many merchants followed Buddhism or Jainism, and Faxian saw the heads of Vaishya families running houses that gave food and medicine to the poor and sick.
rise: The varna came by birth, but trade could make a family rich and powerful: guild heads sat on councils and advised kings, and some historians think the Gupta dynasty itself was of Vaishya origin.
you: You trade or lend money, belong to a guild that has its own laws and takes deposits, and as one of the twice-born you may study the Vedas.
kid: Vaishyas were traders, bankers and farmers, and many of them belonged to guilds.

text: The Shudras were the fourth varna, born, the law books said, to serve the three twice-born varnas. They were barred from studying the Vedas and from Vedic sacrifice, and a Shudra was punished more harshly for offences against a person of higher varna. The Manusmriti held that a Shudra was created for servitude, should not pile up wealth and might have his goods taken by a Brahmin. Yet the texts disagree with one another, and Shudras were freer of the many ritual rules that bound the twice-born. The Arthashastra counted Shudras among the free Aryas, who could not be sold into slavery, and allowed them to serve as soldiers. In practice Shudras were peasants, artisans, labourers and servants, and by the 7th century the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang described the Shudras as the farmers. Some grew rich, and the Nanda kings who ruled Magadha before the Mauryas were said to be Shudras.
rise: Birth fixed the varna in the texts, but some Shudras grew rich and powerful, and an outcaste group that took up farming could be absorbed into the Shudras.
you: You farm, labour or work at a craft; the law books say you were born to serve the twice-born and may not study the Vedas, but they leave you freer of their ritual rules.
kid: Shudras were farmers, workers and craftspeople, and old law books said they should serve the higher groups.

text: Below and outside the four varnas the texts placed groups they held to be impure, above all the Chandalas, along with the Shvapachas and peoples such as the Nishadas. The law books explained the Chandala as the child of a Shudra father and a Brahmin mother, the most condemned of mixed unions. The Manusmriti ordered that Chandalas live outside the village, own only dogs and donkeys, wear the clothes of the dead and eat from broken dishes, and gave them the work of carrying away the corpses of people with no kin and executing condemned criminals. Faxian, around 400, found that the Chandalas lived apart; when they entered a town or a market they struck a piece of wood so that others could avoid touching them, and only they hunted, fished and sold meat. Historians such as Vivekanand Jha see untouchability spreading over these centuries and reaching its height after 600; Patrick Olivelle notes that the law books speak of the impurity of individuals rather than of whole varnas.
rise: The texts made the status hereditary, but over generations groups could move: a forest people that settled to farming could be absorbed among the Shudras.
you: You live outside the village, carry away the dead or hunt and fish for a living, and when you enter a town or a market you must strike a piece of wood so that others can avoid your touch.
kid: Chandalas were made to live apart from others and did work such as carrying away the dead.

text: Slavery existed throughout these centuries, but historians such as Romila Thapar note that slaves were not the main labour force, as they were in Greece; most served in households as servants, nurses and attendants, and some worked the land beside hired labourers. Megasthenes thought India had no slaves, perhaps because slaves there could buy back their freedom. Ashoka's edicts urged proper treatment of slaves and servants, and his edict on the Kalinga war records 150,000 people deported. The Manusmriti lists seven kinds of slave: the war captive, the one who serves for food, the house-born, the bought, the given, the inherited and the one enslaved as punishment. The Arthashastra forbade selling a free Arya into slavery, though a man in distress might pledge himself. It let slaves keep their earnings and inheritance, and an owner who abused a slave, or forced himself on a slave woman, lost the price he had paid; she went free.
rise: Freedom came by the owner's grant or by paying one's price, a right the Arthashastra recognised; a free man pledged for a debt was released when it was repaid.
you: You were captured in war, born in the house or bought; you serve your owner's household, but you may keep what you earn and, if you can pay your price, buy back your freedom.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay, often as servants in homes.

== mobility
In theory birth fixed a person's place, but power was open to newcomers. Chandragupta Maurya, whose origins are obscure, overthrew the Nanda kings, who were themselves said to be Shudras. Around 185 BCE the Brahmin general Pushyamitra Shunga killed the last Maurya king and founded his own dynasty, and the Gupta records never name the family's varna. Brahmins gave rising royal families Kshatriya genealogies, and the law books fitted foreign conquerors, Greeks and Shakas, into the scheme as fallen Kshatriyas. New groups formed as the state grew: the Kayasthas, scribes and record-keepers, appear as officials in Gupta inscriptions. Wealth mattered too: guild heads sat on councils, and some Shudras grew rich. The Buddhist and Jain orders took members of every varna, though the Buddhists refused slaves and debtors, and a forest people that settled to farming could join the Shudras. Falls were real as well: the law books cast out of their varna those who committed grievous sins. Genetic and textual evidence suggests that the barriers hardened over these centuries.

== women
A woman's rank followed her father's and then her husband's varna. The law books placed her under the protection of father, husband and sons, and the Manusmriti declared that a woman should never be independent, though it let her own property, such as wedding gifts; Tim Dyson sees women's rights declining in the Indo-Aryan heartland from Mauryan times. Royal women could hold real power: the Licchavi princess Kumaradevi appears beside her husband Chandragupta I on Gupta gold coins, and Chandragupta II's daughter Prabhavatigupta ruled the Vakataka kingdom as regent for about twenty years from around 390, issuing land grants in her own name. Buddhist nuns had an order of their own, though bound by more rules than monks, and tradition says Ashoka's daughter Sanghamitta took it to Sri Lanka. Megasthenes noted women studying philosophy among the ascetics. Widow-burning was rare: the first Indian inscription recording one, at Eran, dates from 510. Enslaved women served in royal households.
