title: Social Hierarchy of the Delhi Sultanate: From Sultan to Enslaved
desc: The Tughluq sultanate's social ladder: sultan, nobles, royal slaves, scholars, Hindu chiefs, Brahmins, soldiers, townspeople, peasants and the enslaved.
intro: Under the Tughluq sultans (1320–1413) the Delhi Sultanate reached its greatest extent and then broke apart. People were ranked by their place in the sultan's service, by their faith and by whether they were free. At the top stood the sultan and a military nobility of khans, maliks and amirs, many of them immigrants from Iran and Central Asia, who drew the land revenue of districts assigned to them. In the eyes of the sultanate's chroniclers the Muslims were the people of the state, although most of them were Indians who had converted. Non-Muslims were classed as dhimmis and owed the jizya, yet the sultans relied on Hindu chiefs and village officials to collect the revenue. Slavery reached far up the ladder as well as down it: Firuz Shah Tughluq is said to have owned 180,000 slaves, who served him as guards, officials and craftsmen, while war and unpaid taxes drove many more into bondage.

text: The sultan granted and confirmed the iqtas that paid his nobles, and for many Muslim observers his rule was justified by the protection and advancement of the faith. Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq, a frontier commander, took the throne in 1320 by defeating and killing Khusrau Khan. His son Muhammad bin Tughluq (1325–1351) ruled the widest realm any sultan held, but his schemes cost him dearly: a forced move of the Muslim elite to Daulatabad, coins of base metal that people forged at home, and revolts from 1327 on. Firuz Shah Tughluq (1351–1388) ruled for 37 years; in his memoir he wrote that he had banned torture such as amputation and the tearing out of eyes.
rise: No fixed rule decided the succession. Ghiyath al-Din seized the throne in 1320, and some historians, among them Vincent Smith, hold that his son killed him in 1325; after Firuz Shah's death the nobles and his slave guard made and unmade sultans.

text: The great men of the sultanate bore the titles khan and malik, and the elite of its early years were overwhelmingly immigrants from Iran and Central Asia: Persians, Turks, Ghuris and Khalaj. Most were paid with an iqta, an assignment of the land revenue of a territory. Its holder, the muqti, collected the revenue, paid his troops from it, sent the surplus to the treasury and, in theory, had no further claim on the peasants. Muhammad bin Tughluq set a separate officer over the administration and paid soldiers from the treasury, and he cruelly punished nobles who would not move to Daulatabad, earning a hatred they long remembered. Firuz Shah made the iqtas hereditary to please the nobles.
rise: Office and revenue came from the sultan, and in theory an iqta was not inherited until Firuz Shah made it so. Birth still counted: when the frontier commanders Jalaluddin Khalji and Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq reached for the throne, they found little support because of their un-noble origins.
you: You hold the revenue of a district the sultan has assigned to you, pay your horsemen from it and send the rest to Delhi; under Firuz Shah your son may inherit it.
kid: Nobles called khans and maliks collected the taxes of lands the sultan gave them and used the money to pay soldiers.

text: Slaves in the sultan's service could stand above most free people. The sultanate's army had begun as the Turkic slave soldiers of Muhammad of Ghor, its nucleus were household cavalry archers of slave origin, and the sultans bought Turks to build a corps of mounted archers whose loyalty was to their master, not to any people. Firuz Shah Tughluq vastly increased the slaves in his own service and in that of the Muslim nobles. They were converted to Islam, taught to read and memorise the Quran and employed in many offices, above all in the army, and about 12,000 of them were skilled artisans. Known as the Ghulaman-i-Firuz Shahi, they formed an elite guard that became influential in the state.
rise: Men entered the corps by capture or purchase. Their service could carry them far: after Firuz Shah's death his old slaves turned against Sultan Abu Bakr Shah and had Nasir ud-Din Muhammad Shah placed on the throne, and under the Khaljis the slave general Malik Kafur, born to a Hindu family, had held power himself in 1316.
you: You were enslaved, made a Muslim and taught the Quran, and now serve in the sultan's guard, whose leaders will one day put a sultan of their choosing on the throne.
kid: Some enslaved boys were trained to be soldiers and officials for the sultan, and his slave guard became very powerful.

text: The sultanate followed the Hanafi school of Islamic law, and its judges and learned men wore ample gowns and an Arabic robe, the durra, unlike the soldiers and clerks, who wore the qaba coat. Muhammad bin Tughluq ordered the sayyids, shaikhs and ulama, with the nobles, to move to Daulatabad, seeing them as propagandists of his empire and hoping the Sufis would win converts in the Deccan. Sufi saints were honoured with great tombs: the first major monument of the Tughluq age, built in Multan between 1320 and 1324, was the tomb of the saint Shah Rukn-e-Alam. Strict chroniclers such as Ziauddin Barani urged harsher treatment of non-Muslims, and Firuz Shah acted against Shi'a and other sects he judged heretical.
rise: Learning and descent made a scholar: sayyids claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad. The sultan's favour decided the rest, and Firuz Shah would not tolerate preachers of sects he held to be deviant.
you: You teach Hanafi law or keep a Sufi lodge, and when the sultan orders the learned to settle in the Deccan you must go with them, to win converts there.
kid: Muslim scholars and judges taught and used Islamic law, and Sufi holy men were honoured with great tombs.

text: The sultanate claimed to be the paramount power rather than the only one, as earlier Hindu kingdoms had done. It left some conquered Hindu rulers their autonomy and their armies, and took Hindu vassals and officials freely into its service. In the countryside it relied on Hindu subordinate elites, such as the rawats and the patwaris, to collect the land revenue, and the Tughluqs employed many Hindus in their corps of architects and builders. The chiefs' loyalty was never assured. By 1339 Hindu kings in the south had revolted and declared their independence, the Vijayanagara Empire arose in answer to the sultanate's attacks, and the Kangra State defeated the army Muhammad bin Tughluq sent over the Himalayas.
rise: A chief kept his rank by submitting and paying, and lost it by defeat; a rising, as in the south in the 1330s, could make him independent again.

text: The towns held the sultanate's merchants, craftsmen and labourers. Their trade lived under close control: Alauddin Khalji had replaced the private markets with four state markets, fixed the price of everything and required merchants to register, and Barani wrote that even after the controls were lifted on his death in 1316 people feared his spies and avoided trading in costly goods. Muhammad bin Tughluq's coins of base metal (1329–1332) failed because people minted their own at home. New machines came in from the Islamic world, such as geared water-wheels, and the earliest clear reference to a spinning wheel in India dates from 1350. Hindustani, or Hindavi, took shape as the common speech of the period.
rise: Migrants fleeing the Mongol raids on Central and West Asia brought traders, artists and artisans into the towns. War could also carry artisans away: in 1398 Timur's army massacred the people of Delhi and took skilled craftsmen to Samarkand as slaves.

text: The peasants paid for the sultanate. Alauddin Khalji had raised the tax on harvests from a fifth to half and limited the grain cultivators could keep for themselves. Their revenue went to the iqta holders, who in theory could claim nothing more from them once it was paid, and it was collected through local Hindu officials. Non-Muslim men owed the jizya too, from which Hanafi jurists exempted women, children, the old and the poor. After Muhammad bin Tughluq's failed coinage, famine, poverty and rebellion spread across the kingdom. Firuz Shah dug a canal from the Yamuna to steady the food supply, offered converts freedom from taxes and the jizya, and raised the jizya on those who stayed Hindu.
rise: Conversion lightened the tax under Firuz Shah, while a village that resisted or failed to pay could be enslaved: Balban had ordered it in Awadh, and Alauddin Khalji made it lawful for revenue defaulters.

People were enslaved in war and through the revenue system: the sultans and their officers had their armies seize the people of villages that resisted or did not pay, and Alauddin Khalji made it lawful to enslave those who defaulted on the jizya and land tax. == mobility
The sultanate's ladder was open at the top to soldiers and slaves. Its first rulers were slave generals: Qutb ud-Din Aibak, a former slave of Muhammad of Ghor, became the first sultan in 1206. Under the Khaljis Malik Kafur, born to a Hindu family, and Khusrau Khan, another slave general of Indian origin, held power, and Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq rose from frontier commander to sultan in 1320. Firuz Shah's slave guard ended by choosing sultans. Conversion was the other road: most Muslims in India were Indians who had converted, Firuz Shah rewarded converts with freedom from taxes and the jizya and with presents and honours, and his grand vizier, Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul, was a Telugu Muslim. Migration kept the elite changing, as Mongol raids on Central and West Asia drove soldiers, scholars, mystics, traders and artisans into India. Downward the roads were short and steep: a village that failed to pay could be enslaved, and nobles who refused Muhammad bin Tughluq's order to move to Daulatabad were cruelly punished.

== women
Women's lives followed their family's rank and faith. In the century before the Tughluqs a woman had ruled: Razia Sultana reigned from 1236 to 1240, one of the few women to rule in the Islamic world, though nobles and clergy resented a female monarch, and in 1236 Shah Turkan had run the government for her son Rukn ud-Din Firuz. Alauddin Khalji forbade marriages between noble families, to keep the nobility from combining against him. Under Hanafi law women were exempt from the jizya. Enslaved women bore children to Muslim masters, and those children were raised as Muslims, and non-Muslim women whom Muslim soldiers and nobles had taken converted to Islam to escape rejection by their own communities. In 1398 Timur's army carried women and children off from Delhi into slavery.
