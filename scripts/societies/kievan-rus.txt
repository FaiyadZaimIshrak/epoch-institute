intro: Kievan Rus' was ruled by one family, the descendants of Rurik. The grand prince of Kiev held the lands around the city, and his formally subordinate relatives ruled the other cities and paid him tribute. Every prince kept a druzhina, a retinue of warriors whose senior members, the boyars, held the high offices and advised him. The law code of the 11th century, the Russkaya Pravda, ranked everyone else by what it allowed and what it charged: a community paid more for killing one of the prince's men, the fine for killing a woman was half the usual one, a smerd's killer paid no more than a kholop's, and a kholop could be killed or sold by his master. The towns grew rich on trade with Byzantium, the Church came with Vladimir's baptism, and most people farmed.

text: The grand prince of Kiev controlled the lands around the city, while his formally subordinate relatives ruled the other cities and paid him tribute. He gave the land its law: Yaroslav the Wise (r. 1019–1054) wrote the first part of the Russkaya Pravda, and his sons and his grandson Vladimir Monomakh added to it. The prince's court judged cases, and the fines of the Extensive Pravda went to the prince and his administration, with less compensation for the victims. In winter the ruler of Kiev went on rounds among the subject tribes, who paid tribute in money, furs or slaves. Rivalry within the family was constant, and princes killed their own kin to gain the throne.
rise: After Yaroslav's death in 1054 no settled rule of succession took hold: the throne passed from elder to younger brother, from uncle to nephew and from father to son, and the princes fought over it. A defeat could cost a grand prince his city: after the Cumans beat the princes on the Alta River in 1068, Iziaslav fled to Poland to seek support and returned in a couple of years.

text: Rurik's descendants shared power over the land. Junior members of the dynasty usually began as rulers of a minor district, moved on to richer principalities and then competed for the throne of Kiev. Yaroslav the Wise assigned his sons principalities in order of seniority: Kiev and Novgorod to the eldest, Iziaslav; Chernigov and Tmutarakan to Sviatoslav Yaroslavich; Pereyaslavl and Rostov-Suzdal to Vsevolod; Smolensk to Vyacheslav and Volhynia to Igor. The order did not hold. The princes of Polotsk contested the grand prince's power by occupying Novgorod, and the branches of the family, the sons of Oleg in Chernigov, of Monomakh in Pereyaslavl and of Iziaslav in Turov–Volhynia, fought one another, until the princes met at Liubech in 1097 to seek an understanding.
rise: Princely rank came only by birth into the dynasty, and a prince's place in the order of seniority decided which city he could hope for. A prince could also lose his lands: by 1130 Mstislav the Great had exiled all the descendants of Vseslav of Polotsk to the Byzantine Empire.

text: The boyars were the senior members of the prince's druzhina and the highest nobility of Rus', second only to the princes. They wielded power through their military support of the princes, and their power and prestige came to depend almost completely on service, on a family history of service and, to a lesser extent, on land. They held the highest offices, military, domestic and urban, and through a council (duma) they advised the grand prince; they received extensive grants of land and were the chief legislators of Kievan Rus'. In appearance they were very like knights. Their loyalty was to the prince in person, so a druzhina could, to a degree, choose whom to support.
rise: A boyar's standing came from service to a prince and from his family's record of service, rewarded with offices and land. Because the bond was personal it could break: when Boris refused the throne of Kiev that his father's druzhina offered him in 1015, the druzhina dispersed, and his brother Sviatopolk's agents murdered him.
you: You are a senior man of the prince's druzhina: you sit in his council, command in his wars and run a town for him, and he rewards you with land, but your fortune lasts only as long as you and he keep faith with each other.
kid: Boyars were the prince's chief warriors and advisers, and he rewarded them with land.

text: Below the boyars stood the rank and file of the druzhina, the "youths" of the junior retinue, and the prince's servants. At its core the druzhina was the prince's permanent bodyguard; more broadly it was his household, his court. A wise prince was expected to keep it close, feast with it, consult it and reward it. It had its own hierarchy and offices, and military and administrative work ran together: the "swordman" (mechnik) also had a part in gathering tribute. The law of Yaroslav's sons made a community answer more heavily for killing the prince's men on its land: his tiuns, privileged servants of princes and boyars; his starostas, low-ranking administrators; his otroks, low-ranking soldiers; and his other servants.
rise: A prince recruited where he could: free young warriors, Varangians hired for his wars, as Yaroslav brought them to Novgorod, and kholops, mainly house serfs, who filled the ranks of the princely servants, including the military.

text: Most people of Rus' were peasants: the Slavs were above all farmers, growing cereals and raising livestock. In the law they were smerds, a word from a root meaning "ordinary man" or "dependent man". Free at first, the smerds gradually lost their freedom, in part or wholly, and their legal status differed from group to group. Unlike slaves they had their own property and paid fines for their offences, yet they never held full rights: a smerd's killer paid the same fine as a kholop's, and the prince inherited a dead smerd's property. The Pravda forbade torturing a smerd under examination in court without the prince's consent. Their villages formed communes (verv, hromada) within a volost.
rise: Debt pulled smerds down: a peasant who took a loan became a zakup until he repaid it, and one sold for his debts became a kholop.
you: You grow grain and keep cattle in a village commune; you own what you farm and pay your own fines, but the law values your life no higher than a kholop's and gives the prince your property when you die.

A master had unlimited power over a kholop: he could kill him, sell him or hand him over to settle a debt, though he answered for his kholop's deeds, such as insulting a free man or stealing. People became kholops through capture, by selling themselves, by being sold for their debts, after committing crimes, or by marrying a kholop. Slaves were also a trade good: the Rus' floated them down the Dnieper to Byzantium, and some subject tribes paid their tribute in slaves. == mobility
Rank in Kievan Rus' rested above all on birth and service. Only Rurik's descendants could be princes, and among them seniority and war decided who ruled which city. Service to a prince brought a boyar his offices and his grants of land, and the prince's household took in free warriors, hired Varangians and even kholops, mainly house serfs. Trade made fortunes in the towns. The strongest current ran downward, through debt: a peasant who took a loan became a zakup, and a man who sold himself, or was sold for his debts, became a kholop. The law moved with unrest: the Pravda of Yaroslav's sons laid down severe punishments for arson and for collective encroachment on rich people's property, new provisions are believed to have been added after the revolts in Kiev, Novgorod and the Rostov-Suzdal land in 1068–1071, and Vladimir Monomakh's code followed the Kiev uprising of 1113. Over the following centuries the smerds lost more of their freedom: in Novgorod and Pskov in the 14th and 15th centuries they were forbidden to seek a new master.

== women
The Russkaya Pravda valued a woman's life at half a man's: if the victim of a killing was a woman, the fine was the poluvir'ye, half the usual vyra. Yet women had certain rights, among them to own and to inherit property. Princesses were the dynasty's links to the rest of Europe. Olga had ruled Kiev as regent for her son Sviatoslav after 945 and converted to Christianity; Yaroslav the Wise married his sister and three daughters to the kings of Poland, France, Hungary and Norway, and his granddaughter Eupraxia, the daughter of Vsevolod, married the emperor Henry IV. At the bottom of society, marriage to a kholop made a free woman, or man, a kholop too.
