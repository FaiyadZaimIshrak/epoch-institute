title: Social Hierarchy of Han China: From Emperor to Enslaved
desc: Han China's social ladder: the emperor, kings and marquesses, scholar-officials, great landed families, farmers, artisans, merchants and the enslaved.
intro: Han China ranked its people by the state's measure. At the top stood the emperor and his kin, the kings and marquesses; below them the officials, chosen by recommendation and, from the time of Emperor Wu, schooled in the Confucian classics. Every free man could also hold one of twenty ranks of honour, granted by the emperor, which brought privileges at law and in the village. In the usual Confucian scheme of four occupations, scholars came first, then farmers, artisans and, last, merchants. This was an ideal rather than a legal code, though some laws did single out merchants, who were barred from office and from wearing silk yet could grow richer than nobles. Most people were small farmers who paid taxes, and their men gave a month's labour each year and served a term as soldiers. Slaves, perhaps one person in a hundred, stood at the bottom.

text: The emperor (huangdi), the Son of Heaven (tianzi), was always a man of the Liu family descended from the founder, Liu Bang. In theory there were no limits to his power: he was supreme judge and commander-in-chief, he alone could make or repeal laws, and he alone appointed every official paid 600 bushels or more. Subjects could not speak his name and addressed him indirectly as bixia, "beneath the steps" of the throne, and anyone who entered the palace without permission could be put to death. In practice he governed with his ministers, whose court conferences an emperor overruled at his peril. Many emperors came to the throne as children and were ruled by regents, often the empress dowager, the emperor's mother, who could issue edicts and, if he died without an heir, choose the next emperor. Wang Mang, a regent from such a consort family, took the throne himself in 9 CE; the Han was restored in 25.
rise: The throne passed to the son the emperor named heir apparent or, failing one, to a prince the empress dowager chose. Liu Bang himself had been a commoner who rose in the revolts against the Qin.
you: You are the Son of Heaven, whose name no subject may speak and who alone makes and repeals the laws, but if you come to the throne as a child, your mother's family may rule in your name.
kid: The Han emperor was called the Son of Heaven, and people were not allowed to say his name.

text: Below the emperor stood the kings, his brothers, uncles, nephews and sons other than the heir. Liu Bang first gave kingdoms to his generals, but by 196 BCE all the kings save one were his kin, and an edict reserved kingdoms for the Liu. In the early Western Han the kingdoms covered two-thirds of the empire and their kings appointed most of their own officials. After the Rebellion of the Seven States in 154 BCE the court cut their powers: from 145 BCE they could appoint no official above 400 bushels and became nominal lords, living on a share of their kingdom's taxes. Below them were the marquesses, the top two of the twenty ranks: a full marquess (liehou) drew part of the taxes of a fief the size of a county, an ordinary marquess only a pension. Kings' sons, princesses' husbands and high officials were made marquesses, and a marquess of a thousand households drew some 200,000 cash a year.
rise: Kingdoms passed to the king's eldest son by his queen, but from 127 BCE all a king's sons were made marquesses with fiefs from his lands, so kingdoms shrank with each generation. A king who rebelled, or even murdered a slave, could lose his kingdom.
you: You are a king of the Liu clan, ranked just below the emperor and living on a share of your kingdom's taxes, while officials appointed by the emperor govern your lands.
kid: Han kings were usually relatives of the emperor, and they lived on taxes from the lands they were given.

text: Those who served in government held a privileged place just below the nobles. An official could not be arrested without the emperor's leave, and one condemned to death was often allowed to take his own life instead. Officials were graded by salary in bushels of grain, about half paid in coin: the three highest ministers drew 10,000 bushels, the Nine Ministers 2,000 and the lowest clerks 100. Men entered office by recommendation. From 134 BCE, on Dong Zhongshu's advice, Emperor Wu had every commandery send men judged "filial and incorrupt" (xiaolian) to the capital each year, and in 124 BCE he founded the Imperial Academy (Taixue) to teach the Confucian classics; it had fifty students at first and some 30,000 by the 2nd century CE. Office was open in principle to educated commoners other than merchants, but in the Eastern Han the same families filled the high posts: a third of the officials with biographies in the Book of Later Han were sons or grandsons of officials.
rise: A young man rose by study, a patron's recommendation or service as a clerk in his commandery. Wealth could also buy office when the court sold posts, as it did on a large scale under Emperor Ling (168–189).
you: You have studied the Confucian classics and been recommended as filial and incorrupt; now you draw a salary in grain and coin and cannot be arrested without the emperor's leave.
kid: Many Han officials studied the books of Confucius and were chosen for being good sons and honest men.

text: Below the officials, and often overlapping with them, stood the great landed families. Land could be bought and sold, and rich families, many founded by officials or merchants, built up large estates worked by tenants who paid about half their harvest as rent, along with hired labourers and a few slaves. They kept retainers, guests who lived on the estate and served as advisers, bodyguards or fighters. The Western Han court feared them: from Liu Bang's reign thousands of noble and wealthy families were moved by force to the region of the capital, and attempts to limit landholding under Emperor Ai and Wang Mang failed. The Eastern Han, restored with the help of great landholding families, relied on them, and their sons filled the offices: for 46 of the 110 years from 86 to 196 CE a member of the Yuan or Yang clan held one of the three highest posts. When the state weakened, landless peasants became their tenants and retainers.
rise: Families rose through office or trade and bought land with the profits. The court could resettle them or confiscate their estates, but by the late Western Han, Cho-yun Hsu argues, moving them by force had become unthinkable.
you: Your family owns wide fields worked by tenants who hand over half their harvest, keeps armed retainers, and sends its sons into office.
kid: Rich Han families owned large farms, and poorer families rented their fields and gave them about half the harvest.

text: Farmers were the great majority and, in Confucian eyes, the "root" of the state, second only to scholars. In the Western Han most were small owner-cultivators, ranked above tenants and hired labourers: by the official Chao Cuo's reckoning a family of five farmed no more than about 100 mu, some 11 acres. The land tax was light, a thirtieth of the crop for most of the dynasty, but adults paid a poll tax of 120 coins a year, and men aged fifteen to fifty-six owed a month's labour each year on public works. Until conscription was largely abandoned after 30 CE, each man also trained a year as a soldier and served a year on the frontier or in the capital. Debt, floods and taxes drove many to sell their land, even their children, and become tenants paying half the crop; by the late Eastern Han most peasants were landless. Their distress fed risings such as the Red Eyebrows (about 18 CE) and the Yellow Turbans (184).
rise: Any free man could earn ranks of honour, granted on great occasions or bought with grain, that brought a better seat at village feasts, lighter punishments and relief from labour service. Study could carry a farmer's son into office.
you: You farm a few acres with your family, pay a poll tax in coin and a small share of your grain, and give the state a month's labour every year; one day you may be sent to guard the frontier.
kid: Most people in Han China were farmers, and men had to work for the government for one month each year.

text: Artisans stood between farmers and merchants in the Confucian order. Scholars thought their work less essential than farming, since the state lived on grain, but did not despise it, and the law treated artisans better than merchants: they could wear silk, ride horses and carriages, and hold office. Some worked for themselves or for great households; others worked in government workshops, where master craftsmen were well paid to make bronze mirrors, lacquerware, silks and weapons, while convicts, conscripted labourers and state slaves did the heavy work. Han craftsmen were highly skilled: lacquered cups and boxes went out as luxury goods, a pair of metal calipers bears the very day it was made, and in 105 CE Cai Lun, director of the imperial workshops, presented an improved way of making paper. Skills were handed down by word of mouth from father to son.
rise: Skill could make an artisan prosperous, and no law kept him from office, though few took that path: one painter who worked at the Imperial Academy turned down repeated nominations.
you: You cast mirrors or lacquer cups in a workshop, may wear silk and ride a horse as no registered merchant may, and earn well if you become a master of your craft.
kid: Han craftspeople made shiny lacquer cups, bronze mirrors and fine silk cloth.

text: Merchants came last of the four occupations, scorned by scholars as parasites, yet many were rich. Registered merchants, mostly small shopkeepers of the city markets, paid commercial taxes and a double poll tax of 240 coins, had to wear white, and from Liu Bang's reign were barred from wearing silk, riding horses and holding office. They could be singled out for conscription and sent to settle the malarial south, and in 119 BCE Emperor Wu forbade them to buy land. Great traders who moved goods between cities often escaped registration, and a fortune of ten million cash, fifty times a marquess's yearly income, marked a great merchant. To break the salt and iron magnates, some of whom employed a thousand peasants or more, Emperor Wu nationalised both industries by 117 BCE and put former merchants such as Sang Hongyang in charge. Trade along the new Silk Road carried Chinese silk to Central Asia and India.
rise: Merchants bought land with their profits despite the bans, and a few entered office when the court needed their skills, as under Emperor Wu, though an edict of 7 BCE again barred them from the bureaucracy.
you: You may grow richer than a marquess trading salt, iron or silk, but as a registered merchant you must wear white, pay a double poll tax and may never hold office.
kid: Some Han merchants grew very rich, but laws kept many of them from wearing silk or working for the government.

text: Slaves (nubi) were few, perhaps one person in a hundred, far fewer than in the Roman world. Some belonged to the state: prisoners of war, the families of men convicted of treason, gifts from foreign rulers and slaves confiscated from their owners, put to work in palaces, offices, workshops and stables. Others were privately owned, often peasants who had sold themselves for debt; they cooked, served, guarded their masters or performed as musicians, dancers and acrobats, and fewer worked the fields, where tenants were probably cheaper. Owners paid a poll tax of 240 coins a year on each slave, and slaves' children were born slaves. Masters could beat their slaves but not kill them: kings lost their kingdoms for murdering slaves. The emperor could free state slaves for old age or merit, and private slaves could buy their freedom. In 9 CE Wang Mang banned the buying and selling of slaves, but the ban was lifted in 12.
rise: Freedom came by the emperor's grant, a master's choice or purchase. In one exceptional case the former slave Jin Midi rose to be one of the regents of the empire.
you: You belong to a master who pays a tax on you each year; he may beat you but may not kill you, your children will be born slaves, and only your master or the emperor can set you free.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay; in Han China there were few of them.

== mobility
Han society was more open than the four occupations suggest, above all in the Western Han, when, Patricia Ebrey notes, office and promotion were open to a wider part of the people than later. The founder Liu Bang was a commoner, and Gongsun Hong, a former pig-breeder, became Emperor Wu's chancellor. Wei Qing, the son of a servant woman in a princess's household, worked as a sheepherder and stableboy before his half-sister Wei Zifu became Emperor Wu's empress; he rose to be Grand General and led the armies against the Xiongnu. Recommendation as filial and incorrupt and study at the Imperial Academy carried educated commoners into office, ranks of honour rewarded ordinary men, and merchants turned their fortunes into land. Falls were as common: debt turned owner-farmers into tenants or slaves, and the emperor could strip a noble of his fief or an official of his life. By the Eastern Han great families had closed ranks, and the same clans filled the high offices.

== women
A Han woman was expected to obey her father, then her husband, then her adult son; the historian Ban Zhao wrote in her Lessons for Women that a woman's great virtues were respect and compliance. Practice was looser. Mothers outranked their sons, an empress could give orders to her own father, and an empress dowager could rule for a child emperor and choose his successor. Sons divided the family property equally, while daughters received dowries, usually smaller but sometimes as large as a brother's share. Women could sometimes initiate divorce, and remarriage, though frowned upon, was common, and a law of 189 BCE raised the poll tax fivefold on unmarried women aged fifteen to thirty. Women were exempt from labour service but worked hard: they raised children and wove cloth for the family and the market, sometimes in spinning groups or textile businesses employing hundreds of women, and some became physicians, merchants, singers or sorceresses.
