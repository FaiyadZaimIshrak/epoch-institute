title: Social Hierarchy of the Khmer Empire: From God-King to Slave
desc: The Khmer Empire's social ladder at Angkor: the god-king, nobles, Brahmins and monks, traders, rice farmers, temple servants and slaves, as Zhou Daguan saw it.
intro: Khmer society was arranged in a hierarchy that reflected the Hindu caste system. At its head stood a king whom the cult of the devaraja, the "god-king", identified with Shiva or Vishnu, or under Jayavarman VII (r. 1181–1219) with the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara, and whose temples, Angkor Wat and the Bayon, celebrated his divine rule on earth. Below him the kshatriyas, royalty, nobles and warriors, formed the governing elite, and Brahmins served as priests and often as high officials. Traders and artisans worked in and for the cities, but rice farmers and fishermen made up the large majority, and their harvests, watered by a vast network of canals and reservoirs, fed a capital region of some 700,000 to 900,000 people in the 13th century. Slaves stood at the bottom. No written records of the period survive other than stone inscriptions, so much of what is known of ordinary lives comes from the reliefs of the Bayon and from Zhou Daguan, a Chinese envoy who lived at Angkor from August 1296 to July 1297.

text: Since Jayavarman II proclaimed himself chakravartin, "universal ruler", and devaraja, "god-king", in 802, Khmer kings were held to have the divine quality of living gods, incarnations of Shiva or Vishnu. At the heart of a royal city stood a temple-mountain whose central lingam bore the king's own name. Jayavarman VII, a Mahayana Buddhist, represented himself instead as the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara in the face towers of the Bayon. The king and his officials ran the canals and reservoirs on which the harvests depended, and kings led large armies against Champa and Dai Viet. Zhou Daguan saw the king ride out standing on an elephant whose tusks were encased in gold, holding his sacred sword.
rise: No settled rule governed the succession: of the 27 rulers of Kambuja, eleven lacked a legitimate claim to power. Suryavarman I took the throne by seizing Angkor, Jayavarman VII by retaking it from the Cham, and in 1295 Jayavarman VIII was deposed by his son-in-law Srindravarman.

text: The kshatriyas, royalty, nobles, warlords, soldiers and warriors, formed the governing elite. The king was surrounded by ministers, state officials, nobles and royal kin, and in his procession Zhou Daguan saw the ministers and princes mounted on elephants under innumerable red umbrellas. Rank was written into their homes: the dwellings of the princes and principal officials were much larger than those of the people and laid out differently, their size set by each person's official rank, and only the family temple and the main apartment could be roofed with tiles. Officials managed the waterworks and collected a rent for each trader's place in the market.
rise: Royal and noble birth gave entry to the rank, but its members fought over it: Khmer kings and royal families were often caught up in power struggles over the succession or rivalries over principalities, and the relationship between the rulers and their elites was unstable. A prince could rise as a soldier, as Jayavarman VII did, leading armies under earlier kings before he took the throne.

text: Hinduism was the state religion for most of the empire's history, and its ceremonies and rituals were performed by Brahmins, usually only among the king's family, the nobles and the ruling class. The rite by which Jayavarman II made himself god-king in 802 was taken from the Hindu tradition, and the royal cult was housed in temples to Shiva and Vishnu, from Pre Rup to Angkor Wat. Zhou Daguan had little to say about the Brahmans, except that they were often employed as high officials. Beside them he saw a third group, whom he called by a Chinese name and who appear to have been Shaivites: the only image they revered, he wrote, was a block of stone.
rise: The priests' fortunes followed the king's faith. Jayavarman VII made Mahayana Buddhism the state religion and had Hindu temples altered to show the Buddha; from about 1243 Jayavarman VIII restored Hinduism, made the Bayon a temple to Shiva and replaced Buddha images with lingams. From the 14th century Theravada Buddhism prevailed, and the last Sanskrit inscription dates from 1327.

text: Among the other classes were traders and artisans: carpenters and stonemasons, potters, metalworkers, goldsmiths and textile weavers. Bronze was the preferred material for statues of the gods. The market of Angkor had no permanent buildings: it was an open square where traders sat on woven straw mats on the ground, some under a thatched parasol, and officials levied a rent for each space. Until the 13th century barter was the main way of exchange; Zhou Daguan recorded rice, grain and Chinese coins for small purchases, cloth as another medium, and gold and silver for the largest. Trade was mainly in women's hands. No local people produced silk, Zhou wrote, and they wove cotton from kapok by hand, without a loom.
rise: Newcomers brought crafts of their own: settlers from Siam grew mulberry trees, raised silkworms and wove silk, and the local people asked Siamese women to mend their clothes. A Chinese man arriving at Angkor, Zhou wrote, first took in a local woman, partly to profit from her skill in trade.

text: Rice farmers and fishermen formed the large majority. They planted rice by the banks of the lake and rivers, in the irrigated plains around their villages, or in the hills when the lowlands flooded, and canals and great reservoirs, the barays, watered their fields. Orchards gave palm sugar, coconuts, fruit and vegetables. Fish was the main source of protein, often made into prahok, a fish paste; pigs, cattle and poultry were kept beneath houses of woven bamboo raised on stilts against the floods. Zhou Daguan saw peasant men and women go barefoot with bare chests, wearing only a cloth around the waist. The reliefs of the Bayon show them fishing, butchering, playing a chess-like game and gambling on cockfights.
rise: War reached them all: when the Siamese attacked, Zhou wrote, ordinary people were ordered to face them without strategy or preparation, and soldiers fought naked and barefoot with lance and shield. When droughts and floods damaged the waterworks in the 14th and 15th centuries, many may have left the great cities and moved south.

== mobility
Birth set most people's place, and the sources record movement chiefly at the top. The throne itself changed hands by force again and again: eleven of the 27 rulers of Kambuja lacked a legitimate claim, and kings such as Suryavarman I and Jayavarman VII won it by taking the capital. Faith could lift or lower whole groups: the Brahmins' rites and the Buddhist monasteries rose and fell with the religion of each king, until Theravada Buddhism spread in the 13th century even among the lower classes. Foreigners found places of their own: Siamese settlers wove silk, and Chinese men took local women and shared in their trade. When Angkor declined its people moved too: the court left for the Phnom Penh area in 1431, and in Ayutthaya, which took over Angkor's social stratification, the scribes, court Brahmans, jurists, chamberlains, accountants, physicians and astrologers of the early kingdom usually came from Khmer-speaking elites.

== women
Women ran most of Angkor's trade. "The local people who know how to trade are all women," Zhou Daguan wrote, and the role suggests that they enjoyed significant rights and freedom. They married and bore children young, which may have added to the kingdom's large population, and Zhou, judging by Chinese eyes, thought women of twenty or thirty looked like Chinese women of forty or fifty. Beautiful women were sent to court to serve the king or his family at his whim: in the royal procession three to five hundred palace women with flowers in their hair carried candles, others the royal regalia of gold and silver, and others lances and shields, ahead of the king's guards, while his wives and concubines followed in palanquins and carriages and on horses and elephants. Common women wore a strip of cloth across the chest, noblewomen a longer one over the shoulder, and common women wore hair ornaments and gold rings or bracelets. In a farmer's house the daughters had a room of their own, while the sons slept wherever they found space. Royal women were honoured in stone: Jayavarman VII built Ta Prohm for his mother.
