title: Social Hierarchy of Spanish America: From Viceroy to Enslaved
desc: Colonial Spanish America's ladder: viceroy, Spanish- and American-born Spaniards, clergy, Indigenous nobles, castas, Indigenous commoners and the enslaved.
intro: Spanish law divided the people of its American kingdoms into two "republics": the república de españoles, which came to include everyone of African or mixed descent as well as the Spaniards, and the república de indios, the Indigenous towns with their own nobles, councils and duties. Within them, colonial law and ideology ranked people by descent and colour, alongside wealth, office and legitimacy. Spaniards paid no tribute and could hold office, study and be ordained; Indigenous commoners paid tribute and owed labour drafts; free people of African descent paid tribute too and bore the stigma colonial ideology attached to slavery. The casta paintings of the 18th century show a neat ladder of mixtures, but many historians now see a looser order, in which a person's standing (calidad) turned on wealth, occupation and reputation as well as birth, and could change from one record to the next.

text: No Spanish king ever visited his American kingdoms. In Mexico City and in Lima he was represented by a viceroy (virrey, "vice-king"), appointed for a fixed term and almost always a Spanish-born nobleman, soldier or churchman: in the 17th century several bishops and archbishops served as viceroy of New Spain. As captain-general he commanded the armed forces of the provinces under him; as vice-patron he oversaw the Church and the Inquisition; he appointed the district magistrates (corregidores) and answered for good government, economic development and the humane treatment of the Indigenous population. A court gathered around him and the vicereine. His power had limits. The judges of the Audiencia, the high court, could write to the Council of the Indies in Spain without his leave, and at the end of his term every viceroy faced a residencia, a public inquiry into his conduct. From the 1780s the Bourbon reforms set intendants beside him, whose fiscal powers cut into his authority.
rise: The king chose viceroys from the Spanish nobility, the army and the higher clergy, often after service elsewhere in the empire: Manuel de Amat y Junyent governed Chile before he became viceroy of Peru in 1761. The post ended with recall, death or the end of the term.
you: You govern in the king's name, command his armed forces and oversee the Church, but the judges of the Audiencia can write to Spain without your leave, and a public inquiry into your conduct awaits you when your term ends.
kid: The viceroy ruled New Spain or Peru for the king of Spain, who never crossed the ocean to visit.

text: Spaniards born in Spain, the peninsulares, were a small minority who held most of the highest offices. The viceroys, most Audiencia judges and other royal officials, among them the intendants of the 1780s, came from Spain, and under the Bourbons the crown preferred Spanish-born men for the bishoprics too. The great overseas trade was run by family firms based in Spain, and in New Spain silver mining lay largely with Spanish-born mine owners and their merchant backers. In law a peninsular was simply a Spaniard; the difference lay in office and the crown's trust. Many arrived young and poor, as clerks in a kinsman's business, and married into American-born families, so that their children were criollos. American-born Spaniards called them gachupines in Mexico and chapetones in Peru, and resented them most when the Bourbon reforms gave them offices that criollos had held: in the late 17th century, when the crown sold seats on the Audiencias, American-born men had held 45 per cent of them.
rise: A Spaniard became a peninsular by sailing to the Indies with the crown's licence, meant only for Catholics who could show they had no Jewish or Muslim ancestors. His children born in America were criollos.
you: You came from Spain, perhaps as a royal official or as a young clerk in a kinsman's business; the crown trusts you with its highest offices, and the American-born resent you as a gachupín.
kid: Spaniards born in Spain held most of the top jobs in the colonies, as judges, bishops and royal officials.

text: Spaniards born in the Americas, the criollos, formed the largest privileged group. In law they were españoles: they paid no tribute and could hold office, enter the universities and be ordained. Their elite owned great haciendas and some mines, controlled the town councils (cabildos), bought royal offices when the crown sold them, and some families won titles of nobility. By the 18th century, though kept from most of the highest posts, criollos were the wealthy class of landowners, miners, merchants, lawyers, clergy and officers. Most were far humbler. Spaniards were often the largest group in the colonial cities, and many worked as artisans, estate supervisors, shopkeepers and clerks, or lived in poverty. Criollos took pride in their homeland: in 1648 a criollo priest, Miguel Sánchez, published the story of the Virgin of Guadalupe, widely read as a sign of God's favour to Mexico. They also nursed grievances: the Bourbon reforms gave Spanish-born men offices criollos had held, and the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767 exiled their Jesuit sons.
rise: Birth to Spanish parents made a criollo, but so, in practice, could wealth and a Spanish way of life: the child of a castizo and a Spaniard counted as Spanish, and prosperous families paid to obscure a mixed ancestry. A criollo family that lost its fortune kept its legal status but could sink into poverty.
you: You were born in America to a Spanish family: you pay no tribute and may study, be ordained or sit on the town council, but the highest posts usually go to men born in Spain, and your family may own an hacienda or work as artisans.
kid: Criollos were people of Spanish families born in America; some were rich landowners and many were craftsmen or clerks.

text: The Catholic Church reached into every town, and its clergy formed an estate of their own. Under the Patronato real the crown chose the bishops, drew the parish boundaries and collected the tithe, a tenth of farm produce, for the Church's support. Ordained clergy had the fuero eclesiástico, the right to be tried in church courts, until the Bourbon reforms curtailed it. Parish priests, mostly criollos, drew salaried benefices; clerics without one lived on chantries (capellanías), endowments for Masses for the dead that pious families often entrusted to a son. Religious orders, above all the Jesuits, and convents owned estates and city property, and the Jesuits ran the best colleges until the crown expelled them in 1767. Convents took the daughters of families who could pay a large dowry: Mexico City had 22. A church council barred Indigenous men from the priesthood in 1555, and men of African descent were excluded too, while mestizos could be ordained. The Inquisition watched over the faith of everyone except the Indigenous.
rise: Sons of Spanish families, and some mestizos, entered the priesthood through the seminaries and universities; daughters entered convents with a dowry. The highest posts went mostly to men born in Spain, and the Bourbon reforms kept criollos further from them.
you: You serve the Church as a priest, friar or nun: church courts rather than royal ones try you, and you may live on Masses your family endowed for its dead.
kid: Priests, friars and nuns ran the churches, schools and hospitals of Spanish America.

text: Spanish rule rested on the Indigenous nobility. The crown recognised the old ruling families of central Mexico and the Andes as nobles so long as they were loyal and Catholic: caciques in New Spain, kurakas in Peru, where Inca nobles who proved their descent could be recognised as hidalgos of Castile. They bore the titles don and doña, paid no tribute, could ride horses and carry swords or firearms, and some held entailed estates (cacicazgos). They governed the Indigenous towns, collected the tribute and supplied men for the labour drafts. Many prospered as ranchers, traders and muleteers, and some owned enslaved Africans. A cacique family petitioning the viceroy of New Spain in 1769 listed what it expected: a seat apart from commoners at public functions, freedom from tribute and from jail for debt, and its name among the nobles in official registers. In the 18th century rising royal demands squeezed them between the state and their communities; in 1780 the kuraka Túpac Amaru II led the Andean rebellion.
rise: Rank came by descent, proved in genealogies before Spanish courts, but the colonial authorities could also favour a compliant rival over the rightful heir. After the rebellion of 1780–1781 the crown stopped recognising many kurakas and banned the dress and emblems of the Inca nobility.
you: You are a cacique or kuraka, addressed as don or doña: you pay no tribute and may ride a horse and carry a sword, but you must collect your town's tribute and send its men to the labour drafts.
kid: Indigenous nobles kept their titles under Spanish rule and governed their own towns for the king.

text: Mestizos, the descendants of Spaniards and Indigenous people, were the first and the most favoured of the castas, the people of mixed descent. Early on, many were raised by their Spanish fathers; others grew up in their mothers' communities. The law counted them in the república de españoles. Unlike Indigenous commoners and people of African descent, mestizos paid no tribute, and they could be licensed to carry weapons and, by a grant of Philip II, ordained as priests. They were forbidden to live in Indigenous towns, though some did and even rose to office there. As early as 1543 officials in Mexico classed them with vagabonds (vagabundos), and colonial writers held that the "stain" of Indigenous ancestry disappeared in three generations of marriage with Spaniards: a mestizo and a Spaniard had a castizo, a castizo and a Spaniard a Spaniard. In the towns and on the estates mestizos worked as artisans, traders, foremen and labourers.
rise: An Indigenous villager who learned Spanish, lived as a mestizo and moved to a town could be recorded as one and so escape tribute; a mestizo family that married Spaniards could, in two generations, be written down as Spanish.

text: Most people were Indigenous commoners, legally free vassals of the king, grouped in the república de indios. They lived in their own towns, with Indigenous councils (cabildos), community land, churches and confraternities (cofradías), and non-Indians were forbidden to settle among them. Commoners paid the crown a yearly tribute, and their towns owed labour drafts. In Peru the mita, organised by Viceroy Francisco de Toledo from 1573, took one adult man in seven from much of the southern Andes to the silver mines of Potosí, and others to the mercury mines of Huancavelica, work so feared that many fled their villages. In New Spain the repartimiento drafted smaller numbers for a few weeks a year, for a wage. District officials also forced goods on them at high prices. Yet the law also protected them: they were outside the Inquisition's reach, and in Mexico a General Indian Court, funded by a half-real tax, heard their cases. Communities sued to defend their land and water, and when lawsuits failed they rose in revolt.
rise: Moving to a Spanish town, estate or mine, learning Spanish and passing as a mestizo could free a man from tribute and the labour draft; a few proved noble descent in court and joined the nobility.

text: By the 17th century free people of African descent outnumbered the enslaved in New Spain, and by 1800 they were about one person in ten there. Most were of mixed descent (mulatos or pardos); free Black people were called morenos. They belonged to the república de españoles but bore the stigma colonial ideology attached to slavery, which writers held never washed out. They paid tribute like Indigenous commoners but, unlike them, answered to the Inquisition. Laws from 1577 forbade them to carry weapons, and they were barred from the priesthood and from most schools and universities. They worked as artisans, shopkeepers and servants, some bought land, and a few wealthy mulattoes held minor offices. Free men of colour also served in militia companies of their own, which late in the 18th century brought the fuero militar, the only special privilege open to men of mixed descent. In Lima Martin de Porres, son of a Spanish nobleman and a freed woman, was first admitted to the Dominicans only as a servant.
rise: Freedom came by purchase, an owner's grant or birth to a free mother. After that a skilled trade, militia service, wealth and marriage could raise a family, and some descendants were recorded as mestizos or even Spaniards.
you: You are free, of African descent: you pay tribute, answer to the Inquisition and cannot be ordained, but you can work as an artisan, buy land and serve in a militia company of your own.

text: From the 16th century some 200,000 enslaved Africans reached New Spain and some 95,000 Peru, by modern estimates, many under the asiento contracts the crown sold to foreign traders. Captives born in Africa were called bozales, those who spoke Spanish ladinos, and those born in the colonies criollos. They were property, and could be bought, sold, punished and separated from their families. Many lived in the cities as servants, cooks, laundresses and skilled artisans, often hired out; others worked on the sugar estates of Veracruz and coastal Peru, in vineyards and in textile workshops (obrajes). In New Spain slavery declined after 1640; by 1810 few enslaved people remained. Spanish law, from the Siete Partidas on, left them some rights: to marry in church, to keep savings and to buy their freedom, often in instalments (coartación), and freedom was commoner in towns than in the countryside. Many resisted by running away, changing masters or forming maroon communities (palenques): in 1609 Gaspar Yanga's maroons near Orizaba won recognition as a free town.
rise: Freedom came by purchase, often in instalments, by an owner's grant in life or in a will, or by flight; a child followed its mother's status, so a free mother's children were born free.
you: You belong to an owner who can sell you and keep your children, but the law lets you marry in church, keep savings and buy your freedom in instalments, as many do in the towns.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay; many saved money to buy their freedom.

== mobility
A person's calidad, the standing written into parish books, censuses and lawsuits, rested on descent but also on wealth, occupation, legitimacy, dress and reputation, and the same person could appear under different labels in different records. Indigenous villagers who moved to the towns and spoke Spanish could be recorded as mestizos and escape tribute, while a mestizo might pass as an Indian to escape the Inquisition. Prosperous mixed families who married Spaniards could be recorded as Spanish within a few generations, and from 1795 the crown sold a few wealthy pardos dispensations from their colour (the gracias al sacar). Militia service gave men of colour privileges, and the courts were open to ambitious claims: Túpac Amaru II sued for the Marquisate of Oropesa, a title held by Inca descendants, before he rebelled. Many historians now read the casta paintings as an idealised scheme, not daily practice. Independence ended the legal ranking: the Plan of Iguala of 1821 made all inhabitants of New Spain citizens without distinction, and Simón Bolívar abolished titles of nobility in Peru in 1825.

== women
Women took their family's calidad, and honour weighed most on Spanish women: the Royal Pragmatic on Marriage of 1776 let parents veto their children's unequal matches. Spanish law let women own and inherit property, and widows ran estates and businesses. Convents took elite daughters with large dowries, and in 1724 Corpus Christi opened in Mexico City for noble Indigenous women. Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, an illegitimate criolla, became a nun in 1669 so that she could study, and in 1691 defended women's right to learning in her Reply to Sister Philotea. Cacicas held titles and estates in their own right. Maya women's weaving helped pay their households' tribute, and women of African descent, enslaved and free, worked as cooks, laundresses and servants. Indigenous and enslaved women had little protection from sexual abuse by Spanish men. Micaela Bastidas, of Indigenous and African descent, commanded rebels beside her husband Túpac Amaru II and was executed in Cusco in 1781.
