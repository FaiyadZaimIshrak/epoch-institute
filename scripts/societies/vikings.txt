title: Social Hierarchy of the Vikings: From Kings to Thralls
desc: The Viking social ladder: kings, jarls, chieftains, retainers, free farmers, freed slaves and thralls in Scandinavia from about 793 to 1066.
intro: In Viking Age Scandinavia, from about 793 to 1066, the deepest line ran between the free and the unfree. The Eddic poem Rígsþula, of uncertain date and preserved in a 14th-century manuscript, tells how the god Ríg fathered three sons, Thrall, Karl and Jarl, the ancestors of slaves, free farmers and nobles, and archaeology confirms a society divided along broadly those lines. Free men owned weapons and met at assemblies called things to make law and settle disputes. The free farmers were the great majority. Above them stood chieftains, jarls and kings, whose many small realms grew during the period into the kingdoms of Denmark, Norway and Sweden. Below them were freed slaves and the thralls, many of them captives taken on raids. Birth mattered, but wealth won by raiding, trade and service abroad could raise a family, and capture or debt could make a free person a slave.

text: Kingship in Scandinavia began small: around 800 Norway alone had some 30 petty kingdoms, and kings' names are reliably known only for the later Viking Age. A king (konungr) was above all a war leader who held power by winning followers, rewarding them with gifts and leading them to plunder and conquest. Kings were acclaimed at the assemblies of free men, and the early kings had little power to compel: they led largely by consent. Royal power grew over the period. In Denmark Gorm the Old founded a lasting dynasty, and his son Harald Bluetooth raised the great runestone at Jelling, which claims that he won all Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian. Kings kept armed retinues, called out the ship levy (leiðangr) and spread the new faith, sometimes by force, and Cnut the Great ruled England, Denmark and Norway together. No throne was safe: Harald Bluetooth was overthrown by his own son, Sweyn Forkbeard, and Olaf Tryggvason was killed in battle in 1000.
rise: Kings came from royal families, but no fixed rule of succession existed: sons and kinsmen competed, and the throne went to whoever could win over the chieftains and the assemblies and win the battles.
you: You lead your warriors and reward them with gifts, and the assembly acclaims you, but your own son may rise against you, and you may die in battle.
kid: Viking kings led armies and fleets, gave gifts to their warriors and in time made their people Christian.

text: A jarl (earl) was the greatest lord below a king. The title could mark an independent ruler or a king's deputy. In Norway the jarls of Lade ruled Trøndelag and Hålogaland with as much power as many kings, and Håkon Sigurdsson (Haakon Jarl) governed most of Norway from about 975 to 995, at first under the Danish king. The earls of Orkney ruled the Northern Isles under the Norwegian crown, among them Thorfinn the Mighty in the 11th century. In Denmark the jarl was the king's deputy, as Ulf Jarl was to Cnut the Great, and when Cnut conquered England he made Eric of Lade earl of Northumbria. Jarls owned great estates with large halls, horses and many thralls, kept their own armed followers and showed their rank in silk clothing, brooches and arm rings. Rígsþula pictures Jarl as a warrior who rides, hunts with hawks and hounds, and wins eighteen estates by war.
rise: The title passed within great families such as the jarls of Lade and the earls of Orkney, or was granted by a king to a deputy; a jarl who lost a struggle with a king could lose everything, as Håkon Sigurdsson did when Olaf Tryggvason seized Norway in 995.
you: You hold great estates and a hall full of armed followers and thralls, and you rule a whole region, for a king or in his place.
kid: Jarls were powerful lords, one step below the king, who owned great farms and ruled whole regions.

text: Below the jarls stood the chieftains of districts, wealthy landowners who led their neighbours in war and law. In Norway a hersir commanded the men of his district and owed loyalty to a jarl or king; the later sagas give each jarl four or more hersar. Iceland, settled from about 870 and without a king, was led by its goðar: there were 36 chieftaincies (goðorð), later 39, which could be inherited, bought or shared. Every free farmer of means had to follow a goði of his choice, who backed him in lawsuits and could call on him for armed support, and the goðar ran the local assemblies, sat in the law council of the Althing and named its judges. Pagan worship had no separate order of priests: chieftains kept the halls where the communal sacrifices and feasts were held, and three runestones on the Danish island of Funen name local men as goði. From the early 11th century Norwegian kings drew chieftains into their service as "landed men" (lendir menn).
rise: Chieftaincy passed mostly within families, but land, followers and success in feuds and raids could make a rich farmer a chieftain, and in Iceland a goðorð could simply be bought.
you: The farmers of your district follow you to the assembly and to war; in return you back them in their lawsuits and hold the feasts at your hall.
kid: Chieftains led the farmers of their district in war and at the assemblies where laws were made.

text: Kings, jarls and chieftains kept armed households of free men, called lið and in later sources hirð, who lived with their lord, fought at his side and were rewarded with gifts and pay. Such a retainer was a housecarl (húskarl) or a drengr, a "warrior"; the skald Sigvatr Þórðarson called the retainers of Olaf II of Norway heiðþegar, "receivers of pay". They were bound to their lord and expected to avenge him if he was killed. The best equipped had a helmet, a mail shirt and a sword, marks of wealth beyond most farmers. Many served far from home. The Turinge runestone in Sweden remembers Þorsteinn, who fell "in the east in Garðar" (Russia) as commander of a retinue. So many Swedes joined the Byzantine emperor's Varangian Guard, formed in the late 10th century, that a law of Västergötland barred anyone staying in "Greece" from inheriting, and Cnut's conquest brought a royal guard of housecarls to England, which fought for Harold Godwinson at the Battle of Hastings in 1066.
rise: Free men skilled in arms joined a lord's following by his favour, and loyal service was rewarded with gifts; in Norway the king's men could rise to be his landed men (lendir menn), the highest rank in the royal household.
you: You live in your lord's household, fight at his side and are paid in gifts for your service; if he is killed, you are expected to avenge him.
kid: Kings and chieftains kept bands of warriors who lived with them and were rewarded with gifts.

text: Most free Scandinavians were farmers (bœndr), the karlar of Rígsþula, who worked their own land with their households. A farm centred on a longhouse; its people grew barley, rye, oats and flax, kept cattle, sheep and pigs, fished, hunted and made woollen cloth. Wealth ranged from great farms with many thralls to small farms with one or two, or none. A free man was expected to own weapons, typically a spear, a shield and a knife, and to answer the call to war, in Norway through the ship levy (leiðangr), first recorded in the late 10th century. He had a voice at the thing, the assembly of free men of full age, which made law, judged disputes and acclaimed kings. Few farmers were ever raiders: others sailed as traders or settled in Iceland, the British Isles and the lands to the east. In trading towns such as Hedeby, Birka and Kaupang lived craftsmen and merchants, and free but landless servants (húskarlar) worked on other men's farms.
rise: Sons inherited family land, cleared new land or won wealth abroad; debt, crime or capture in war could reduce a free person to a thrall.
you: You farm your family's land with your household, carry your own spear and shield, and stand with the other free men at the thing when laws are made and disputes judged.
kid: Most Vikings were farmers, not raiders: they grew grain, kept animals and met at assemblies to make laws.

text: A thrall could be freed by the owner, in the owner's will, or by buying freedom with money earned; according to the sagas the Norwegian magnate Erling Skjalgsson set each of his thralls a price and let them earn it by working for themselves. The freed slave (leysingi) was free but not equal. He still owed loyalty to his former master and had to support him at the assembly; if he died without children, the former master inherited his land and goods; and the penalty for killing a freed slave was lower than for a freeborn man. Only after at least two generations did a family become fully free. Freedom often came with land to farm: Aud the Deep-Minded, who settled in Iceland in the late 9th century, freed the thralls she brought with her and gave them land, according to Landnámabók. Most of these rules are known from laws written down in the 12th and 13th centuries.
rise: Freedom came only by the owner's choice, by will or by purchase; the freed slave's grandchildren could expect to count as fully free.
you: You have won your freedom, perhaps with a plot of land, but you still owe loyalty to your former owner, who will inherit your farm if you die without children.
kid: Some thralls were set free, and some were even given land to farm by their former owners.

text: Thralls were property: they could be bought, sold and punished, and the 12th-century Gulating law made anyone who harmed a thrall pay the owner, as for damage to cattle. People became thralls by birth to thralls, through debt or as a punishment, and above all through capture: raiders carried off monks from Lindisfarne in 793 and, the Annals of Ulster say, "a great number of women" from an Irish village in 821. Most captives were sold on, through Hedeby, Dublin and the eastern rivers, to the Islamic world and Byzantium for silver and silk. Those kept at home herded animals, fished, cut wood, built fences, cooked and milked; some women were forced to become their owners' concubines, and some trusted thralls managed farms. Excavations suggest that thralls were sometimes killed to be buried with their owners. Some kept their faith, like the captive nun at Hedeby whose psalms moved Archbishop Rimbert to give his horse for her freedom; others ran away or saved to buy their freedom.
rise: Freedom came only through the owner: as a gift, in a will, or by purchase with money the thrall had earned. Slavery faded between the 12th and early 14th centuries, after Scandinavia became Christian.
you: You belong to an owner who can sell you abroad; you herd animals, cut wood and build fences, and you may try to save enough to buy your freedom.
kid: Thralls were enslaved people, often captured in raids, who were owned by others and made to work without pay.

== mobility
Viking society was more open than the fixed orders of Rígsþula suggest. Raiding, trade and service abroad brought silver to men of modest birth, and a leader who won followers could become a chieftain; settlers in Iceland, the Faroes and Greenland claimed new land for themselves. Fortune turned fast at the top. Olaf Tryggvason won Norway in 995 with wealth gained as a Viking leader in England and lost it with his life at the Battle of Svolder in 1000, defeated by the kings of Denmark and Sweden and by Eric of Lade, the son of Håkon Jarl, who had been killed as Olaf seized power. Lower down, people fell as well as rose: debt, crime or capture turned free people into thralls, while freed slaves rose only slowly, their families counting as fully free after two generations. In the 11th century the ranks began to set more firmly, as kings turned chieftains into royal officials and the Church, which held that Christians should not own fellow Christians, undercut the slave trade.

== women
Free women took the rank of their fathers and husbands, but law and custom gave them more room than women in most of Europe. The housewife (húsfreyja) had authority over the indoor household and managed the farm's stores and business; the keys found in many women's graves are usually read as a sign of that role. Families arranged marriages, but a bride brought her own property, a wife could divorce and remarry, and widows and unmarried women over twenty were legally independent. Women inherited land: the 11th-century Hillersjö runestone in Uppland tells how Gerlög inherited the estate of her daughter Inga. Women also raised runestones, and some were priestesses (gyðjur) or seeresses, while the Oseberg ship held two women buried with great wealth. A 2017 DNA study showed that the weapon grave Bj 581 at Birka was a woman's, but whether she fought is debated, and the shieldmaidens of the sagas are literature. Enslaved women had none of these rights.
