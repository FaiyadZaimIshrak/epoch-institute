intro: British India was ranked by race and caste as well as by wealth and office. The viceroy governed in the Crown's name, and a little over a thousand men of the Indian Civil Service, almost all of them British, ran the districts; Indians held the lower posts. Nearly 600 princes ruled their states under British paramountcy, and the zamindars held the great estates of the north and east: both were rewarded for keeping out of the rebellion of 1857. Colonial rule also made caste a central mechanism of administration. From 1881 the census ranked thousands of jatis by their supposed purity, administrative jobs went only to the upper castes, and the Criminal Tribes Act of 1871 declared whole communities criminal by birth. Most Indians were peasants, who paid the land tax on which the Raj's finances rested and died in the tens of millions in the famines of the later 19th century; the communities treated as untouchable stood outside the caste order altogether, and more than 1.6 million Indians were shipped overseas as indentured labourers.

== mobility
Historians such as Nicholas Dirks argue that caste affiliation was loose and fluid before colonial rule and that the Raj enforced it rigorously, building a stricter hierarchy than before; the censuses of 1901 and 1911 hardened caste identities, and jobs, schooling and the right to own land were allotted by caste. Communities answered by organising: caste associations, a by-product of the census, petitioned for a higher varna, and Kurmis, Ahirs, Jatavs and others claimed Kshatriya status. Education opened a narrow door into the middle class and the lower civil service, and the Indian Civil Service slowly admitted Indians: from 1922 they could sit its examination in Delhi, the Lee Commission of 1924 set future intakes at 40 per cent Europeans and 40 per cent Indians, the rest promoted from the provincial services, and by 1945 Indians outnumbered Britons in the service. Land changed hands too: zamindari estates were auctioned for unpaid rent and small farmers lost their land to moneylenders, while in the north-west the Land Alienation Act of 1900 barred its transfer to non-agricultural castes. From the 1920s the government reserved a share of its jobs for the lower castes. For the poorest, indenture overseas was a way out with a heavy price; many stayed, and their descendants are now about two-thirds of the people of Mauritius.

== women
No woman was ever recruited into the Indian Civil Service; women worked in government only as typists and clerks in the lower ranks of the uncovenanted service, and over 90 per cent of the 36,000 college students of 1911 were men. An ICS officer's widow drew £300 a year, hence the saying that an ICS marriage was worth "three hundred a year alive or dead". After 1857 the British avoided intervening in religion, even over the remarriage of Hindu child widows, though sati had been banned before the rebellion; Indian reformers took up the cause, from Pandita Ramabai, who championed the remarriage of Brahmin widows, to Gokhale's Servants of India Society. Ambedkar argued that sati, enforced widowhood and child marriage had grown up to reinforce marriage within the caste. A zamindar's lawfully wedded wife could inherit the estate if he named her heir, and Travancore had queens regent. Indentured women were paid nine pence to a man's shilling, worked in the fields as well as at home, and suffered widespread sexual exploitation; on early ships there might be one woman to forty men, and from 1868 the rules required 40 women to every 100 men.
