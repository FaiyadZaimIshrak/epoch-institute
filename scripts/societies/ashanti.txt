desc: Asante's social ladder 1764–1896: the Asantehene, paramount and divisional chiefs, queen mothers, Kumasi officials, big men, free farmers and the enslaved.
intro: The Asante, an Akan people, ranked their people by lineage, by office and by freedom. At the top the Asantehene sat on the Golden Stool, believed to hold the spirit of the nation, and governed with councils of the chiefs of the member states. Every chief, from the village to the king, was chosen from a particular female line, swore fealty to the one above him and could be destooled for breaking his oaths, and a queen mother of his line had a say in choosing him. In Kumasi, the capital, a bureaucracy of appointed officials, chosen from Osei Kwadwo's reign for their ability rather than their birth, ran the state's ministries. Most people were free farmers, citizens of the metropolitan states, while conquered provinces paid tribute. War captives and slaves sent as tribute worked for Asante masters or were sold to the coast. Much of the evidence comes from European visitors, among them Thomas Bowdich, who reached Kumasi in 1817, and from Asante traditions.

text: The Asantehene, king of all Asante, was enthroned on the Golden Stool, the Sika dwa, believed to house the spirit of the nation: the living, the dead and those yet to be born. The stool must never touch the ground, and only the king might handle it. He was also ruler of Kumasi. He alone could pass a sentence of death, every member state sent him tribute each year, and in war he was supreme commander, though in the 19th century the Ministry of War in Kumasi increasingly ran the fighting. He did not rule absolutely: he shared lawmaking and government with the bureaucracy, and by law he never ignored the decisions of the Asanteman council. Upon the stool he was sacred, the intermediary between the people and the ancestors.
rise: The senior woman of the royal lineage nominated the eligible men, the elders chose, and the assembled people could reject the choice; between reigns the Mamponghene ruled as regent. A king who broke his oaths could be destooled, as Kusi Obodom was after a failed invasion of Dahomey.
you: You sit on the Golden Stool as king of all Asante, and only you may pass a sentence of death; but you may not ignore the decisions of the Asanteman council, and if you break your oaths the kingmakers can destool you.
kid: The Asantehene was the king of the Asante, and his throne was the sacred Golden Stool.

text: The empire joined metropolitan states, whose people were Asante citizens, to provincial states, kingdoms it had absorbed. Each metropolitan state was headed by a paramount chief, the omanhene, who held executive, legislative and judicial power in his own state. The leaders of Nsuta, Mampong, Dwaben, Bekwai and Kokofu had met before the final break with Denkyira, and after the victory at Feyiase in 1701 the allied rulers recognised Kumasi as the centre of the union. The paramount chiefs, with other provincial chiefs, formed the Asanteman council, which advised the king and whose decisions bound him. In the Kotoko council the state chiefs and their ministers, with the king and the queen mother, formed the aristocratic party. The Mamponghene, the king's deputy, ruled as regent between reigns.
rise: A paramount chief was elected from the female line that provided his state's chiefs, as the Asantehene was, and swore fealty to the one above him; like the king, he could be destooled for breaking his oaths.
you: You rule one of the metropolitan states with full powers of government and justice and sit on the Asanteman council, whose decisions even the Asantehene may not ignore.
kid: Paramount chiefs ruled the Asante states under the king and met in his great council.

text: Beside the chiefs stood the queen mothers. The queen, the ohenemaa, was the most powerful woman in Asante politics, for the chief represented her family and was her son or her sister's son. She had to be consulted when a chief or the king was installed and played a major part in his nomination: for the Golden Stool itself the senior woman of the royal lineage nominated the eligible men. She settled disputes involving women, took part with the council of elders and chiefs in judging and lawmaking, and shared in decisions on war and on the distribution of land; the queen mother also sat on the Kotoko council. In 1900, after the king's exile, Yaa Asantewaa, queen mother of Ejisu, led the War of the Golden Stool against the British.

text: Below the paramount chiefs stood the divisional chiefs, the highest order in each state's divisions, which grouped many villages together. An ohene was a man chosen from his mother's family to represent it, and his main task was to advise his paramount chief. Their titles included the Krontihene, Nifahene, Benkumhene, Adontenhene and Kyidomhene; Amankwa Tia, who led Asante armies in the early 18th century, was the first Kontihene of Kumasi. Each chief swore fealty to the chief above him, and a chief who failed to punish wrongs risked the anger of the ancestors and his own removal. Dress marked their rank: prominent people wore silk, laws kept certain kente designs for the nobility, and some patterns were the king's alone, worn only by his leave.
rise: An ohene was chosen from the female line that provided his division's chiefs and, like any chief, could be destooled for breaking his oaths.
you: You speak for your mother's family as chief of a division of villages, advise your paramount chief and wear the silk and kente the law keeps for people of rank.
kid: Divisional chiefs ruled groups of villages and gave advice to the paramount chiefs.

text: The Asantehene governed through a bureaucracy in Kumasi with separate ministries, among them a foreign office, small but divided into departments for relations with the British, French, Dutch and Arabs, and in the 19th century a Ministry of War. Osei Kwadwo (r. 1764–1777) began appointing central officials for their ability rather than their birth. The civil service, headed by the Gyaasehene, drew men talented in trade, diplomacy and war, and the king appointed them all, including men from the Arabian Peninsula, Sudan and Europe. Although Asante had no writing of its own, the state recruited literate men: in 1817 Bowdich saw "Moorish secretaries" taking notes at a trial, and couriers carried despatch boxes. Ivor Wilks and Larry Yarak disagree over how much power these officials held beside the king.
rise: Office came by the Asantehene's appointment and, from Osei Kwadwo's reign, by ability rather than birth, so that even foreigners could serve.

text: Successful entrepreneurs who gathered great wealth and many followers, or who distinguished themselves by heroic deeds, were honoured as abirempon (singular obirempon), "big men". By the 18th and 19th centuries the title had become formal and political, given to those whose trade benefited the whole state, and the state marked them with the mena, an elephant tail, as their badge. An obirempon handled the administration and economic affairs of his region and sat as its supreme judge, with a fair amount of lawmaking power. The title was old: Ray Kea describes the early town of Asantemanso as the centre of abirempon who organised gold production, trade and political authority around it.
rise: The title was earned rather than inherited: trade that benefited the whole state, or deeds of valour, brought it by the state's award.

text: The metropolitan districts had several police forces. In Kumasi a uniformed police, known by their long hair, saw that no one entered or left the city without the government's permission. The ankobia, a special police, were the king's bodyguards and the empire's special forces, gathered intelligence and put down rebellion. Along the roads the nkwansrafo, the road wardens, checked the movement of traders and strangers, scouted and collected tolls. In war, small cadres of regulars guided the levies and contingents called up from the provinces, and in the early 19th century the Asantehene used the annual tribute to set up a permanent standing army armed with rifles. Asante armies even had medical units.

text: Most people were free Asante, the amanfo, citizens of the metropolitan states, and most of them farmed. The family, extended and traced through the mother, was the basic political unit, led by the head of the lineage; each village had a chief, the odikro, who kept order, presided over the village council and stood between his people, the ancestors and the gods. Farmers burned their fields before the rains, worked them with iron hoes for two to four years and left them fallow, growing plantains, yams, maize, manioc and much else. Ordinary people wore cotton. Only the king could put an Asante to death, sentences were more often commuted than carried out, and under Osei Kwame Panyin (1777–1803) the sale of Asante citizens into slavery was banned.

text: During the Atlantic slave trade Asante was the largest slave-owning and slave-trading state in the lands of present-day Ghana. Most of the enslaved were captives taken in war; others came as tribute, like the 500 slaves a year that conquered Dagomba paid from the early 1770s. Many were sold to the coast, but Ivor Wilks argues that slaves mattered more to Asante as labour on farms and in crafts than as exports. Their lives varied widely. Some acquired wealth, owned slaves of their own and married into their master's family, and a slave who was badly mistreated could ask for a new master. Others were killed at funerals, for the Asante believed that slaves would follow their masters into the afterlife. Slaves were dressed in black cloth.
rise: Capture in war and tribute made people slaves; a master who married an enslaved woman could pass part of his property and status to their children. When Britain conquered Asante it let the chiefs keep their slaves, and the ban of 1908 was enforced only from the 1920s.
you: You were taken captive in war and now work on an Asante master's farm; you may acquire wealth of your own and ask for another master if he mistreats you, but at his funeral you could be killed to follow him.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay; most were captured in wars.

== mobility
Rank in Asante rested on lineage and office. The stools passed within the female lines that provided the chiefs, but no one inherited them outright: each holder was chosen by elders and could be rejected by the assembled people, and a king or chief who ruled badly could be destooled and lose his stool, regalia and powers, though he remained a member of his royal family. Below the stools the state opened careers to ability. Osei Kwadwo began appointing officials for their skill rather than their birth, foreigners served in the civil service, and traders whose business served the state were honoured as abirempon. Some Asante studied in Europe: in 1837 Kwasi Boachi, a son of Kwaku Dua I, and his cousin Kwame Poku were sent to the Netherlands for a European education. War moved people most of all. Conquest turned free people into captives, and the provinces paid tribute in gold, cattle, cloth and slaves; when the Aowin sacked Kumasi in 1717 they carried off thousands of Asante women and children. In 1896 the British exiled Prempeh I and dissolved the union, and they assured the chiefs that they could keep their slaves.

== women
Asante families were matrilineal: a child belonged to the mother's clan, a mother's brother was the legal guardian of her children, and the chiefs represented their mothers' lines, which gave the queen mothers their power to nominate kings and chiefs, to judge women's disputes and to share in decisions on war and land. Women could initiate divorce, while a husband had legal rights over his wife, among them the right to cut off her nose for adultery. Only girls had public puberty rites. Menstruating women were held ritually unclean: they lived in special houses during their periods, swore no oaths and could be put to death for entering a stool house. Families with many children were respected and barren women scorned, and twin girls born into the royal family became potential wives of the king. Some men preferred to marry an enslaved woman or a pawn, because she had no lineage to stand up for her in a quarrel and her husband had full control of their children. In 1900 Yaa Asantewaa, queen mother of Ejisu, led the last armed resistance to British rule.
