title: Social Hierarchy of the Ottoman Empire: From Sultan to Enslaved
desc: The Ottoman social ladder: sultan, viziers, cavalry, religious scholars, Janissaries, merchants, peasants, Christian and Jewish subjects and the enslaved.
intro: Ottoman society rested on one great divide. The askeri, "the military", took in everyone who served the sultan as soldier, official or religious scholar: they paid no taxes and lived on the empire's revenues. The reaya, "the flock" of peasants, herders and townspeople, Muslim and non-Muslim alike, paid for them. Within the ruling class the highest posts went, paradoxically, to men who were legally the sultan's slaves (kul): Christian boys taken in the devshirme, raised as Muslims and trained in the palace, so that no hereditary nobility could rival the dynasty. Religion drew a second line. Christians and Jews were protected subjects who kept their faith but paid a poll tax and ranked below Muslims in law, and the enslaved were captives from beyond the frontiers. The empire grew from about 12 million people in the 1520s to perhaps 30 million by 1600, and a few thousand men governed it.

text: The sultan, the padişah or "lord of kings", was the source of every office and, in law, the owner of the empire's farmland. He governed through the sharia and through kanun, the dynastic law issued in his own name, and Suleiman the Magnificent was remembered as Kanuni, "the Lawgiver". After Selim I conquered the Mamluk Sultanate in 1516–1517, the sultans also claimed the title of caliph. Everyone in the ruling class held his post, his fortune and his life at the sultan's pleasure: Suleiman had his grand vizier and boyhood friend Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha strangled in 1536. From the 15th century the sultans had their heirs by slave concubines rather than by foreign princesses. No rule fixed the succession. Princes governed provinces until their father died and then fought for the throne, and under Mehmed II the law allowed the victor to put his brothers to death: Mehmed III had nineteen executed in 1595. From the late 16th century the sultans left day-to-day government to the grand vizier.
rise: Only a son of the dynasty could reign, and the throne went to the prince who prevailed over his brothers, sometimes before his father's death: Selim I forced his father Bayezid II to abdicate in 1512.
you: Every office, fortune and life in the ruling class is yours to give or take, and in law the empire's farmland is yours, but you fought your brothers for the throne, and the law let you put them to death.
kid: The Ottoman sultan ruled from Topkapı Palace in Istanbul and could appoint or dismiss any official.

text: At the top of the ruling class stood the grand vizier, the sultan's deputy, with the other viziers of the imperial council (divan), the governors-general of the provinces (beylerbeyi), eight in 1527 and 32 by 1609, and the district governors (sancakbeyi) under them. After Mehmed II had his grand vizier Çandarlı Halil Pasha executed in 1453, these posts went more and more to the sultan's kul, his "slaves": boys taken from Christian families by the devshirme, converted to Islam and trained in the palace school, some 300 of them inside Topkapı Palace. Here lay the paradox. The most powerful men of the empire were legally the sultan's slaves: they commanded armies, drew revenue grants (has) worth over 100,000 akçes a year and owned slaves themselves, yet their posts, fortunes and lives were his to take. The historian Halil İnalcık argued that a convert was no longer a slave in the ordinary sense. Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, born an Orthodox Christian in Herzegovina, was grand vizier from 1565 to 1579.
rise: Pages left the palace for posts as officers and governors, and a governor could rise to vizier. A word from the sultan could end a career: Bayezid II had the grand vizier Gedik Ahmed Pasha executed in 1482.
you: You were taken from a Christian family as a boy and trained in the sultan's palace; now you command armies and own slaves yourself, but in law you are still the sultan's slave, and your post, your fortune and your life are his to take.
kid: Many Ottoman viziers and governors had been taken from Christian families as boys and trained in the sultan's palace.

firm: no
terms: timar, sipahi
people:
text: The provincial cavalry, the timariot sipahis, made up the bulk of the classical Ottoman army. Each held a timar: not land but the right to collect taxes worth up to 20,000 akçes a year from named villages, in place of a salary, in return for riding to war with armed retainers (cebelü) in proportion to his revenue; officers held larger grants (zeamet) of up to 100,000 akçes. The timariot kept order in his villages and saw that their fields stayed in cultivation, and the peasants owed some days' work on his own plot, but he could punish no one without a judge's verdict. A timar was not hereditary, though it was often given to a son who served, and a sipahi who missed campaigns for seven years lost it. Some timariots were Christians in the 15th century; by the 16th almost all were Muslims. Firearms and long wars reduced their value, and after the Battle of Keresztes in 1596 some 30,000 absentees were stripped of their timars.
rise: Timars went to cavalrymen's sons who served, and as rewards to Janissaries and other servants of the sultan; a commoner who distinguished himself in war might also win one.
you: You collect the taxes of a few villages instead of a salary and keep order there, and when the sultan calls you ride to war with your armed retainers; stay away too long and you lose your grant.
kid: Ottoman cavalrymen called sipahis were paid with the taxes of villages instead of wages.

text: The ulama, scholars of Islamic law trained in the madrasas, formed the learned branch of the ruling class and, like the rest of it, paid no taxes. Unlike the kul, they were freeborn Muslims. They taught in the madrasas, led prayers and preached, and above all served as judges: in every district a qadi (kadı) heard cases, drew up inheritances and endowment deeds and checked the officials, for even a timariot needed his verdict to punish a peasant. Two chief judges, the kadıaskers of Rumelia and Anatolia, sat in the imperial council and proposed candidates for judgeships, and the chief mufti of Istanbul, the şeyhülislam, headed the hierarchy. Over the 16th century the learned career became a graded ladder from madrasa teacher to judge of a great city. Ebussuud Efendi, şeyhülislam from 1545 to 1574, worked with Suleiman the Magnificent to reconcile the sultan's law (kanun) with the sharia, and issued thousands of legal opinions (fatwas), on matters from land tenure to the drinking of coffee.
rise: Any Muslim boy could enter a madrasa, many of them paid for by pious endowments, so the poor could rise by learning; graduates who took a town judgeship at once were paid sooner but rarely rose as high as those who began as teachers.
you: You trained in Islamic law in a madrasa and now hear your district's cases, draw up its inheritances and check its officials; you pay no taxes, and if you began as a teacher you may one day judge a great city.
kid: Ottoman judges called kadis were trained in Islamic law and settled disputes in every district of the empire.

picture: janissaries
text: The sultan's standing army, the kapıkulu or "slaves of the Porte", were salaried professionals: the Janissary infantry and six divisions of household cavalry. Most came through the devshirme. Every few years officials took one boy from every forty Christian households, mainly in the Balkan countryside, converted him, sent him to a Turkish family in Anatolia to learn the language and then trained him as a cadet. A full Janissary lived in barracks, drew his pay in cash, could not marry until Selim II allowed it in 1566, and in peacetime policed Istanbul and fought its fires; only his own officers could punish him. Legally the sultan's slaves, the Janissaries were in practice a privileged corps: every new sultan paid them an accession gift, and they could mutiny for more, as they first did in 1449. As firearms grew in importance, so did they. The corps grew from about 6,000 men in 1475 to some 40,000 by 1609, and from 1594 freeborn Muslims and Janissaries' own sons could join.
rise: A Janissary rose by seniority within his unit; the best were promoted to the household cavalry or given timars, and a few rose far, like Mimar Sinan, levied in 1512, who commanded Janissary units before becoming the sultans' chief architect.
you: You were taken from a Christian household as a boy, converted and trained as a cadet; now you live in barracks on the sultan's pay, may not marry, and answer to no one but your own officers.
kid: Janissaries were the sultan's elite foot soldiers, who lived in barracks and marched to the music of their own band.

text: The townspeople were reaya, taxpayers, but the sultans needed them. Mehmed II resettled Muslims, Christians and Jews in Constantinople to revive it after 1453, and Bayezid II welcomed Jews expelled from Spain in 1492, who settled in Istanbul, Salonica and other towns. Craftsmen and shopkeepers belonged to guilds (esnaf), which kept up standards, limited production and helped members in hard times, and through which the state watched over the bazaars and the townspeople; by the 1580s the guilds were a fixture of town life, as the account of the great procession of 1582 for the circumcision of Murad III's son shows. Merchants traded along the overland routes between Europe and Asia and lodged in caravanserais, and Cairo prospered on the new trade in Yemeni coffee. Jews, Greeks and Armenians were prominent in trade and finance; the Jewish financier Joseph Nasi rose so high at court that Selim II made him duke of Naxos in 1566. The levy of boys spared the great cities and the craftsmen's sons.
rise: Guild rights passed within families, and guilds went to court against outsiders who took up their trade; a merchant's wealth brought comfort and influence, but not a place in the ruling class.
you: You keep a shop in the bazaar at a trade that your guild regulates and the state watches over, you pay your taxes, and your sons are spared the levy of boys.
kid: Ottoman craftsmen belonged to guilds that set standards for their trades and helped members in hard times.

people:
picture: -
text: The great majority of the sultan's subjects were peasants, the reaya ("flock") of the countryside, and they paid for the empire. All farmland belonged in law to the ruler, but a peasant family held its plot as hereditary tenants, passing it from father to son. In the Balkans a household typically gave a tithe of 10–25 per cent of its harvest to the timariot, the cavalryman who held its village, or to the treasury; it also paid a farm tax (the çift resmi for Muslims, the ispence for Christians, who owed the poll tax as well) and other dues, and owed some days' work on the timariot's own fields. A headman spoke for the village to the authorities, and the government tried to keep the peasants unarmed. Many Muslims were herders: in the 1570s about one household in six in four Anatolian provinces was nomadic. The late 16th century brought inflation, crowding on the land and famine, and when demobilised musketeers turned to banditry, the Celali revolts spread anarchy across Anatolia from the 1590s.
rise: The Ottoman saying ran "the son of a reaya is a reaya", but a Muslim villager could rise by study in a madrasa, by distinction in war or, from the late 16th century, by enlisting as a soldier.
you: You farm a hereditary plot that belongs in law to the sultan, give a tenth or more of your harvest to the cavalryman who holds your village, and work some days on his own fields.
kid: Most people in the Ottoman Empire were farmers, who gave part of every harvest as tax.

text: Christians and Jews were dhimmis (zimmi), protected peoples under Islamic law. They kept their churches, synagogues and property and worshipped freely, but paid a poll tax, the jizya (cizye), and the ispence in place of the Muslims' farm tax; in law they could not bear arms or ride horses and had to dress apart from Muslims, though such rules were not always enforced, and the law ranked them below Muslims. They also ran much of their own affairs: Mehmed II left the Orthodox Church its autonomy and lands in return for loyalty, and church and rabbinical courts judged family and communal matters, though the formal "millet system" took shape only in the 18th and 19th centuries. Refugees from Spain after 1492 made the empire's Jews, some 150,000 by 1600, the largest Jewish community in the world. Rural Christian families also gave up sons to the devshirme every few years. Some converted to escape these burdens; in 1565 Albanian villagers killed the levy's officials.
rise: Conversion to Islam lightened taxes and opened the ruling class, and the devshirme carried some village boys to the highest offices; Christians and Jews could also prosper in trade, finance and medicine, and Jewish physicians served the sultans.
you: You keep your church and your community's courts, but you pay the poll tax, may not bear arms, and every few years the officials may come to take one of your village's sons for the sultan's service.
kid: Christians and Jews in the Ottoman Empire could follow their own religion but paid an extra tax.

text: Islamic law forbade enslaving Muslims or the empire's own Christian and Jewish subjects, so the enslaved were outsiders: prisoners of war, people seized by Barbary corsairs, Africans brought across the Sahara and the Red Sea, and the victims of the Crimean Tatars' raids into Poland-Lithuania and Russia, which on one tentative estimate carried some 2 million people into the empire between 1500 and 1700. Sold in the slave markets, they made up perhaps a fifth of Istanbul's people in the 16th and 17th centuries. They served as household servants and concubines, rowed the galleys and worked on farms; the ruling class were the biggest buyers, and African eunuchs guarded the palace harem. A few rose very high: most sultans' mothers had been slaves. A child whom a master acknowledged as his own was born free, and its mother was freed at his death. Freeing a slave was a pious act, and many were freed after a fixed term of service. Some fled, but a runaway was hard to hide.
rise: Freedom came by manumission, often after a fixed term of service or by the owner's will, or for a concubine whose child the owner acknowledged; freed slaves often stayed with their former owners, for few other livelihoods were open to them.
you: You were taken in war or seized in a raid and sold in a slave market; you serve in a household or row the galleys, and your hope of freedom rests on your owner's piety or a promise of release after a fixed term.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay; many were later set free.

== mobility
Ottoman society was steep but, at the top, unusually open, because the sultans recruited their rulers from outside any hereditary elite. The devshirme could turn a Christian village boy into a vizier: Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, born to a Serbian Orthodox family in Herzegovina, governed the empire as grand vizier for fourteen years, persuaded the sultan to restore the Serbian Patriarchate of Peć and placed relatives, Muslim and Christian, in high posts. A captive could rise as far. Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha, a boy from Parga who came to the palace as a slave, became Suleiman's grand vizier, and the enslaved Hürrem became the sultan's wife. Freeborn Muslims climbed through the madrasas to the judgeships, and a commoner who distinguished himself in war might receive a timar. Conversion opened every door, and the levy of boys was hated as a "blood tax" even as some families sought it. Falls were as steep: viziers were executed and their fortunes seized, and in 1596 some 30,000 cavalrymen who had stayed away from the war lost their timars.

== women
Women's lives followed their household's rank and religion, but Islamic law gave Muslim women a legal personality of their own. They owned and inherited property, a daughter receiving half a son's share, went to court in person and founded pious endowments: of the Ottoman fountains of Istanbul still standing in the 1930s, nearly 30 per cent were women's endowments. A peasant who left only a widow and daughters could pass on his land only if they paid an entry fee (tapu). The harem became a centre of power. Hürrem, a captive from Ruthenia, was freed and married by Suleiman around 1534, wrote to King Sigismund II Augustus of Poland and founded a mosque complex in Istanbul with a women's hospital, opening the age historians call the Sultanate of Women. Jewish women such as Esther Handali were the harem's business agents (kira). Enslaved women were at their owners' disposal, though one who bore an acknowledged child was freed at his death.
