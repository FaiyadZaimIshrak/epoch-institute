title: Social Hierarchy of Old Babylonia: From King to Enslaved
desc: Old Babylonia's social ladder under Hammurabi: king, officials, priests and nadītu, scribes, merchants, soldiers, awīlum, muškēnum and slaves.
intro: Babylonian society in the age of Hammurabi was ranked by law, by service to the king and the gods, and by wealth. The Code of Hammurabi, written near the end of his reign, sorts people into three classes and punishes by class: the awīlum, the free man; the muškēnum, a commoner whose exact standing scholars still debate; and the slave, wardum or amtum. Above them stood the king, called by the gods, he declared, "to prevent the strong from oppressing the weak", with his officials and the priests of the great temples. The palace granted fields to soldiers, officials and craftsmen in return for service. Merchants and moneylenders prospered in the cities, while most free families farmed the irrigated plain, where a bad harvest could bring debt and drive a wife or child into bondage. The ladder was steep, but kings eased it by edicts that cancelled debts for necessities.

text: The king of Babylon ruled as the servant chosen by the gods. The prologue to the Code of Hammurabi tells how the gods gave kingship to Babylon and named Hammurabi "the shepherd" who would bring justice to the land. The king led the army, consulted diviners before battle, built temples, city walls and canals, and heard appeals: just over 200 of Hammurabi's letters survive, many ordering officials to investigate a complaint or a disputed field. The palace (ekallum) was both his residence and a great landowner. Each year was named after a pious deed, a new canal or a victory of the king, and at their accession kings proclaimed "justice" (mīšarum) by cancelling debts. Hammurabi, sixth king of an Amorite dynasty, turned a small state into an empire, taking Larsa in 1763 BCE and Mari soon after, and ended his reign as "king of the four quarters" of the world. His successors lost most of it, and a Hittite raid ended the dynasty around 1595 BCE.
rise: The throne stayed in one Amorite family for three centuries and normally passed from father to son: Hammurabi succeeded his father Sin-muballit in 1792 BCE, and his son Samsu-iluna succeeded him.
you: You are hailed as the shepherd chosen by the gods: you lead the army, hear appeals from your subjects and, when you take the throne, proclaim "justice" by cancelling debts.
kid: The king of Babylon led the army, built temples and canals, and had his laws carved on a tall stone.

text: Hammurabi governed through a small circle of men bound to him personally. Letters from Mari name two ministers (šukkallum), Erra-nada and Sin-bel-aplim, the second of whom received foreign embassies, and private secretaries, among them Sin-iddinam, who became governor of Larsa after its conquest. About a hundred of Hammurabi's letters to Sin-iddinam survive, on canals, troops, taxes, corvée labour and lawsuits; another official, Shamash-hazir, ran the land registry of the Larsa region and allotted the crown's service fields. In each city, governors, mayors and elders kept order, collected dues and heard local disputes with the king's judges. Officials were rewarded with rations and with fields held for as long as they served, and men who called themselves "servant of Hammurabi" on their seals were probably palace officials. The Code punished a judge who reversed his own verdict with a twelvefold payment and removal from the bench (law 5), and Hammurabi had men accused of taking bribes sent to him with the witnesses.
rise: Office depended on the king's favour: a secretary such as Sin-iddinam could rise to govern a province, and during the war with Elam Hammurabi brought three officials of a rival court, Ekallatum, into his council.
you: You serve the king as a governor or minister, answering his letters about canals, taxes and lawsuits, and you hold your fields only for as long as you serve him.
kid: Officials helped the king run his kingdom, looking after canals, taxes and court cases.

text: Each city's great temple was the household of its god, run by a chief administrator (šangûm) and served by priests of many kinds: some fed and clothed the god's statue, others sang laments to calm the gods, and diviners (bārûm) read the will of the gods in the livers of sacrificed lambs, as kings did before battle. Temples owned fields and herds, lent grain and silver, and had to ransom a captured soldier of their city who could not pay (law 32). Many temple offices had become hereditary shares, counted in days of service, which families inherited and sold. Beside the temples lived the nadītu, women dedicated to a god. At Sippar about 200 nadītu of the sun god Shamash lived in a walled cloister (gagûm). They entered with a dowry, did not marry or bear children, and many leased out fields, lent silver and bought slaves on their own account. They came from smiths' families and kings' alike: a sister of Hammurabi was one of them.
rise: Priestly offices passed within families or were bought; a girl became a nadītu when her family dedicated her and guaranteed her keep, and what she owned went back to her family when she died.
you: Your family dedicated you to the sun god, and you live in the walled cloister at Sippar; you may not marry, but you lease out your fields and lend silver in your own name.
kid: Priests cared for the temples of the gods, and some women, called nadītu, lived together in a walled cloister.

text: Scribes wrote the contracts, letters, receipts and court records on which Babylonian life ran, pressing wedge-shaped signs into clay. Most learned the craft as boys in a teacher's house rather than in a public school: one such house at Nippur, known as House F, held nearly 1,500 fragments of tablets from the early reign of Samsu-iluna, most of them pupils' exercises. Pupils copied signs, lists of words, model contracts and proverbs, then hymns and stories in Sumerian, a language no longer spoken, while everyday writing was in Akkadian; school stories tell of a pupil beaten for poor work. Trained scribes served the palace, the temples, merchants and private families. Literate families often handed the skill from father to son, and at Sippar a few women of the cloister were scribes. The schools also kept Babylonian learning alive: the Code of Hammurabi entered the curriculum and was copied for more than a thousand years.
rise: Writing opened the way into the king's service: Hammurabi's secretaries handled state secrets, and one of them, Sin-iddinam, became governor of Larsa.
you: You spent your boyhood copying signs, word lists and Sumerian proverbs in your teacher's house; now you write the contracts, letters and receipts of the palace, the temples and the merchants.
kid: Scribes wrote letters, contracts and laws by pressing wedge-shaped marks into soft clay.

text: Merchants (tamkārum) were the traders and bankers of Babylonia. In each large city they formed a body, the kārum, named after the quay where goods were landed, under its own head, the overseer of the merchants. They lent silver and grain at interest, which the Code capped at a fifth on silver and a third on grain; they financed travelling agents, whom laws 100–107 required to account for every shekel with sealed receipts; and they turned the palace's produce into silver. Long-distance trade brought the copper, stone and timber that the plain lacked: at Ur the copper merchant Ea-nasir bought ingots by way of Dilmun, and his house held letters from customers complaining of poor copper. A merchant who ransomed a soldier captured on campaign was repaid by the soldier, or if he had no means by his city's temple or the palace (law 32). Royal edicts that cancelled debts for necessities spared commercial loans.
rise: Trading and lending were open to anyone with silver, and temples and nadītu lent at interest too; credit could fail, and part of Ea-nasir's house was later joined to a neighbour's, perhaps after his fortunes fell.
you: You lend silver and grain at interest, send agents out with your capital and buy copper shipped from Dilmun; the Code limits the interest you may charge.
kid: Merchants bought and sold goods such as copper, and lent silver and grain to people who needed them.

text: The king's soldiers held land from the crown in return for service, the obligation called ilkum. The Code names two kinds, the rēdûm ("soldier") and the bā'irum (often translated "fisherman" or "auxiliary"), and guards their holdings closely (laws 26–41). A soldier's field, orchard and house could not be sold, nor written over to his wife or daughter, nor given for a debt. A soldier who refused a royal campaign or sent a hired substitute was to be put to death, and the man who denounced him took his estate. If he was captured, his son took over the land and the service, or his mother received a third of it to raise a young son; a soldier who left his field lost it to whoever had done his service for three years. Officers who robbed a soldier, hired him out or took the king's gifts to him faced death. Hammurabi's letters to Shamash-hazir show the crown checking who held these fields.
rise: Service land passed to a son who could do the service, so soldiering often ran in families; a man who failed to serve, or left his field, lost it to another who did.
you: You hold a field, an orchard and a house from the king, and when he calls a campaign you must march; your land cannot be sold or taken for your debts, and if you are captured your son will take over your service.
kid: Soldiers were given land by the king, and in return they had to fight whenever he called them.

text: The awīlum, literally "man", was the free man of full standing in the Code of Hammurabi, and his household was the basic unit of Babylonian society. Most awīlū farmed barley and dates on the irrigated plain, on family land or on fields rented from the palace, the temples or richer neighbours; others herded sheep, worked as craftsmen or hired themselves out for wages that the Code set (laws 257 and 273). Injuries to an awīlum were repaid in kind: whoever blinded his eye was blinded, whoever broke his bone had a bone broken, and whoever knocked out the tooth of an equal lost a tooth (laws 196, 197 and 200). Local judges, elders and the city assembly heard most disputes, and the Code's epilogue invited any wronged man to have the stele read aloud to him. Free families lived close to the margin: a flood or drought could bring debt, though the Code let a debtor whose crop was destroyed put off repayment, without interest, for that year (law 48).
rise: A family that prospered could buy land, lend at interest or win a service field; one that fell into debt could lose its land or have to send a wife, son or daughter into debt service.
you: You farm barley and dates on the family plot or on rented land; the law repays any injury to you in kind, but one flood or drought can put your family in debt.
kid: Most free Babylonians were farmers who grew barley and dates in fields watered by canals.

text: The muškēnum stood between the full free man and the slave, and who he was is still debated. The word, perhaps from a verb meaning "to bow down", survives in Arabic miskīn and French mesquin. Some scholars see the muškēnū as simply the lower class of free people; others as dependants of the palace, holding land or rations from the crown. The Code links them with the palace: an ox stolen from a god or the palace was repaid thirtyfold, from a muškēnum tenfold (law 8), and helping a slave of either to escape meant death (law 15). The law valued a muškēnum less than an awīlum, but also charged him less. Whoever blinded his eye or broke his bone paid 60 shekels of silver rather than suffering the same (law 198); a surgeon's fee was five shekels for him and ten for an awīlum (laws 215–216). Muškēnū owned property and slaves, and as Samsu-iluna prepared to take the throne he remitted the debts of crown servants, soldiers and muškēnū.
rise: How a family came to be counted among the muškēnū, or rose out of them, is not known.
you: You are free and may own a field and even a slave, but the law counts you for less than an awīlum: whoever blinds you pays silver instead of losing his own eye.
kid: The laws treated muškēnū as free people, but of lower rank than the full free men called awīlū.

text: Slaves (wardum, amtum) were property: they could be sold, pledged for debt, given as part of a dowry and inherited, and they wore a distinctive hairlock (abbuttum) that no barber might shave off without the owner's consent (law 226). They were war captives, slaves bought abroad, children of slave mothers or people enslaved for debt or crime, and most served in households. Helping a slave escape through the city gate or sheltering a runaway meant death, while whoever caught one received two shekels (laws 15–17), and a slave who denied his master could have an ear cut off (law 282). Yet a slave of the palace or of a muškēnum who married a free woman fathered free children (law 175), and a master's children by his slave woman went free at his death and inherited if he had acknowledged them (laws 170–171). Debt could also bind free people: a wife, son or daughter given for a man's debt served three years and went free in the fourth (law 117).
rise: Owners could free slaves, often by adopting them, as nadītu at Sippar did with the women they made their heirs; family members bound for debt went free after three years or when a king proclaimed a release.
you: You belong to another household, which can sell you, pledge you for its debts or give you away with a dowry; your hairlock marks you as a slave, and anyone who shelters you if you run away risks death.
kid: Slaves were owned by other people and had to work without pay, though some were set free.

== mobility
Babylonian society was steep, and debt moved more people down it than anything else. A bad harvest could lead a farmer to pledge his land, then his wife or children, and finally himself. The Code limited the bondage of family members to three years (law 117), and kings met hard times with edicts of "justice" (mīšarum) cancelling debts for necessities: Hammurabi issued one in his first year, about 1792 BCE, and another for the Larsa region after its conquest, and the surviving edict of Ammi-saduqa freed citizens bound for debt, though not slaves born in a household. The ways up ran through service and wealth: fields held from the king, posts in his administration, trade and lending, and a secretary such as Sin-iddinam could become a governor. Adoption moved property and people between families: a craftsman who raised a child and taught him his trade could keep him (law 188), and nadītu adopted women they had freed. After Hammurabi, decades of war and the loss of the south left Babylonian society poorer and more militarised in its last century.

== women
In the Code of Hammurabi a woman's life turns on marriage, dowry and family. A bride brought a dowry (šeriktum) from her father, and the groom's family paid a bridewealth (terḫatum); the dowry stayed hers, passed to her children (law 162) and normally left with her if her husband divorced her. A wife who refused a husband who neglected and disparaged her could return with her dowry to her father's house, if her city quarter found her blameless (law 142), but a husband's debts could send her into three years of debt service (law 117). Women kept taverns, wove cloth and, as nadītu, managed fields, loans and slaves; some nadītu at Sippar were scribes, such as Amat-Mamu, and a sister of Hammurabi and daughters of other kings were nadītu of Shamash. A woman shared her family's rank: a blow that made an awīlum's daughter miscarry cost ten shekels, a muškēnum's daughter five and a slave woman two (laws 209, 211 and 213).
