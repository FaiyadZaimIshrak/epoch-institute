title: Social Hierarchy of the Kingdom of Benin: From Oba to Enslaved
desc: Benin's social ladder c. 1440–1690: the sacred Oba, the hereditary Uzama, palace and town chiefs, royal craft guilds, free villagers and the enslaved.
intro: The Kingdom of Benin ranked its people around a sacred king, the Oba, who lived secluded in his palace at Benin City and in principle owned the kingdom's brass, ivory and coral. Birth decided the top: the royal line and the hereditary chiefs called the Uzama. Below them rank came mainly from office. Oba Ewuare (r. 1440–1473) is credited with creating two orders of chiefs, of the palace and of the town, whose titles the Oba conferred, and freeborn men rose towards them through the palace associations. Craft guilds worked for the court, most people farmed in villages run by their elders, and war captives and condemned criminals were enslaved. The evidence is the court's oral tradition, written down above all by the Benin historian Jacob Egharevba from the 1930s, the reports of Portuguese and Dutch visitors, including Olfert Dapper's book of 1668, and the palace's brass plaques.

text: The Oba was a sacred king. The Edo held that the survival and prosperity of every family depended on him, and it was punishable by death to say that he ate, slept or washed like other men. He left the palace only for great ceremonies; in public, attendants supported his arms, and the palace plaques show him flanked by two of them. He was supreme commander in war and appointed the chiefs of the palace and the town. In principle he owned all brass, ivory and coral, and a chief wore coral beads only by his grant. Oba Ewuare (r. 1440–1473) rebuilt Benin City with walls and broad streets, reorganised its government and, by tradition, fixed the succession on the Oba's eldest son. Before he could reign, the heir had to bury his father with elaborate rites and set up an altar to him, with a commemorative brass head.
rise: The throne passed within the royal line, in principle to the eldest son, but brothers fought for it: after Ozolua's death Esigie overthrew his brother Arhuaran. In the 17th century reigns grew short, the father-to-son line may have broken after Ohuan's death in 1641, and the chiefs' power grew.
you: You are held to be sacred and leave the palace only for great ceremonies; every chief of the palace and the town owes his title to you, and the kingdom's brass, ivory and coral are yours.
kid: The Oba was Benin's king, and his people believed he was sacred.

text: The Uzama were the senior hereditary chiefs of Benin, and their titles passed from father to son. Tradition links them to the council of elders that, before the Oba dynasty, invited a prince from Ife to restore the kingship, and their leader was the Oliha. They kept the right to crown each new Oba. Before Ewuare they also chose him and could hold him in check. Ewuare broke their power to choose by fixing the succession on the eldest son, for whom he created the title of Edaiken, and by building up orders of chiefs whom he appointed himself. The change was not smooth: tradition says that his son Olua ruled amid much dissent, and that a council of chiefs governed for some three years before Ozolua was made Oba in 1483. The Uzama kept their precedence and their part in the coronation, but their authority was much reduced.
rise: Birth alone made an Uzama: the titles were inherited. What the Uzama could lose was power, as Ewuare's reforms showed.
you: Your title came to you from your father; you will help crown the next Oba, but since Ewuare's reforms you no longer choose him.
kid: The Uzama were chiefs who inherited their titles and crowned each new Oba.

text: Oba Ewuare is credited with creating the palace chiefs, the Eghaevbo n'Ogbe, as one of two orders of chiefs appointed by the Oba and answerable to him. They led the three palace associations, the Iwebo, the Iweguae and the Ibiwe, which between them kept the Oba's regalia and wardrobe, served his person and ran the quarters of his wives and children; the craft guilds of the court were attached to these associations. Freeborn men of the capital served in these associations for small rewards, and in time the Oba might grant one of them a title, which did not pass to his sons. Like the town chiefs, the palace chiefs collected tribute, heard disputes and took part in the affairs of state. The palace they served was vast: Olfert Dapper's book of 1668, compiled from Dutch reports, compares it to the town of Haarlem, with apartments for courtiers and galleries whose wooden pillars were covered with cast metal plaques.
rise: Service in a palace association was the usual road to a palace title, which only the Oba could confer and which did not pass to the holder's sons.
you: You hold a title the Oba gave you after long service in a palace association; you help keep his regalia and wardrobe, attend his person or run the quarters of his wives.
kid: Palace chiefs looked after the Oba's treasures and household, and the Oba chose them himself.

text: The town chiefs, the Eghaevbo n'Ore, formed the second order of appointed chiefs that Ewuare is credited with creating. Their leader, the Iyase, is often described as the kingdom's prime minister, and in war he and the Ezomo commanded the Oba's armies under the king himself. Like the palace chiefs, the town chiefs were appointed by the Oba and answerable to him; they collected tribute, heard disputes and took part in the affairs of state, and their titles did not pass to their sons. In the towns he conquered, Ewuare replaced the old ruling families with chiefs of his own system. War chiefs who served well received leopard ornaments from the Oba, an emblem otherwise worn only by the king and his highest officials, and the palace plaques show chiefs and warriors ranked by their dress and emblems. Over the 17th century officials grew stronger as reigns grew shorter, and around 1689 the Iyase Ode led a revolt of officials that began years of civil war.
rise: The Oba conferred town titles, often on men who had proved themselves in war; a son did not inherit his father's title.
you: The Oba gave you your title, not your father; you collect tribute, hear disputes and may lead men to war under the Iyase.
kid: Town chiefs collected tribute for the Oba, and their leader, the Iyase, led armies in war.

text: Ewuare is credited with dividing Benin City into separate quarters for different crafts, and its craftsmen were organised in guilds that worked for the court. The brass casters, the Igun Eronmwon, ranked highest among the guilds attached to the Iwebo palace association, followed by the blacksmiths and the ivory and wood carvers. The casters lived in a quarter of their own under the Oba's direct control; their guild still works in Igun Street. By the lost-wax method they cast the commemorative heads for the royal altars and the plaques that covered the palace pillars, which record the court's ranks in dress and emblems; much of their brass came from manillas, the brass rings that Portuguese traders brought from the Rhineland. The blacksmiths forged swords and spearheads for the army. Since in general only the Oba could own works of brass and ivory, the guilds depended on him, and their work was finest when he was strong enough to employ many specialists. Membership usually passed from father to son.
rise: Most members were born into their guild's ward. A guild's fortunes rose and fell with the power of the Oba it served.
you: You were born into the casters' ward and cast brass for the Oba, who alone may own what you make.
kid: Benin's brass casters made famous metal heads and plaques for the Oba's palace.

text: Most of the Oba's subjects were free farmers in the villages outside the capital, Edo-speaking in the heartland and of many other peoples in the conquered lands. They grew yams, whose first shoots were blessed each year at the royal festival of Ague, and other crops. A village was run by its elders, led by the oldest or most respected man, the odionwere, and sent tribute to the capital through the chiefs, who also settled its quarrels. When the Oba went to war, the village regiments, called up as needed, formed the bulk of his army, while the picked regiments of the capital stayed under arms. Tradition holds that Ewuare introduced marks cut into the skin of the freeborn to set them apart from slaves. Debt could be met by pawning: a person, often a child, served a creditor until the loan was repaid, protected by kinship from being sold as a slave.
rise: A freeborn man could move to the capital and enter a palace association, the first step towards a title, and war brought rewards. A crime could lead to execution or sale into slavery.

text: Slavery was part of Benin's order. Many of the enslaved were captives from the wars of expansion of the 15th and 16th centuries; others came as tribute from subject towns or as gifts to the Oba, and criminals could be sold into slavery. They were owned by the Oba, by chiefs and by ordinary people, and many slaves marked a man's standing. On the account of the historian Philip Igbafe, drawn mainly from the 19th century, they were the chiefs' main labour force and also served as fighting men. Slaves and ivory were traded at the Oba Market in the capital, and captives were sold to Portuguese and later Dutch traders, although pepper, ivory and later cloth counted for more in Benin's trade with Europe. Tradition honours a slave named Edo, who saved Ewuare's life and gave the city its name. Condemned criminals and slaves could be put to death in royal rites; historians such as James Graham argue that European accounts exaggerated how often.
rise: People became slaves through capture in war, a criminal conviction or tribute from subject towns; the sources for this period say little about how any left slavery.
you: You are one of the captives of the wars, part of a chief's labour force and one of his fighting men; he may sell you, even to European traders.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay; many were captives from wars.

== mobility
Benin's ladder was steep at the top but open in the middle. The kingship and the Uzama titles passed by birth, while most chiefly titles were the Oba's to give, so a freeborn man could rise by long service in a palace association or by valour in war, which the Oba rewarded with leopard ornaments and other emblems of rank. Ewuare used such appointments to build up chiefs loyal to himself, and in the towns he conquered he put his own men in place of the old rulers. Favour could raise a woman too: Idia, a wife of Oba Ozolua, became the first Iyoba. Falls were as sharp. A prince who lost the struggle for the throne, as Arhuaran did against Esigie, lost everything, and criminals could be executed or sold. Over the 17th century the balance tilted towards the chiefs: after Ehengbuda's death in 1606 reigns grew shorter, officials increasingly controlled the army and trade, and around 1689 the Iyase Ode led the revolt that opened a civil war.

== women
A woman's rank followed her father's and then her husband's, and almost every title was held by men. The great exception was the Iyoba, the queen mother. Tradition says that Oba Esigie created the title for his mother, Idia, who had helped him win the throne and the war against the Igala in 1515–1516, and that he ended an older custom of putting the new Oba's mother to death. The Iyoba ranked with the senior chiefs, had her own palace at Uselu, whose town was attached to the title, and her own regiment, and she was the only woman who could take part in war; after her son's installation she never saw him again, but advised him by messenger. The Oba's many wives lived within the palace. Women also traded: tradition remembers Emotan, a market trader who helped Ewuare win back the throne. Priestesses served Olokun, the god of the sea.
