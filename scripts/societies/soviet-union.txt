title: Social Hierarchy of Stalin's Soviet Union: From Party Elite to Gulag
desc: Stalin's Soviet Union by party, passport and camp: leader, nomenklatura, townspeople, collective farmers, the disenfranchised, deported kulaks, Gulag prisoners.
intro: Soviet law and ideology described society by class. The party counted the working class as its social base, and in Marxist–Leninist theory the better-off peasants, the kulaks, were class enemies of the poor. Under Stalin these categories became a ladder of rights. At the top a party elite, the nomenklatura, held every key post by the party's approval. From 1932 townspeople were given internal passports and residence permits, which peasants could obtain only by special application, and collectivization bound the peasants to farms they had no right to leave. Below them stood those the state excluded: the disenfranchised (lishentsy), until 1936; the peasants deported as kulaks; and the prisoners of the Gulag, whose camps and colonies held more than a million people by the late 1930s. In the famine of 1932–1933 some six to seven million people starved to death.

text: Joseph Stalin, the first general secretary of the Communist Party, ruled through the Politburo and the party apparatus he had built. His close attention to the party's appointments earned him the nickname "Comrade File Cabinet", and he placed his clients throughout the party's bureaucracy; after Lenin's death in 1924 he took control of the government. The great measures of the period bore his mark: the system of camps (June 1929), the order to "eliminate the kulaks as a class" (1929–1930), the Law of Spikelets, which he drafted, in August 1932, and the internal passport decree of December 1932. Under the tsars he had himself been sent into exile four times, and escaped three times.

text: The nomenklatura were the people who held, or were listed as fit to hold, the key posts in government, industry, agriculture and education, posts filled only with the party's approval. Every party committee, from the all-union level in Moscow down to the districts and towns, kept two lists: a basic list (osnovnoi spisok) of the posts it filled and a registration list (uchyotny spisok) of people suitable for them; the Politburo filled the ministerial and ambassadorial posts. Lenin had named the criteria: reliability, political attitude, qualifications and administrative ability. Virtually all were party members. The Yugoslav politician Milovan Đilas, a critic of Stalin, called them a "new class", seen by ordinary citizens as a privileged bureaucratic elite that had replaced the old capitalist one.
rise: No one was born into it: a party member entered by being placed on a committee's list and appointed to a post. Richard Pipes argued that the system continued the tsarist regime, as many former tsarist officials and careerists joined the Bolshevik government.
you: Your name is on the party committee's list, and with its approval you run a factory, a farm district or a department of a ministry; your post depends on the party that gave it.
kid: Party officials ran the factories, farms, schools and offices, and the Communist Party chose every one of them.

text: The party counted the working class as its social base, and millions moved through the country to train, take new jobs or escape repression as supposed kulaks. Passports and "labour books" recording work discipline were meant to restore order and, above all, keep peasants fleeing the famine out of the towns. text: Collectivization made the peasants members of collective farms (kolkhozy). The Standard Charter, law from the early 1930s, called the kolkhoz a cooperative of peasants who "voluntarily unite" and manage it democratically. In practice membership was forced, members had no right to leave and could take nothing with them if they did, and the farm's assembly rubber-stamped the plans of district and provincial officials. Members worked in brigades and were paid a share of the harvest by labour days, often with no cash; the state bought the grain at low fixed prices that hardly changed between 1929 and 1953. Private plots of about an acre, 3.9 per cent of the sown land in 1938, gave 21.5 per cent of farm output in 1937.
rise: Members had no right of free exit, and the passport that a move to town required was given to peasants only on special application; a capable member could become a brigade leader (brigadir), usually a local man.

text: The constitutions of 1918 and 1924 deprived whole categories of people of the right to vote or to be elected: those who hired labour for profit or lived on unearned income, private merchants and brokers, monks and clergy of all denominations, and the policemen and military officers of the old regime. The Bolsheviks used disenfranchisement as repression against those they classed as "enemies of the working people", and decrees added new categories. A lishenets (plural lishentsy) lost far more than the vote: he could hold no government post, receive no higher or technical education, join no collective farm or other cooperative, and was denied the help others had for jobs, housing and pensions. The Constitution of 1936 brought universal suffrage and abolished the category.
rise: Local election commissions could restore a lishenets's rights on proof of productive labour and loyalty to the Soviets; the Central Election Commission and the Presidium of the Central Executive Committee had the final word.

text: The Gulag, the Main Directorate of Correctional Labour Camps, was set up under the OGPU in 1930 and run by the NKVD from 1934. Its camps held ordinary criminals and political prisoners, many sentenced without trial by NKVD troikas; petty crimes and jokes about the government could bring a term, and Article 58 punished "counterrevolutionary activities". Most camps lay in remote north-eastern Siberia, the far north and the steppes of Kazakhstan, where prisoners felled timber, mined, and built canals and railways. Naftaly Frenkel's "nourishment scale", which tied food to output, often killed weaker prisoners within weeks. From 1934 to 1940 deaths in the camps ran four to six times the Soviet average, and under NKVD Order No. 00447 tens of thousands of inmates were executed in 1937–1938.
rise: Between 1934 and 1953, 20 to 40 per cent of the prisoners were released each year, and good workers could earn early release; Naftaly Frenkel himself went from inmate to camp commander. On release many political prisoners were barred from the big cities and sent into exile in remote regions.

== mobility
The state decided who stood where. The top was open only through the party: a member placed on a committee's list could be appointed to a post, and, as Richard Pipes noted, many former tsarist officials and careerists had joined the Bolshevik government. Labels could fall on anyone. Officials applied the definition of a kulak arbitrarily and settled personal rivalries with it; in 1932 and 1933 it was stretched to anyone who resisted the grain procurements, and the term came to be used for anyone who opposed or even seemed dissatisfied with the government. The party activists who helped the secret police with the arrests and deportations were, in Vasily Grossman's words, "all people who knew one another well, and knew their victims". Between 1934 and 1941 the number of prisoners with higher education rose more than eightfold. A few ways led back: local commissions could restore a lishenets's rights, and the 1936 Constitution ended the category; the Gulag released 20 to 40 per cent of its prisoners each year, though many were kept out of the big cities afterwards. The passport system kept most peasants in the countryside.

== women
Women belonged to every rank, and the regime's categories reached them through their families. On the collective farms a woman member was a kolkhoznitsa; in one farm the minimum was 130 labour days a year for every able-bodied adult and 50 for boys aged 12 to 16, and a few women became brigade leaders. The repression struck families: the deported kulaks were sent away with their relatives, and the family members of those condemned as "traitors of the Motherland" were placed under a special category of repression (ChSIR). The camps had separate zones for mothers with babies (mamki) and for juveniles. Women were among the prisoners: Yevgenia Ginzburg recalled that the inmates could tell when Nikolai Yezhov no longer headed the NKVD, because one day conditions eased. The propiska too followed family ties: spouses could always give one another a permanent propiska, and children received theirs at a parent's address.
