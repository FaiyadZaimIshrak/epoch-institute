title: Social Hierarchy of the Russian Empire: From Emperor to Serf
desc: Russia's social ladder before 1861: emperor, nobles, Orthodox clergy, merchants, townspeople, Cossacks, soldiers, state peasants, serfs and house serfs.
intro: Imperial Russia sorted its people by law into estates (sosloviya), and the Code of Laws of 1832 named four: the nobility, the clergy, the town-dwellers and the rural people. The first two paid no poll tax and gave no recruits to the army; the townspeople and the peasants paid the tax and filled the ranks. Above them all stood the emperor, an autocrat. State service could lift a man: Peter the Great's Table of Ranks of 1722 made officers and officials noble once they reached a set grade. The deepest divide ran through the peasantry, more than four people in five. In the official count of 1857–1859 some 23 million were serfs on private estates, bound to the land and to their lords' will, while about as many state peasants were personally free but tied to their communes. Serfdom ended only in 1861.

text: The emperor was an autocrat: no assembly shared his power, and titles, ranks and privileges were his to grant. Three emperors ruled in these decades: Alexander I (1801–1825), who drove Napoleon's army out of Russia in 1812; his brother Nicholas I (1825–1855), who began his reign by crushing the Decembrist revolt of noble officers; and Nicholas's son Alexander II (1855–1881), who freed the serfs. The emperor governed through ministers, the State Council and a bureaucracy graded by the Table of Ranks, and through the Holy Synod, overseen by his lay procurator, he directed the Orthodox Church. Emperors lived in Saint Petersburg but were crowned in the Dormition Cathedral of the Moscow Kremlin. Even an autocrat moved carefully: fearing unrest and the landlords' resistance, Alexander I and Nicholas I studied emancipation in secret committees, and in a speech of 30 March 1856 Alexander II said: "It is better to abolish serfdom from above, than to wait for that time when it starts to abolish itself from below."
rise: The throne passed to the emperor's eldest son or, failing sons, to a brother: Alexander I left no son and was succeeded by his younger brother Nicholas I, after their brother Constantine had given up his claim.
you: You rule as an autocrat, crowned in Moscow and sharing power with no assembly, but you must keep the landlords loyal and fear unrest among the serfs.
kid: The emperor, or tsar, ruled Russia alone, with no parliament to share his power.

text: The nobility (dvoryanstvo) was the first estate. It included old princely families, Baltic German barons and, after the partitions of Poland, a mass of Polish gentry, 62.8 per cent of the empire's nobles in 1858. Nobility was hereditary or personal: Peter the Great's Table of Ranks granted personal nobility in its lower grades and hereditary nobility higher up, until 1845 at the eighth civil grade. Since 1762 nobles had been free of compulsory service, and the Charter to the Gentry of 1785 confirmed their privileges: freedom from the poll tax and corporal punishment, full rights over their estates and serfs, and assemblies of the nobility in every province. Most still served for some years as officers or officials. Wealth was very unequal: in 1858 some 1,400 great landowners held three million serfs, while 79,000 small ones held two million between them, and by 1859 two-thirds of all serfs were mortgaged to banks or the state. In 1825 noble officers, the Decembrists, rose for a constitution and were crushed.
rise: Nobility came by birth, by imperial grant, or by rising through the Table of Ranks: Mikhail Speransky, a village priest's son, rose through the civil service to become Alexander I's leading adviser and was made a count in 1839.
you: You own an estate and the serfs who work it, pay no poll tax and cannot be flogged, and you need not serve, though you probably spend some years as an officer; your estate may well be mortgaged.
kid: Many nobles owned large country estates and the serfs who farmed them.

text: The Orthodox clergy was the second estate free of the poll tax and of the recruit levy, but it was divided. The black (monastic) clergy lived in monasteries under vows of celibacy, and all bishops were drawn from them. The white clergy were the parish priests and deacons, who were expected to marry and could do so only before ordination. In practice they formed a hereditary class, whose sons trained in the seminaries and were expected to become priests. Most parish priests were poor and closer to the peasants than to the bishops. Since Peter the Great the church had been governed by the Holy Synod, under a lay procurator who answered to the emperor; in 1764 Catherine II took most of its lands and closed 569 of its 954 monasteries, and from 1797 bishops, not parishioners, chose parish priests. Philaret Drozdov, a cleric's son who became a monk in 1808, headed the see of Moscow from 1821 to 1867 and was the church's leading figure.
rise: Priests' sons usually followed their fathers, but the seminaries also fed the civil service: Mikhail Speransky, a village priest's son, taught at the seminary in Saint Petersburg before entering state service and rising to the top.
you: You are a priest's son and a priest yourself, married before your ordination; you pay no poll tax and cannot be taken as a recruit, but your parish is poor and you live much like your parishioners.
kid: Orthodox parish priests could marry, and their sons were expected to become priests too.

text: The merchants (kupechestvo) were enrolled in three guilds according to the capital they declared, under Catherine II's rules of 1775, each guild with its own trading rights. Membership was paid for and not simply inherited: a merchant registered with the state for a fee, his relatives could trade on his behalf, but his grown sons had to qualify on their own, so a family that could no longer meet the requirements fell back among the townspeople. Rich merchants founded factories, and the law let them buy serfs to work in them from 1721 to 1762 and again from 1798 to 1816; in 1825 about half of all factory workers were serfs. From 1832 the most distinguished could become honorary citizens, ranking between the merchants and the nobles, free of the poll tax, the recruit levy and corporal punishment. Yet the merchants had little political weight and were looked down on by the nobility, though only the nobles were more literate.
rise: Anyone who could declare enough capital and pay the fees could enrol in a guild, and some merchants began as townspeople or even serfs: Savva Morozov, a serf, bought freedom for himself and his sons in 1820, and the family went on to found the Morozov textile companies.
you: You have declared your capital and enrolled in a merchant guild, and your business may grow rich, but the nobles look down on you and you have no say in how the empire is ruled.
kid: Merchants were traders who paid to join a merchant guild, which gave them the right to trade.

text: The meshchane were the lower urban estate, ranked above the peasants and below the merchant guilds: small traders, artisans, shopkeepers and urban workers who were registered in a town but could not meet the merchants' requirements. Craftsmen could also enrol in craft guilds (tsekhi). The status was hereditary, but a man who owned some property in a town, worked there at a trade, a craft or a service and paid the taxes could enrol himself, and the townspeople's own board (meshchanskaya uprava) could expel members. Like the peasants, the meshchane paid the poll tax and gave recruits to the army, and they were not spared corporal punishment. Towns were few and small: as late as 1851 only 8 per cent of Russians lived in them, and some of those who did were registered as "urban peasants" rather than townspeople. Unlike Western Europe's, Russia's towns had produced no strong middle class.
rise: A townsman who gathered enough capital could enrol as a merchant, and peasants allowed to settle in a town could join the meshchane if they owned property and worked there.
you: You are registered in your town as one of its meshchane, keep a small shop or work at a craft, pay the poll tax and may be taken as a recruit.
kid: Townspeople ran small shops and workshops in Russia's towns and paid taxes to the government.

text: The Cossacks, once free frontier communities of runaway serfs, adventurers and steppe warriors, had become a hereditary military estate of the empire by the end of the 18th century. They lived in hosts, the Don, the Black Sea, the Ural, the Terek and others, settled in villages (stanitsas) along the southern and eastern frontiers, and kept a degree of self-government. Every Cossack family held land free of taxes, and in return its men owed military service for many years, bringing their own horses and equipment; they were spared the ordinary recruit levy. Cossack regiments scouted, raided and patrolled the frontiers for the army: in 1812 Matvei Platov, ataman of the Don Host, led his Cossacks against Napoleon's retreating army. The elite of the hosts was admitted to the nobility, and some rich Cossacks owned serfs. Many Cossacks of the Ural and the Terek were Old Believers. The government remembered the risings of Stenka Razin and Yemelyan Pugachev and kept the hosts loyal with land and privileges.
rise: Cossack status passed from father to son, and the officers of the hosts could be admitted to the nobility.
you: You hold land free of taxes in your Cossack village, and in return you serve for years in your host's regiments with your own horse and equipment.
kid: Cossacks were horsemen of the frontier who were given land in return for serving in the army.

text: The army's rank and file were recruits drawn from the estates that paid the poll tax, above all the peasants. Each levy demanded a quota of men aged about twenty; village elders or landlords chose whom to send, often by lot, and landlords used the levy to rid themselves of troublesome serfs. From 1793 a recruit served for 25 years; in 1834 the term was cut to 20 years with 5 more in the reserve, and in 1855 to 12 years with 3 in the reserve. A serf taken as a recruit left his master's power, and a soldier who survived his service was a free man, but the service was long and harsh, far from home. Literate recruits might become clerks, and good soldiers non-commissioned officers, while officers' commissions went overwhelmingly to nobles: in the Crimean War nine officers in ten were noble. Some villages of state peasants were turned into military settlements, where the men were soldiers and farmers at once.

text: About as many peasants lived on the land of the state as on private estates. In law the state peasants were "free rural inhabitants": no one could sell them or interfere in their family life, and they could go to court, trade, own property and, from 1801, buy land of their own. They lived in village communes on state land, paid the poll tax and a quit-rent (obrok) to the treasury and gave recruits to the army. They were attached to their communes, and their freedom of movement was restricted. Their numbers grew as church lands were secularised and new lands conquered, from 19 per cent of the people in 1724 to 45 per cent by 1858 in the same territory. Shrinking allotments and heavier dues led to unrest, such as the potato riots of 1834 and 1840–1841, and from 1837 Pavel Kiselyov's new Ministry of State Property reformed their administration and dues. The appanage peasants, who belonged to the imperial family, were likewise personally free.

text: Serfs were peasants owned, with the land they lived on, by noble landlords, and bound to it since the Law Code of 1649. Most lived in villages whose commune (mir) shared out the strips of land among households, while the lord answered to the state for their poll tax. They served their lord by labour on his fields (barshchina), commonly three days a week, which Paul I's decree of 1797 called normal, or by a quit-rent (obrok) in money or kind, often earned away from home. Landlords took at least a third of their peasants' income. The lord judged and punished his serfs, had to approve their marriages, could send them into the army or at times into Siberian exile, and could sell them with or without land. The law forbade him to kill them; Alexander I banned advertising serfs for sale without land (1801) and selling them at fairs (1808), and from 1833 families could not be split at public sales. Serfs resisted by flight and revolt.
rise: A serf could be freed by his master, buy his freedom if the master agreed, or leave serfdom as a recruit. From 1803 lords could free whole villages as "free agriculturalists", but by 1858 only about 152,000 male serfs, 1.5 per cent, had been freed that way.
you: You work your lord's fields three days a week or pay him a quit-rent, and he can punish you, sell you, send you to the army and must approve your marriage.
kid: Serfs were farmers who belonged to a landowner and were not allowed to leave his estate.

text: Beside the field serfs lived the household serfs (dvorovye), who had no land and served in their master's house and stables as cooks, coachmen, grooms, maids, footmen, nurses and stewards. Having no land, they could be sold on their own. Their share of the serfs grew from 4.14 per cent in 1835 to 6.79 per cent by 1858, as landlords who expected emancipation turned field serfs into servants. Great households kept many of them, and some were trained as musicians, painters, singers or actors: the Sheremetev counts ran a serf theatre whose star, the soprano Praskovia Zhemchugova, was a serf blacksmith's daughter. Household serfs lived closest to their owners, which could bring favour or cruelty. Taras Shevchenko, born a serf in 1814, was made a house servant of his lord at fourteen and whipped for drawing at night; his talent won him friends in Saint Petersburg, who raised 2,500 roubles in 1838 to buy his freedom. At emancipation in 1861 household serfs were freed, but received no land.
rise: Freedom came by an owner's grace or by purchase: Count Nikolai Sheremetev freed Praskovia Zhemchugova in 1798 and married her in secret in 1801.

== mobility
The estates were fixed in law, but the state opened some doors. The Table of Ranks turned officers and officials into nobles, and by the 1850s fewer than half of all civil servants were nobles: Mikhail Speransky, a village priest's son, became Alexander I's leading adviser and a count. Trade lifted others: Savva Morozov, a serf, bought freedom for himself and his sons in 1820, and the family went on to found the Morozov textile companies. Serfs left bondage as recruits, by purchase or through talent: Taras Shevchenko, a household serf bought free in 1838, wrote the poetry now regarded as the foundation of modern Ukrainian literature. Others simply fled, as runaways had long done to the Cossack frontier. Falls came too: the Decembrist officers were hanged or exiled to Siberia, and indebted nobles mortgaged their serfs. The great change came with Alexander II's manifesto of 19 February 1861 (3 March in the Western calendar), which freed some 23 million serfs, though they had to pay for their land over 49 years.

== women
A woman belonged to her father's estate and then her husband's. Noblewomen were better placed than in much of Europe: since the 1730s they could inherit land, and married noblewomen and merchants' wives owned and managed their own property, which let some run estates and businesses. Girls of the nobility were taught at home, mostly to be wives and mothers, and women could hold no office; in 1859 Saint Petersburg University let women attend its lectures, but the permission was withdrawn four years later. Peasant women shared the field work, spun, wove and kept the house. A serf woman needed her owner's consent to marry; the legal age of marriage was raised in 1830 to 16 for women, and a wife's standing in her husband's family rose with each son she bore. The serf soprano Praskovia Zhemchugova married Count Nikolai Sheremetev in 1801, a match that scandalised society.
