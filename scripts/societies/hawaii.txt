title: Social Hierarchy of Hawaii under the Kapu: From Aliʻi Nui to Kauwā
desc: Hawaii's social ladder under the kapu, 1778–1819: the paramount chief, high chiefs, priests and experts, land agents, commoners and the outcast kauwā.
intro: Hawaiian society at Cook's arrival in 1778 ranked people by birth, as steeply as any in Polynesia. The chiefs (aliʻi) traced their descent from the gods and were believed to carry mana, a sacred power greatest in the highest-born, and a web of kapu, sacred restrictions often enforced by death, guarded it: commoners lay face down before the most sacred chiefs, and men and women ate apart. Each island kingdom, and after Kamehameha I's conquests of 1795–1810 the whole chain, had one ruler, who shared out the land among the chiefs and their agents (konohiki). Priests and experts (kahuna) served the chiefs, the commoners (makaʻāinana) farmed and fished for them all, and the outcast kauwā stood below everyone. Most of what is known comes from the Hawaiian scholars David Malo, Samuel Kamakau and John Papa ʻĪʻī, writing after the kapu ended in 1819, and from European visitors' journals.

text: Each island kingdom had its paramount chief (aliʻi nui), the greatest of the chiefs in power, if not always in birth. All land was held to belong to the gods, and the ruler shared it out among the chiefs at his accession, so that every chief owed his lands to him. Only a ruling chief could build a luakini, a war temple where human sacrifices were offered: others who built one were treated as rebels. At the end of each Makahiki season warriors hurled spears at him as he came ashore, and he had to parry them to prove himself worthy to rule. In 1782 Kamehameha, guardian of the war god Kūkāʻilimoku, defeated his cousin Kīwalaʻō, Kalaniʻōpuʻu's son and successor, at Mokuʻōhai, and by 1795, with Western guns, ships and advisers, he had conquered every main island but Kauaʻi and Niʻihau, whose ruler accepted him as overlord in 1810. As king he governed through appointed governors, controlled the sandalwood trade and upheld the kapu until his death in 1819.
rise: A ruler usually named a high-born son as his heir, but rivals could take the land by war: Kamehameha overthrew Kalaniʻōpuʻu's successor Kīwalaʻō in 1782, and Kalanikūpule of Oʻahu, defeated at Nuʻuanu in 1795, was captured and sacrificed to the war god.
you: You share out the land among your chiefs, you alone may build a war temple, and at the end of each Makahiki you must parry the warriors' spears to prove yourself worthy to rule.
kid: Kamehameha I was a great chief who united the Hawaiian Islands and became their first king.

text: The chiefs (aliʻi) traced their descent from the gods, and a chief's rank depended on the genealogy of both parents. Eleven grades were distinguished. Highest were the nīʻaupiʻo, children of a full brother and sister (piʻo) or of half-siblings and other close kin (naha), who were considered living gods, though David Malo, Samuel Kamakau and Abraham Fornander define some grades differently. Kamehameha I was of the lower wohi grade, which exempted him from prostrating himself before the highest. The most sacred chiefs held the kapu moe: commoners had to fall face down in their presence, and to touch such a chief's shadow or clothing, or to stand with one's head above his, could be punished by death. High chiefs wore cloaks and helmets of red and yellow feathers, a privilege of the highest ranks, kept attendants who carried feather standards (kāhili), and governed the districts (moku) as aliʻi ʻaimoku, or under Kamehameha whole islands as governors. Their lands were the ruler's gift and could change hands with each reign.
rise: Rank came by birth from both parents, so a chief who married a higher-born wife had children who outranked him, while lines that married lower sank. Lands and offices, unlike rank, were won in war: the chiefs who backed Kamehameha after 1782 shared out the lands of the defeated.
you: You trace your descent from the gods, wear cloaks of red and yellow feathers and govern a district for the ruler, and commoners must not touch your shadow or stand with their heads above yours.
kid: High chiefs wore cloaks made from many thousands of tiny red and yellow bird feathers.

text: Below the great chiefs stood the lesser chiefs (kaukaualiʻi), born of a lower-ranking parent, whose lines sank further with every marriage below their rank. They served the ruler and the high chiefs as attendants and stewards and, above all, as konohiki, the agents who managed an ahupuaʻa, a land division that usually ran from the mountains to the sea. A konohiki was usually a kinsman of the chief who held the land. He shared out the plots and the water among the farmers, oversaw the fields and their upkeep, closed and opened the fishing seasons with the priests, and made sure that the right tribute reached the chief at the right times: at the Makahiki he gathered the pigs, taro, sweet potatoes, dried fish, bark cloth (kapa) and mats offered at the altar on the boundary of the ahupuaʻa. His post depended on his chief. When the land passed to a new chief, the farmers usually stayed, but under a new konohiki.
rise: Lesser chiefs rose by serving a successful chief or by marrying into a higher line, and sank by marrying lower; a konohiki kept his post only as long as his chief held the land.
you: You run an ahupuaʻa for your chief: you share out the plots and the water among the farmers and gather their pigs, taro and bark cloth at the Makahiki, but a new chief may bring his own konohiki.
kid: Konohiki looked after a slice of land for a chief and collected the food and cloth the farmers gave him.

text: A kahuna was an expert, and the word covered priests as well as the masters of a craft or body of knowledge: healers (kahuna lapaʻau), canoe builders, navigators, house builders, genealogists and chanters. The priests served the gods at the temples (heiau), and the greatest of them served the ruler. Priesthood often passed within families: Hewahewa, kahuna nui (high priest) to Kamehameha I and his son, descended from the priest Paʻao, said to have come from Tahiti, and his great-grandfather had been priest to Kalaniʻōpuʻu. Hewahewa's order of the war god Kū rose with Kamehameha's wars and displaced the priests of Lono. Only chiefs and the priests of Kū could enter the luakini, and at the Makahiki priests carried the image of Lono around the island to receive the offerings of each land division. Kamehameha built Puʻukoholā, completed in 1791, on the advice of a respected kahuna, Kapoukahi, and gave Hewahewa Waimea Valley on Oʻahu. A master trained each apprentice in his own household, and the two were often relatives.
rise: Priestly office often passed from father to son and craft knowledge from master to apprentice, and a kahuna who served a victorious ruler could gain lands. In 1819 Hewahewa himself ordered the images burned and the temples destroyed: the temple priesthood ended, though healers and craftsmen carried on.
you: You are a kahuna: a priest of the gods at a temple, or a master of a craft such as healing or canoe building, trained as an apprentice in your master's household.
kid: Kahuna were experts: some were priests, and others were healers, canoe builders or navigators.

text: Most Hawaiians were commoners (makaʻāinana), who farmed, fished and practised the simpler crafts. They grew taro in irrigated pond fields and sweet potato on drier slopes, and kept pigs, dogs and chickens. Each household worked plots within an ahupuaʻa and gave part of its harvest and catch to the chief through his konohiki; it also owed him labour, and in war the men fought for him. Thousands built Puʻukoholā for Kamehameha in 1790–1791, passing its stones along a human chain said to have stretched for miles. Commoners had to fall face down before the most sacred chiefs, and breaking a kapu could mean death, unless the offender reached a place of refuge (puʻuhonua) such as Hōnaunau, where a priest absolved him. In the 1790s Kamehameha pardoned a fisherman who had struck him while defending his family, and proclaimed the Law of the Splintered Paddle: old people, women and children might lie by the roadside in safety. Unlike serfs, commoners could move to another chief's land, though how freely is debated.
rise: Commoners were born to their rank and could not become chiefs, though the child of a high chief and a commoner counted as a chief of low grade (aliʻi noanoa). A family could move to another chief's land, but not out of its class.
you: You farm taro or fish within an ahupuaʻa, hand part of your harvest and catch to the chief's konohiki, and must fall face down before a sacred chief; you may move to another chief's land.
kid: Most Hawaiians were farmers and fishers who grew taro, caught fish and gave part of their food to the chiefs.

text: At the bottom stood the kauwā, a hereditary group set apart from every other class and despised by it. The kauwā worked for the chiefs, and marriage between kauwā and anyone of a higher class was strictly forbidden, so their condition passed to their children. Some accounts say that kauwā men were often taken as victims for the human sacrifices of the luakini, the war temples, which also claimed lawbreakers and defeated enemies, such as Kalanikūpule of Oʻahu, sacrificed to the war god in 1795. Beyond this, little is certain. The sources say little about the kauwā and differ on their origin, and nobody recorded how many there were. Writers in English have called them slaves, servants or outcasts, and the Hawaiian sources do not settle which word fits best. The end of the kapu in 1819 ended the human sacrifices.
rise: There was no known way out: kauwā status passed from parents to children, and marriage outside the group was forbidden.
you: You are kauwā, set apart and despised: you work for the chiefs, no one of a higher class may marry you, and you may be taken as a sacrifice at a war temple.
kid: The kauwā were outcasts: other Hawaiians were forbidden to marry them, and they worked for the chiefs.

== mobility
Rank in Hawaii was genealogy, so no one could make himself a chief, but the order was not still. Rank came from both parents: a chief's children by a higher-born wife outranked him, and Kamehameha I lay on his back to let his children by the sacred Keōpūolani sit on his chest. Lesser chiefs climbed by marrying into higher lines, and lines that married lower sank towards the commoners. Power, unlike rank, was won in war. Every new ruler shared out the land again, and the chiefs who backed Kamehameha after 1782 took the lands of the defeated: his ally Keʻeaumoku Pāpaʻiahiahi, once a fugitive, became governor of Maui, and Kalanimoku, a young chief captured on the losing side at Mokuʻōhai, rose to be Kamehameha's chief minister and treasurer. The British sailor John Young, detained in 1790, married into the chiefly class and governed the island of Hawaiʻi from about 1802 to 1812. Commoners could change chiefs but not class, and the kauwā could leave neither.

== women
Rank passed through mothers as well as fathers, and women could be chiefs in their own right. Keōpūolani, of naha rank, outranked her husband Kamehameha I: commoners had to lie face down before her, and only her sons succeeded him. The ʻai kapu bound women of every class. They ate apart from men, in their own eating house, were forbidden pork, coconuts, most bananas and some fish, and kept apart from the community while menstruating. In ordinary households, women made kapa bark cloth and wove mats, while men grew the taro and cooked in the earth oven (imu). In 1819 Kaʻahumanu, Kamehameha's favourite wife, became kuhina nui, co-ruler with his young son Liholiho. That year she and Keōpūolani, with the high priest Hewahewa, persuaded the new king to eat with women, and the ʻAi Noa, "free eating", brought the kapu system down.
