title: Social Hierarchy of the Mughal Empire: From Emperor to Enslaved
desc: Mughal India's social ladder: emperor, mansabdar nobles, zamindars, religious elites, merchants, artisans, peasants, the lowest castes and the enslaved.
intro: Mughal India ranked people by their place in the emperor's service, by their rights over land and by birth. The emperor granted every rank in an imperial service that was army, nobility and government at once: the mansabdars, a deliberately mixed elite of Central Asians, Iranians, Afghans, Indian Muslims and Rajputs, paid with assignments of land revenue that he moved at will. Below them the zamindars held hereditary rights over the revenue of the countryside, from Rajput kings to village landholders, and religion gave its own honour to Muslim scholars, Sufi shaikhs and Brahmin priests. The great majority were peasants, who owed a third or more of their harvest to the state, and society was divided into many castes, the lowest held to be untouchable. The ladder was steep: around 1650 the 445 highest-ranked nobles drew 61 per cent of the empire's revenue.

firm: yes
terms: peacock-throne, red-fort
people: akbar, jahangir, shah-jahan, aurangzeb
text: The emperor, the pādshāh, was the source of every rank. He granted, raised and cut the mansabs of the nobility and assigned and withdrew the revenue that paid them, and when a noble died his estate came under the emperor's control, the heirs receiving what he chose to give them. Akbar divided the power of a single chief minister among four, for finance, the army, the household and religious grants, so that none could dominate. In law the emperor was the final judge: Jahangir hung a "chain of justice" in the Agra Fort that any wronged subject could shake. The revenue of the crown lands, the khalisa, which was never assigned to nobles, kept his household and paid his own troops. No rule fixed the succession. Princes held ranks and armies of their own and fought for the throne: Aurangzeb defeated his brother Dara Shikoh in 1659 and kept their father Shah Jahan a prisoner until his death in 1666.
rise: Only a prince of the dynasty, descended from Timur, could reign, and the throne went to the son who won the struggle that followed an emperor's illness or death. Akbar came to it in 1556, aged thirteen, under the regent Bairam Khan.
you: You grant and take away every noble's rank and income and hear appeals as the final judge, but your sons command armies of their own and may fight each other, or you, for your throne.
kid: The Mughal emperor gave every noble his rank, and when he died his sons often fought each other for the throne.

firm: yes
terms: mansabdari-system, jagir
people: man-singh-i, todar-mal, abdul-rahim-khan-i-khanan, jai-singh-i
text: Under the mansabdari system every officer of the empire above the lowest levels, judges apart, held a mansab, a numbered rank in one service that was army, nobility and civil government at once. From about 1595 each rank had two numbers: the zat fixed a man's pay and precedence, the sawar the horsemen he had to keep. Ranks ran from 10 up to 5,000, later 7,000, for anyone but a prince, and only holders of 500, later 1,000, and above counted as nobles (umara). Lesser officers were often paid in cash; most of the rest held a jagir, the right to collect the land revenue of named villages, which gave no ownership of the land and was moved every three or four years. The mansabdars numbered 1,823 in 1595 and some 8,000 by 1650, when the 445 highest drew 61 per cent of the empire's revenue. The emperors mixed them on purpose: Turanis from Central Asia, Iranis, Afghans, Indian Muslims, Rajputs and, under Aurangzeb, Deccanis and Marathas.
rise: A newcomer needed a patron to present him to the paymaster general and money for horses and men; promotions came at festivals, on campaigns or for a handsome present. No rank or title was inherited, but sons of the service, the khanazads, held nearly half the high ranks under Aurangzeb.
you: You hold a numbered rank, keep a troop of horsemen ready for the emperor, and collect the revenue of villages assigned to you until he moves you on, every three or four years.
kid: Mughal nobles held numbered ranks that showed how many horsemen they had to keep ready for the emperor.

text: The zamindars held hereditary rights over the revenue of the land, and the word covered men of very different weight. Mughal chroniclers used it for the rajas who ruled their own territories from forts, two to three hundred of them under Akbar by one contemporary count, for intermediaries over whole districts, and for landholders with rights in a single village. They collected the land revenue from the peasants for the state and kept a customary share, and were expected to advance loans and tools in hard times. Many commanded armed kinsmen: the Ain-i-Akbari puts the zamindars' forces at 384,558 horsemen and over four million foot, a local militia rather than an army. Most great zamindars were Hindus of high castes, among them Rajputs, Brahmins and Kayasthas, but there were Jat and Muslim zamindars too, and Rajput kings who entered the imperial service kept their ancestral lands as hereditary jagirs. Under too heavy a demand, zamindars took up arms.
rise: Zamindari rights passed by inheritance, though the choice of heir depended in part on the emperor's pleasure. A chief who submitted might be given a mansab; in eastern Bengal Isa Khan accepted only a nominal Mughal overlordship and kept his autonomy.
you: You hold the hereditary right to collect the revenue of your villages and keep a share of it, and you lead armed kinsmen who may one day hold your fort against the emperor's troops.
kid: Zamindars were local landholders and chiefs who collected taxes from farmers and kept part for themselves.

text: Learning and holiness formed a rank of their own in both great faiths. The ulama, scholars of Islamic law, sat as qazis, from the district judge to the chief qazi who travelled with the imperial camp, and Sufi shaikhs kept shrines that emperors themselves visited: Akbar prayed at the tomb of Moinuddin Chishti at Ajmer and built Fatehpur Sikri beside the shrine of Salim Chishti. Brahmin priests and scholars performed the rites of temple and household and kept Sanskrit learning alive; the Brahmin poet Tulsidas retold the Ramayana in Awadhi. A minister, the sadr, managed the madad-i-ma'ash, tax-free grants of land revenue to the learned and the pious, and Jahangir gave many more than Akbar. Favour followed policy: Akbar's House of Worship, built in 1575, came to host debates among men of every faith, while Aurangzeb had 500 jurists compile the Fatawa-i-Alamgiri and in 1672–73 ordered the grants held by Hindus taken back.
rise: Muslim scholars rose by learning and patronage, and a trained jurist could be appointed qazi by the emperor or the sadr. Brahmin rank came by birth, and some Brahmins lived as landholders, officials or farmers instead.
you: You teach the law or keep a holy man's shrine and live on a tax-free grant of revenue from the emperor, which a new reign or a new policy can take away.
kid: Muslim scholars and judges, Sufi holy men and Hindu priests called Brahmins were honoured for their learning.

text: The great merchants and bankers were rich, and the empire's silver economy ran through their hands. Long-distance trade lay largely with communities such as the Banias, Marwaris and Khatris, the Muslim Bohras, the Armenians and the Parsis. Sarrafs, money-changers and bankers, issued hundis, bills of exchange that could be cashed in distant cities. At Surat, the empire's great port, Virji Vora traded in pepper, bullion, coral and opium through agents from Agra to Calicut, lent to the English and Dutch companies, and was said by the English to be worth 8 million rupees. Wealth brought no rank in the imperial order, however. In 1638 the governor of Surat jailed Virji Vora until Shah Jahan summoned him to court; Aurangzeb taxed Hindu merchants at 5 per cent against 2.5 for Muslims; and Shivaji's raid on Surat in 1664 plundered Virji Vora's house and warehouses.
rise: Trade and banking ran in families and communities. A few merchants crossed into the imperial service, as the Persian diamond trader Mir Jumla II did, rising to be a Mughal general and governor of Bengal; extortion by officials or a raid could ruin a house.
you: You deal in spices, bullion and coral through agents in many cities and lend to the European companies, but a governor can jail you and raiders can strip your warehouses.
kid: Mughal merchants and bankers grew rich from trade and sent money between distant cities using written bills.

text: Mughal India had many large towns: under Akbar, Nizamuddin Ahmad counted 120 great cities and 3,200 townships, and Agra, Lahore and Delhi may each have held several hundred thousand people. Their artisans, shopkeepers, moneylenders and labourers lived by the court and the market. Weaving was the greatest industry: the cottons, calicoes and muslins of Bengal and other provinces sold across Asia and Europe, and in the early 18th century India held a quarter of the world's trade in textiles. In the emperor's karkhanas, or workshops, craftsmen made arms, clothes, jewellery, carpets and tents for the court. Most crafts belonged to particular jatis, hereditary groups that married among themselves. Wages for manual labour were low, and a town's fortunes rose and fell with the court: Shah Jahan's new capital at Delhi, Shahjahanabad, completed in 1648, drew people to it.
rise: Most artisans followed the craft of their family and jati. The most skilled could find work in the imperial workshops, and people moved to wherever the court and its demand went.
you: You weave cloth or work metal at a trade your family has followed for generations, sell to merchants who ship your goods across the seas, and earn low wages.
kid: Mughal weavers made fine cotton cloth, such as muslin, that people bought all over Asia and Europe.

level: 5
band: commoners
name: Peasants
term: raiyat, khud-kāsht, pāhī-kāsht
local: Raiyat
share: rest
sharenote: Everyone not counted in the other tiers: the great majority (Shireen Moosvi reckons 82 per cent of the late-16th-century labour force rural). Several of the other ranks are estimates, so this share is one too.
firm: yes
terms: zabt-system
people:
text: The peasants, the raiyat, were the great majority and paid for the empire. Under the zabt system worked out by Todar Mal from 1580, fields in the core provinces were measured and the state's demand fixed in cash at a third of the average crop of the previous ten years; elsewhere the crop was shared or assessed village by village. In practice, Irfan Habib argued, added cesses and assignees who held their villages for only a few years had pushed the demand to half the crop or more in many places by Aurangzeb's reign. Resident cultivators, the khud-kasht, had a hereditary right to till their fields as long as they paid; the pahi-kasht farmed in other villages at lower rates. Peasants grew wheat, rice and barley to eat and cotton, indigo, sugar cane and opium for sale, and took up maize and tobacco from the Americas between 1600 and 1650. When the burden grew too heavy they fled or rose, as the Jats around Mathura did in 1669.
rise: Clearing new land earned a peasant tax-free years; a heavy demand could drive a family from its fields, and revenue officers could even enslave peasants who failed to pay.
you: You till fields your family has a hereditary right to farm, owe the state a third or more of your crop in cash, and grow cotton or indigo for sale as well as grain to eat.
kid: Most people in Mughal India were farmers who grew wheat, rice and cotton and paid heavy taxes on their harvests.

firm: yes

people:
text: Below the peasants stood the castes that Brahmin texts placed outside the four varnas altogether and that higher castes treated as untouchable, because their hereditary work, such as tanning hides and sweeping, was counted as polluting. The four varnas were a scheme of the texts; lived society was made of many jatis, groups born to an occupation that married among themselves, and historians debate how rigid the order was before British rule, whose censuses and laws hardened it. Irfan Habib argued that custom kept the lowest castes from holding land, so that they worked other men's fields and served as village menials. They were not voiceless. Ravidas, a poet-saint born to a leather-working family, preached against caste, and his verses entered the Sikh scripture, the Adi Granth. In 1672 the Satnamis of Narnaul, a sect that rejected caste and drew many followers from low-caste leatherworkers, rose against Mughal officials; the court chronicler sneered at them as goldsmiths, carpenters, sweepers and tanners.
rise: Caste came by birth. Devotional movements that rejected caste, like those of Ravidas and the Satnamis, offered dignity and community, though not a higher rank.
you: You were born into a caste that the higher castes treat as untouchable; you tan hides, sweep or work other men's fields, for your family holds no land of its own.
kid: People of the lowest castes were kept apart by higher castes and did work such as tanning leather and sweeping.

text: Slavery in Mughal India was mostly domestic. Enslaved men and women served in the households of the emperor and the nobles as servants, concubines and eunuchs, and were given as gifts and dowries: when Prince Salim married the daughter of the Rajput raja Bhagwant Das, her dowry included male and female slaves of Abyssinian, Caucasian and Indian origin. People were enslaved in war, when the women and children of defeated rebels were taken and shared among officers, and through the revenue system: a missionary in Bengal in 1629–30 reported that revenue officers could enslave peasants who failed to pay. Others were bought, among them Ethiopians, called Habshis, prized as soldiers and servants, while Indian captives were sold north to Central Asia. The Fatawa-i-Alamgiri, Aurangzeb's code of Islamic law, denied slaves inheritance and the right to testify and required their owner's leave to marry, but it also set out how they could be freed.
rise: Freedom came by manumission, in whole or in part, under rules the jurists set out. Slave soldiers could rise far: in the neighbouring Ahmadnagar Sultanate the Ethiopian Malik Ambar, brought to India as a slave, became regent and led the Deccan's resistance to the Mughals.
you: You were taken in war or seized for unpaid revenue and now serve in a great household; you may not inherit, testify in court or marry without your owner's leave, though your owner may free you.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay, mostly as servants in rich households.

== mobility
Mughal society was steep, but its summit was open to talent, fortune and the emperor's favour. No rank in the imperial service was hereditary, and emperors kept recruiting new men from Iran, Central Asia and the Deccan. Todar Mal began as a clerk and rose to be Akbar's finance minister with a rank of 4,000. Mirza Ghiyas Beg reached India from Iran impoverished and held only a modest post until his daughter married Jahangir in 1611 as Nur Jahan; he then became chief minister. The Persian diamond merchant Mir Jumla II became a Mughal general and governor of Bengal. Rajput chiefs entered the nobility by alliance, the Kachhwahas of Amber from 1562, and Aurangzeb won over Deccani nobles and Maratha chiefs with high ranks. Yet sons of the service held nearly half the high ranks under Aurangzeb, and below the elite change was slow: castes kept their crafts, peasants could win tax-free years by clearing new land, and a heavy revenue demand drove families to flight or revolt.

== women
Women's lives followed the rank of their families. Some women of the imperial household held great power and wealth. Akbar's wife Mariam-uz-Zamani owned trading ships; Nur Jahan, Jahangir's empress from 1611, held the imperial seal, had coins struck in her name, traded in indigo and cloth, and built caravanserais and her father's tomb at Agra; Shah Jahan's daughter Jahanara held the revenues of the port of Surat, owned ships and laid out Chandni Chowk in Shahjahanabad. Islamic law gave a Muslim daughter half the inheritance of a son and let women own property in their own right. Among Hindus, sati, a widow's burning on her husband's pyre, was practised in some families, and Aurangzeb ordered it banned in 1663. Peasant and artisan women worked alongside their families, and enslaved women were at the disposal of their owners.
