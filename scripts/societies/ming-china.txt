title: Social Hierarchy of Ming China: From Emperor to the Enslaved
desc: Ming China's social ladder: emperor, princes, officials, eunuchs, gentry, clerks, soldiers, clergy, farmers, artisans, merchants, mean people and slaves.
intro: Ming China ranked its people by an old scheme and by the state's registers. In Confucian theory there were four occupations, in descending order scholars, farmers, artisans and merchants, and beneath them the "mean people" (jianmin), entertainers and prostitutes among them. Soldiers, monks, eunuchs, servants and slaves fell outside the four. The founder, the Hongwu Emperor, tried to fix everyone in place, in self-sufficient village communities and hereditary soldier households. By the 16th century the scheme had blurred: artisans worked on farms at harvest and farmers sought work in the towns in hard times, the richest merchants were merging with the landholding gentry, and silver from the Americas ran through the economy. Office went to men who passed the examinations, and the emperor ruled through them and through tens of thousands of palace eunuchs, over perhaps 160 to 200 million people.

text: The emperor stood on a social and legal tier above everyone else, embodying a heavenly mandate to judicial and executive authority. Under the Mandate of Heaven the right to rule rested on virtue, not birth: the Ming founder, the Hongwu Emperor, had been a penniless peasant and Buddhist monk. In 1380 he executed his chancellor, abolished the office and took charge of the Six Ministries himself. Later emperors worked through the Grand Secretariat. The Wanli Emperor (r. 1572–1620) began with able advisers, among them the Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng, but grew tired of his quarrelling ministers and stayed behind the walls of the Forbidden City, where officials had to bribe powerful eunuchs to have their messages carried to him.
rise: The throne passed within the founder's house, and the reigning emperor named his heir: the Hongwu Emperor chose his grandson. It could also be won by war, as the Yongle Emperor took it from his nephew in 1402, and lost by capture: when the Oirats seized the Zhengtong Emperor in 1449 his brother took the throne, and he won it back only by a coup in 1457.

text: Descendants of the first Ming emperor were made princes and given military commands, usually nominal, annual stipends and large estates. Their title was "king" (wang), but unlike the princes of the Han and Jin dynasties they held no feudatories: they served no administrative function and took part in military affairs only under the first two emperors. The Yongle Emperor, himself once the Prince of Yan, disarmed his brothers and moved their fiefs away from the militarized northern border. The princes, the consorts of imperial princesses and ennobled relatives staffed the Imperial Clan Court, which kept the imperial genealogy. Some turned to learning: Zhu Zaiyu, a sixth-generation descendant of the Hongxi Emperor, discovered equal temperament in music.
rise: Princely rank came by descent from the founder, and the emperor could take a prince's power away, as Yongle did his brothers'. Only near the end of the dynasty were some princes allowed to sit the examinations and serve as officials.

text: After 1384, when the founder stopped staffing his bureaus by recommendation only, the officials were recruited through the examinations. They were classed in nine grades, each of two degrees, with salaries nominally paid in piculs of rice. The Six Ministries, of Personnel, Revenue, Rites, War, Justice and Public Works, did the government's work, and the Grand Secretaries, drawn from the Hanlin Academy, coordinated them. Every three years senior officials graded their juniors, who were promoted, kept or demoted; the longest tenure in one office was nine years, and censors could impeach an official at any time. A district magistrate could not serve in his home district.
rise: Office came through the examinations: provincial graduates began in low posts, while those who passed the palace examination became jinshi and were assured high positions. There were only two to four thousand jinshi at any time, "on the order of one out of 10,000 adult males", in Ebrey's words.

text: Below the officials stood the gentry, landholding families whose men had earned examination degrees: by the 16th century some 100,000 shengyuan, "government students", the lowest tier of graduates, with the provincial and metropolitan graduates above them. The examinations tested the Confucian classics, above all the Four Books outlined by Zhu Xi, and from 1487 the intricate "eight-legged essay". Since only a select few could become officials, most of the gentry stayed at home as local leaders: they ran charities and private schools, helped settle minor disputes, supervised community projects, kept order, led Confucian ceremonies and helped collect taxes, and the magistrate depended on their advice. Some kept company with well-educated courtesans outside marriage.
rise: In theory anyone could sit the examinations, which were graded without regard to background, but the years of costly tutoring confined success to families of means: 90 per cent of the population was ineligible for lack of education, while the upper 10 per cent had equal chances. Provincial quotas curbed the hold of the landholding gentry of the most prosperous regions.
you: You have passed the first examination and are a shengyuan: you teach in a private school, advise the magistrate on local projects and practise the eight-legged essay for the provincial examination.

Military officers were graded like officials and evaluated every five years, but held less prestige, both because their service was hereditary and because Confucian values set the cultured pursuits (wen) above the profession of violence (wu). In the early Ming men of noble lineage held the higher commands; later, men of humbler origin displaced them. The hereditary army decayed through desertion, and by 1644 the soldiers were unpaid and unfed.

== mobility
The examinations were the great road up, and historians debate whether they widened or narrowed it. They were graded without regard to background and were open in theory to everyone, but success took years of costly tutoring, so 90 per cent of the people were shut out for lack of education, while the upper tenth had equal chances; provincial quotas spread the degrees beyond the richest regions, and cheap printed classics and model answers widened the pool of candidates. For a man without education the army was the quickest way up, and for some the palace: men castrated themselves to serve as eunuchs. Wealth also moved families, as merchants funded schools, bought books on gentry manners and merged with the landholding gentry. Ideas shook the order too: Wang Yangming taught that anyone, whatever his pedigree, could become as wise as Confucius, his disciple Wang Gen, a salt-mine worker, lectured commoners on education, and people began to question whether the scholar should stand above the farmer. Falls were common as well: farmers who fled the tax registers became drifters and migrant workers, and in the 1640s starving peasants who could not pay their taxes formed huge bands of rebels, and in 1644 the rebel army of Li Zicheng, a peasant soldier, took Beijing.

== women
Women could not sit the examinations, so their standing followed their fathers' and husbands'. The censuses undercounted women and girls: in 1502 the Daming Prefecture reported 378,167 males and 226,982 females, and contemporary authors described female infanticide as "rampant". The early Ming sumptuary laws forbade women ornaments of gold, jade, pearl or emerald. In the palace, women first staffed their own bureaus of attendance, ceremonies, apparel, food and handicrafts, but from the 1420s eunuchs took over all but the Bureau of Apparel. Women of educated families read the new vernacular novels, and some mothers and courtesans were as literate and skilled in calligraphy, painting and poetry as men; the thinker Li Zhi taught that women were men's intellectual equals and should be better educated, and died in prison for "dangerous ideas". In the entertainment trades women were classed among the mean people, and the daughters of poor families could be sold under the guise of adoption.
