intro: Roman society under the emperors was ranked by law as well as by wealth. Citizens of means were enrolled in formal orders (ordines): the senatorial and equestrian orders at the top and, in every town, the council of decurions. Each had a property qualification, a mark of dress and its own seats at the games. Below them lived the great mass of free people: farmers, craftsmen, traders and the urban poor. Most of them lived outside Italy and, until 212 CE, were not Roman citizens. Freed slaves became citizens but carried the mark of their past. The enslaved, perhaps one person in eight, had no rights at all. A few thousand families held the honours of an empire of sixty to seventy-five million people.

text: The emperor was in name the "first citizen" (princeps) and in fact the master of the state. He commanded the armies, controlled the richest provinces, chose the governors and generals, and was the final court of appeal. From Augustus onwards he held a bundle of the old republican powers rather than a crown, and no law fixed the succession: emperors adopted heirs, named their sons, or were made by the army. A household of slaves and freedmen ran much of his government, and his portrait was on the coinage. After his death the Senate could declare a respected emperor a god (divus).

text: The senatorial order stood highest. Augustus fixed the Senate at about 600 members and the property qualification at one million sesterces, and senators wore a broad purple stripe on the tunic. They governed most provinces, commanded the legions, held the old magistracies from quaestor to consul, and judged cases; as a body the Senate passed decrees and confirmed each new emperor. Their wealth lay in land, since open trade was thought beneath them. Twenty quaestors were chosen each year, which kept the Senate at its size. Many seats passed from father to son, but senatorial families died out fast, and the emperors filled the gaps with men from Italy and, increasingly, from the provinces.
rise: A rich equestrian entered when the emperor granted him the broad stripe or enrolled him among the former magistrates. A senator who fell below the property qualification, or offended the emperor, lost his seat.
you: You own estates worth more than a million sesterces, wear the broad purple stripe and may govern a province or command a legion, if the emperor trusts you.

text: The equestrians, or knights, formed the second order. They needed property of 400,000 sesterces and wore a narrow purple stripe and a gold ring. Their name recalls the cavalry of early Rome, but under the emperors they were landowners, men of business and, above all, the emperor's own officials. Equestrian prefects governed Egypt and commanded the Praetorian Guard, procurators ran the imperial estates and taxes, and equestrian officers commanded auxiliary units. These posts were graded by salary, from 60,000 to 300,000 sesterces a year. Most equestrians never held office at all: they were the leading men of the towns of Italy and the provinces.
rise: Sons of town councillors, veteran chief centurions and rich provincials entered the order by the emperor's grant. The most successful equestrians, or their sons, could be raised to the Senate.

text: Every Roman town had a council of decurions, drawn from the leading local families and bound by a property qualification that each town set for itself. The decurions chose the local magistrates, ran the markets, temples, water supply and festivals, and answered to the governor for the town's taxes. They were expected to spend their own money on their town, and inscriptions across the empire record the baths, temples and games they paid for. By the 2nd and 3rd centuries the law counted them among the "more honourable" (honestiores), spared the beatings and degrading punishments it reserved for humbler people. From the 3rd century the duty of guaranteeing the taxes turned the office into a burden that many tried to escape.
rise: Wealthy local men entered the council, often after holding a local magistracy. Freedmen were generally barred, but their sons could enter.
you: You sit on your town's council, help gather its taxes, and are expected to pay for its baths, games or temples from your own purse.

text: The army was a professional force of long-serving volunteers. Legionaries had to be Roman citizens; they served for twenty years or more for regular pay, 225 denarii a year under Augustus, and received land or a cash bounty on discharge. The auxiliaries were recruited among provincials who were not citizens: they served twenty-five years and were granted citizenship at discharge, together with their children. Until about 197 CE serving soldiers could not legally marry. Soldiers enjoyed legal privileges, such as making a valid will without the usual formalities, and the centurions, the career officers, could rise to equestrian rank.
rise: Enlisting gave a poor citizen a steady wage and a provincial a path to citizenship. A capable soldier could become a centurion and, as chief centurion, an equestrian.

text: Most of the empire's free people were small farmers, tenants, labourers, craftsmen and shopkeepers. In the city of Rome some 200,000 citizens received a free grain ration; elsewhere most people lived close to subsistence, and many worked land owned by others, as tenants (coloni) or labourers on the great estates. Until 212 CE most provincials were free non-citizens (peregrini) living under their own local law; that year the Constitutio Antoniniana of the emperor Caracalla made almost all free men citizens. By then the law divided the free into the "more honourable" and the "humbler" (humiliores), who could be flogged, sent to the mines or thrown to the beasts for crimes for which the honourable were exiled.
rise: Wealth from trade, a soldier's career or a patron's favour could lift a family onto a town council within a generation or two. Debt and bad harvests pushed free farmers into tenancy.

text: A slave freed by a Roman citizen became a citizen, something Greek observers found remarkable, but not an equal one. Freedmen could not hold magistracies, sit on town councils or serve in the legions, and they owed their former owner, now their patron, respect, services and a share of their estate. Many carried on in the same trade or household. Some grew very rich: the emperor Claudius's freedmen secretaries Narcissus and Pallas ran the great offices of state, and wealthy freedmen across Italy formed the priesthoods of the Augustales, which honoured the emperor. Their children were born free and carried none of these limits.
rise: The children of freedmen were freeborn citizens and could enter the town councils, and their grandsons sometimes reached equestrian rank.

text: Enslaved people were property. They could be bought, sold, beaten and separated from their families, and they could not legally marry. They came from war captives, the children of enslaved mothers, the trade across the frontiers and abandoned infants. Their lives varied greatly. Gangs on the great estates of Italy and Sicily, and the slaves and convicts in the mines, worked in brutal conditions; household slaves cooked, cleaned, nursed and served; skilled slaves worked as craftsmen, doctors, teachers, secretaries and managers, often allowed to keep savings (the peculium) with which they might buy their freedom. == mobility
Roman society was steep but not closed. The orders were defined by wealth as well as birth, so a fortune made in trade, the army or the emperor's service could carry a family up the ladder over two or three generations: from slave to freedman, from a freedman's son to the town council, from a councillor's son to the equestrian order, and from there to the Senate. The emperor controlled the top of the ladder, granting citizenship, equestrian rank and senatorial status, and he renewed the Senate with men from Italy and the provinces: the emperor Trajan himself came from a family settled at Italica in Spain. Falls were just as real. Senators who lost their fortune or the emperor's favour lost their rank, and free farmers sunk in debt became tenants, whom the laws of the 4th century tied to the land.

== women
Women took the rank of their fathers and husbands: the wives and daughters of senators shared their title. Women could not vote or hold office, and the law placed most of them under a guardian for important business, though Augustus freed freeborn women with three children, and freedwomen with four, from guardianship. Many women owned land and businesses, freed slaves and paid for public buildings: at Pompeii the priestess Eumachia built one of the largest buildings on the forum. Enslaved women had no protection from their owners, and their children were born enslaved.
