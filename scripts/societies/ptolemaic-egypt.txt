desc: Ptolemaic Egypt's social ladder in the 3rd century BCE: the king, Greek officials, priests, Greek settlers, Egyptian soldiers, peasants and the enslaved.
intro: Ptolemaic Egypt was ranked by class and by language. Its kings were Macedonian Greeks who took the Greek title of basileus and the Egyptian title of pharaoh, and a Greek ruling class, which replaced the old native aristocracy, held virtually all political and economic power. Greeks lived under Greek law, were tried in Greek courts and received a Greek education, and their soldiers were given land in return for service. Egyptian priests kept their temples, their prestige and the king's patronage, since the dynasty relied on Egyptian religion to legitimise its rule. Below them lived the peasant farmers, the vast majority, on land owned by the crown, the temples or noble families, and a complex bureaucracy extracted the country's wealth for the king. Egyptians who learned Greek could rise, and within a century intermarriage had produced a large Greco-Egyptian educated class, but wealth and power stayed overwhelmingly in Greek hands.

To the Egyptians he was presented as pharaoh, shown on monuments in Egyptian style and dress, building temples and endowing the priesthood. In theory, if not always in practice, he commanded the whole economy through layers of officials, and the crown controlled the making and sale of papyrus, olive oil, linen and beer. From Ptolemy II, who married his sister Arsinoe II, the kings followed the Egyptian custom of marrying their sisters, and Arsinoe was worshipped as a goddess in sanctuaries of her own.
rise: The crown passed within the dynasty, usually to the king's son, often first as co-ruler: Ptolemy I shared his rule with Ptolemy II in 285 BCE. Under Ptolemy IV royal favourites controlled the government, and in 205 BCE a priest, Horwennefer, proclaimed himself pharaoh in Upper Egypt.

text: Around the king stood a Greek court and a complex bureaucracy built to extract Egypt's wealth for the crown. At court a chief finance minister, a chief accountant and a few other ministers ran the government. Each nome, or province, was governed by a strategos, with an oikonomos for its economic affairs, and each village had its chief administrator, the komarches. Macedonians and other Greeks held virtually all the higher posts and formed the new upper class. The court also kept scholars and poets: Ptolemy II paid stipends to Callimachus and Theocritus, the mathematician Euclid worked under his patronage, and the chief librarian of the Library of Alexandria was also the crown prince's tutor.
rise: Office came by the king's appointment, and Greeks held nearly all of it; over time Egyptians who spoke Greek could rise further. Favour could also be lost: under Ptolemy IV a circle of royal favourites took control of the government.

text: Priests and other religious officials remained overwhelmingly Egyptian and kept much of their old privilege and prestige, because the Ptolemies relied on Egyptian religion to legitimise their rule. The first three kings built and restored temples on a large scale: Ptolemy III began the Temple of Horus at Edfu in 237 BCE. The kings called the priests of all Egypt to synods, at Canopus in 238 BCE and at Memphis in 217 BCE; the decree of 217 BCE, in hieroglyphs, Demotic and Greek, presented the king as Horus and set up his statue in every temple. The High Priests of Ptah at Memphis had great influence, even with the kings, yet the crown taxed temple property heavily.
rise: Priestly office stayed in Egyptian hands. A priest could serve the new kings, as Manetho did when he was commissioned to write a history of Egypt, or lead a revolt against them, as Horwennefer did in 205 BCE.

text: The machimoi were lower-ranking infantry, usually of Egyptian origin. Native troops served in the army from its founding; they were well regarded as fighters, but were sometimes looked down upon and distrusted for a reputation for disloyalty and for aiding local revolts. At least from about 230 BCE they too received land grants: lots of at least five arouras, considered enough for one family. They were sometimes made guards for officials, or helped to enforce the collection of taxes, and their standing was higher than that of the average Egyptian. At the Battle of Raphia in 217 BCE Egyptian soldiers fought beside the Greeks, and after it the ranks they reached and the lands they were given grew.

Jewish tradition held that the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Jewish scriptures, was made for Ptolemy II. text: Slavery in Ptolemaic Egypt was supplied by war, purchase, debt and, probably more rarely, birth. The Zenon papyri show slaves imported chiefly from Syria and Israel, through the slave markets of coastal cities such as Tyre and Gaza; the capture and sale of free Syrians was so widespread that Ptolemy II issued a decree about it. In the 3rd century BCE a woman sold for about 50 to 300 drachmas and a man for about 112 to 150, more in great markets such as Alexandria. The state taxed slave imports, sales and manumissions. Sale documents described each slave carefully for identification, slaves could be handed over to pay debts, and runaways were pursued, often with rewards offered for their capture.
rise: Freedom came by manumission, on which the state took a tax. Running away was the other path, and the Ptolemaic period is the first from which documents of escape from slavery survive in any number.

== mobility
The ladder of Ptolemaic Egypt ran along the line between Greek and Egyptian, and language was the main way across it. Greeks held virtually all political and economic power and Egyptians generally only the lower posts, but over time Egyptians who spoke Greek were able to advance further, and many people identified as Greeks were of Egyptian descent. Within a century of the conquest intermarriage had produced a large Greco-Egyptian educated class, and the cleruchs' descendants married into the Egyptian population and took up Egyptian beliefs. The army was the other ladder: native soldiers received land from about 230 BCE, and after Raphia in 217 BCE their ranks and grants grew. For most Egyptians there was little to share in the kingdom's wealth, and uprisings were frequent. In 205 BCE the priest Horwennefer proclaimed himself pharaoh, and he and his son Ankhwennefer held Thebes and much of Upper Egypt until 186 BCE, the greatest challenge the dynasty ever faced.

== women
The articles say most about the women of the royal house. Queens often ruled beside their husbands, who from Ptolemy II onwards were often their brothers. Arsinoe II was deified, with her own sanctuaries and festivals, and shown both in Greek forms, as Aphrodite or Hera, and as an Egyptian goddess; Berenice II was queen to Ptolemy III. Greek soldiers were allowed to settle in Egypt with their families, and their descendants, the epigonoi, intermarried with Egyptians, so that the mixed Greco-Egyptian society of the Faiyum was made by marriage. Among the enslaved, women were sold for a wider range of prices than men, from about 50 to 300 drachmas in the 3rd century BCE. Of the legal rights of ordinary Greek and Egyptian women under the Ptolemies these articles say nothing.
