title: Social Hierarchy of Majapahit: From God-King to Palace Servant
desc: Majapahit's social ladder in 14th-century Java: the god-king, Bhre princes, ministers, priests, soldiers, foreign merchants, artisans, farmers and servants.
intro: Majapahit, the Hindu-Buddhist kingdom founded in east Java in 1293, ranked people from a king held to be a living god down to the peasants of its villages. Under the king stood his close relatives, the Bhre, who ruled its provinces, a board of ministers headed by the patih, the prime minister, and the officials of the two faiths, Shaivism and Buddhism; the warrior and noble castes led the army's levies, and religious learning passed through the households of kshatriya nobles and brahmana priests. Most people grew rice in the fertile northeast lowlands of Java, but inscriptions name a growing number of crafts and trades, and merchants from China, India and the Muslim lands settled in the ports. Under Queen Tribhuwana (r. 1328–1350) and her son Hayam Wuruk (r. 1350–1389), with the prime minister Gajah Mada, the kingdom claimed tribute from 98 lands from Sumatra to New Guinea, though its direct rule did not extend beyond east Java and Bali. Much of what is known comes from the court poem Nagarakretagama (1365), from inscriptions, and from foreign visitors: Odoric of Pordenone, Ibn Battuta and, in 1413, Ma Huan.

text: The king was the paramount ruler: as chakravartin he was considered the universal ruler and believed to be a living god on earth, regarded as the incarnation of Shiva, Vishnu and the Buddha at once, and held the highest political authority. Poets composed kakawin to worship the king of the gods whose incarnation the king represented, and Prapanca's Nagarakretagama praises Hayam Wuruk. Every year, on the first day of Caitra, representatives of all the lands paying tax or tribute came to the capital to pay court. Kings were deified after death: the first king, Kertarajasa, was portrayed as Harihara in his mortuary temple. Ma Huan saw a king crowned with gold leaves, bare-chested, a dagger in his belt.
rise: The throne passed within the house of Raden Wijaya, and a woman could hold it: when Jayanegara was murdered in 1328, childless, the Queen Grandmother Gayatri Rajapatni, who had become a Buddhist nun, set her daughter Tribhuwana on it. After Hayam Wuruk died in 1389, his son-in-law and his son by a concubine fought over it in the Regreg War of 1405–1406.

text: The title Bhre, "duke or duchess", marked the highest position below the monarch. The provinces of east and central Java were ruled by provincial kings, the Paduka Bhattara, usually the king's close relatives: under Hayam Wuruk there were twelve. They administered their provinces, collected taxes, sent yearly tribute to the capital and managed the defence of their borders. Seven influential elders, mostly close kin of the king, the Bhattara Saptaprabhu, formed a council that advised the king and sat in judgement on important cases: it suspended Gajah Mada over the disaster at Bubat. The highest offices of the court, the Rakryan Mahamantri Katrini, were usually reserved for the king's heir.
rise: Royal birth or marriage brought the rank, and its members fought for more. Bhre Wirabhumi, Hayam Wuruk's son by a concubine, claimed the throne against Vikramavardhana and was caught and beheaded in the Regreg War; the council then sentenced to death the man who had beheaded him.

text: Royal edicts passed from the king to the high officials and on to their subordinates. A board of ministers, the Rakryan Mantri ri Pakira-kiran, ran the daily administration; its head, the Rakryan Mapatih or Patih Hamangkubhumi, was something like a prime minister and decided war and peace with the king. Below the provinces, regencies were run by a wiyasa and districts by an akuwu. Nobles lived in compounds outside the palace, Gajah Mada's separated from it by open fields. Tomé Pires, in 1513, found the lords of Java "revered like gods": great hunters and horsemen, their krises and lances inlaid with gold, keeping many concubines. In war the warrior and noble castes led the levies.
rise: Service could raise a man far. Gajah Mada, who saved Jayanegara with his Bhayangkara guard during the Kuti rebellion of 1319, was promoted to high office and in 1336 became mahapatih; Adityawarman, Jayanegara's cousin, rose to be a senior minister. Office could also end in death: Halayudha, who had plotted against his rivals at court, was sentenced to death, and the minister Demung Sora was sentenced to death for killing Mahisa Anabrang.
you: You sit on the king's board of ministers, live in quarters outside the palace, and ride out to hunt with a gold-inlaid kris; but a minister who breaks the law may pay with his life.
kid: Ministers helped the king run Majapahit, and the greatest of them, Gajah Mada, swore to unite the islands.

text: Religion had officials of its own. The Dharmmadhyaksa oversaw the laws of the state and of religion: above all stood the Dharmmadhyaksa ring Kasewan, the highest Shaivite priest, and the Dharmmadhyaksa ring Kasogatan, the highest Buddhist one, each the authority on the religious law of his faith, while the Dharmma-upapatti dealt with religious affairs. Shaivism, Vaishnavism and Buddhism were all practised, and the state religion was possibly the Siwa Buddha, a joining of Shaivism and Buddhism. Shiva priests and Buddhists had quarters outside the palace walls. Priests led the court's rites, such as the seven-day sraddha for the soul of the Queen Grandmother, and the Buddhist cleric Prapanca wrote the Nagarakretagama.
rise: Learning passed through the households of brahmana families and the Kadewaguruan, schools set in quiet forests or on hills and mountains far from habitation, each led by a mahāresi, a high priest also called dewaguru. Royalty too could enter religious life, as Gayatri Rajapatni did when she became a Buddhist nun.
you: You study under a mahāresi in a Kadewaguruan high in the hills, far from the towns, learning the rites you may one day perform at court.
kid: Priests of Shiva and of the Buddha led the kingdom's ceremonies, and students learned from teachers in schools high in the hills.

text: The army had two kinds of men: prajurit, professional soldiers, and levies taken from the peasants. Majapahit kept some 30,000 full-time troops, soldiers and commanders paid in gold, a standing army only a handful of Southeast Asian empires could hope to maintain. The regular infantry wore scale armour, the richer soldiers of higher rank an armour called kawaca, and war elephants carried nobles and soldiers of higher rank. The Bhayangkara, an elite guard, protected the king and the nobility. The navy, the wwang jaladhi, was a corps of its own with better facilities, sailing the great jong; under Gajah Mada the fleet used gunpowder weapons learned from the Yuan. Troops from subordinate lands fought beside the Javanese.
rise: A soldier could rise far: Gajah Mada led the Bhayangkara guard that saved Jayanegara in 1319 and became prime minister. Many died far from home in the kingdom's wars: in southern Borneo the dead were burned at Tambak Wasi in 1358, and surviving soldiers, sailors and metalsmiths settled in Amuntai, Alabio and Nagara.

text: The fame of the king, the Nagarakretagama says, drew foreign merchants from far and wide: Indians, Khmers, Siamese and Chinese among them. Muslim traders lived in the capital itself by the mid-14th century, for two Muslim tombstones near the palace date from 1368 and 1376, and there were Muslim courtiers. Later Ma Huan found large numbers of Chinese and Muslim merchants, from Arabia, India and above all Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula, settled in Tuban, Gresik and Hujung Galuh. A special tax was levied on some foreigners, possibly those who stayed on in Java and ran some business other than foreign trade. The spices of Maluku passed through the north coast ports, a chief source of the kingdom's income.
rise: Foreigners rose by trade and by the king's favour: Ma Huan counted seven or eight leaders among the foreign families of the capital who served the king. In the 15th century King Kertabhumi gave Muslim merchants trading rights on the north coast in return for their loyalty, and the coastal power of Demak that grew from them overthrew the kingdom in 1527.

Around 1300 the old gold and silver coins gave way to imported Chinese copper coins, small enough for everyday buying and selling; taxes and fines were paid in cash, and savers kept coins in clay money boxes, often shaped like a wild boar. Potters made terracotta figurines, jars, roof tiles and ornaments, builders worked in red brick, and smiths forged steel daggers with handles of gold, rhinoceros horn or ivory.
rise: Court artisans worked for the royal house, preparing the great ceremonies of the court. Others were carried far by the kingdom's wars, as the metalsmiths who settled in Borneo after the campaign of 1358; and when Majapahit fell, many artisans, with courtiers and priests, fled east to Bali.

The northeast lowlands of Java suited rice, and in Majapahit's prime many irrigation projects were undertaken, some with the government's help; Ma Huan wrote that rice was harvested twice a year, with white sesame and lentils but no wheat, and a Chinese writer of about 1339 called Java's fields rich and well watered, its grain twice as plentiful as elsewhere. == mobility
Birth set most ranks, but service to the king could lift a man a long way. Gajah Mada, a guard of the palace, became prime minister and made his Palapa oath to unite the archipelago; Adityawarman, raised in the palace, rose to be a senior minister and went on to found his own dynasty in the Minangkabau highlands. Conquest made lords of commanders: after Bali fell in 1343 its government was shared among the brothers of the general Arya Damar, and Arya Kenceng became the forebear of the royal houses of Tabanan and Badung. The same court was dangerous: Raden Wijaya's most trusted men and former companions in arms, Ranggalawe, Sora and Nambi among them, rose against him and his son, and ministers and princes were put to death. Merchants rose with the ports: Muslim traders, present at court in the 14th century, dominated the north coast through Demak by the end of the 15th. When Majapahit fell to Demak in 1527, many courtiers, artisans, priests and members of the royal family fled east to Bali.

== women
Two queens reigned in their own right, Tribhuwana (1328–1350) and Suhita (1429–1447), the title Bhre was held by duchesses as well as dukes, and the Queen Grandmother Gayatri Rajapatni, a Buddhist nun, was revered as the "protector of the world" and deified after her death. Ibn Battuta wrote that the women of Java rode horses, understood archery and fought like men. Royal marriages served alliance, and could end in bloodshed: when Hayam Wuruk sought the Sundanese princess Dyah Pitaloka as a consort in 1357, Gajah Mada turned the wedding into a demand for Sunda's submission, and almost the whole Sundanese party was killed at Bubat. Tradition remembers Jayanegara for abusing women, the wives of his own officials among them, and the council of royal elders spoke strongly against his wish to marry his half-sisters. In marriage, Ma Huan wrote, the groom went to the bride's family home and three days later brought her back to his own with drums, gongs and fireworks. Among the upper class, widows, concubines and female servants performed sati in the cremation fire.
