title: Social Hierarchy of Ancient Sparta: From Kings to Helots
desc: Sparta's social ladder: two kings, the Spartiate citizens, inferiors, foster brothers, the free perioikoi, freed helots and the helots who farmed the land.
intro: Classical Sparta was ruled by a small body of full citizens who lived from the labour of a far larger subject population. Rank rested on birth and law. A man of Spartiate birth who passed through the state upbringing (the agoge) and paid his dues to a common mess became one of the homoioi, the "equals"; poverty or cowardice could cost him that place. Two kings stood at the head of the state. Around the citizens lived the free perioikoi in their own towns and several in-between groups: inferiors who had lost citizenship, foster brothers raised beside Spartiate boys, and freed helots. At the bottom the helots, bound to the land of Laconia and Messenia, outnumbered the citizens many times over. The ladder was steep and almost closed: the citizen body shrank from about 8,000 men in 480 BCE to fewer than a thousand by Aristotle's day.

text: Sparta had two kings at once, one from each of two hereditary houses, the Agiads and the Eurypontids, both claiming descent from Heracles. The kings commanded the army in war, a kingship Aristotle called "a kind of unlimited and perpetual generalship", and were the state's chief priests, offering its sacrifices and keeping its links with the oracle at Delphi. Both sat in the council of elders, the Gerousia. At home their power was limited. By Herodotus' time their judicial role covered only heiresses, adoptions and the public roads, and the five ephors, elected every year, watched over them; two ephors went with a king on campaign. A king could be put on trial before the elders, the ephors and the other king, as King Pausanias was in 403 BCE. Kings were honoured as heroes after death, and the heirs to the two thrones were the only Spartan boys exempt from the agoge.
rise: The throne passed by birth within the two royal houses, but a king could be deposed: in 491 BCE Demaratus lost his throne after his fellow king Cleomenes I bribed the oracle at Delphi to declare him illegitimate, and he fled to Persia.
you: You command Sparta's army and offer the city's sacrifices, but five ephors elected each year watch over you and can join in putting you on trial.

text: The Spartiates, or homoioi ("equals"), were the full citizens. A Spartiate was born to Spartiate parents, passed through the agoge, the state upbringing that took boys from the age of seven, and was elected to a common mess (syssition) of about fifteen men, to which he owed a fixed contribution of food. Each held an allotment of land (kleros) worked by helots. The law barred Spartiates from trade and crafts so that they could be full-time hoplites, liable to serve until sixty, and a man could not live with his family until he was thirty. Together they formed the assembly, which elected the five ephors each year and, by shouting, the 28 elders of the Gerousia, men over sixty who served for life. Their equality was an ideal: wealth was unequal from early on. Their numbers fell steeply: Herodotus reckons about 8,000 men in 480 BCE; at Leuctra in 371 BCE about 400 of the 700 Spartiates present were killed; and Aristotle put them at fewer than a thousand.
rise: Birth, the agoge and election to a mess made a Spartiate. A man who could no longer pay his mess contributions, or who was judged a coward in battle, lost his full rights and fell among the inferiors.
you: You have passed through the agoge, eat with about fifteen comrades in your common mess and live from land that helots farm for you; the law forbids you to trade or practise a craft.
kid: Spartiates were the citizens of Sparta, men who trained from boyhood to be soldiers.

text: The inferiors (hypomeiones) were men of Spartiate descent who had lost, or never gained, full citizenship. Most had grown too poor: a citizen who could no longer pay his share to his mess lost his place among the equals, and a young man who was not elected to a mess never gained it. Cowardice in battle could also cost a man his rights. Inferiors remained free, but they had no vote in the assembly and could hold no office. The name is known from Xenophon's account of the conspiracy of 399 BCE led by Cinadon, a young man "not one of the equals". According to the informer who betrayed him, Cinadon had pointed out that only about forty of some 4,000 people in the agora were Spartiates, and counted on the hatred of helots, freed helots, inferiors and perioikoi. He was arrested and the plot crushed; asked why he had plotted, he said he wished to be inferior to no one in Lacedaemon.
rise: Men fell into this rank through poverty, failure to win a place in a mess or cowardice in battle; with no regular way back, their number grew as land passed into fewer hands.
you: You were born to Spartiate parents, but you can no longer pay your share to a common mess, so you have lost your vote and your place among the equals.

text: The mothakes, "foster brothers", were boys of free birth but not of full Spartiate standing who were brought up in the agoge beside Spartiate boys, their costs usually paid by wealthy families. They are thought to have been sons of Spartiate fathers and helot mothers or sons of impoverished Spartiates, though the evidence is thin and debated. Unable to pay their own way into a common mess, they were not among the equals, but they fought in the army alongside the perioikoi, and according to Aelian some became full citizens. A few rose to the highest commands. Ancient writers name as mothakes Lysander, who destroyed the Athenian fleet at the Battle of Aegospotami in 405 BCE and blockaded Athens into surrender, Gylippus, who organised the defence of Syracuse and destroyed the Athenian expedition in 413 BCE, and Callicratidas. The term appears above all for the time of the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE).
rise: A wealthy family's sponsorship brought a boy into the agoge beside its own sons; some mothakes later became citizens, while others remained soldiers without a vote.
you: You grew up in the agoge as the foster brother of Spartiate boys, your way paid by a wealthy family; you fight for Sparta and might rise to high command, but you are not one of the equals.

text: The perioikoi, "those who dwell around", were free Lacedaemonians who lived in their own towns, at least 33 of them in Laconia and Messenia. Their towns ran their own local affairs but had to follow Sparta's foreign policy and send men to its army, and the perioikoi had no voice in the Spartan state. Because the law barred Spartiates from trade and crafts, the perioikoi were Sparta's craftsmen and traders: they made the army's weapons and armour, probably also the masks and figurines dedicated at the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia, and unlike the Spartiates they could travel abroad freely. They owned land, and like the Spartiates they held helots. They fought as hoplites, 5,000 of them at Plataea according to Herodotus, and as the Spartiates dwindled they filled a growing share of the phalanx. Their loyalty mostly held: of their towns only Thouria and Aithaia are known to have joined the great helot revolt of 464 BCE.
rise: Perioikoi were born to their status. Freed helots could be raised to a standing like theirs, but the agoge, and with it Spartan citizenship, was closed to their sons except by rare special permission.
you: You live in your own town, farm your land or work as a craftsman or trader, and march with Sparta's army when called, but you have no voice in how Sparta is ruled.
kid: The perioikoi were free people who lived in their own towns and made the weapons for Sparta's army.

text: From the late 5th century Sparta freed helots in return for service as hoplites. The 700 helots who marched with Brasidas to the Chalcidice in 424 BCE were later freed and known as the Brasidians, and freed helots in general were called neodamodeis, a name taken to mean something like "new members of the people". They are attested from about 420 BCE and served in Sparta's army, often far from home: 2,000 of them went with Agesilaus II to Asia Minor in 396 BCE. They never became citizens; their standing seems to have been close to that of the perioikoi. Freedom was also promised for single services: in 425 BCE helots who smuggled food to the Spartiates trapped on the island of Sphacteria were offered their liberty. The historian Moses Finley saw in the arming of helots a grave flaw in the Spartan system, since a hoplite's place in the battle line was a mark of the citizen.
rise: Freedom came only by decision of the state, chiefly as the reward for service as a hoplite; it made a helot free, but never a citizen.
you: You were born a helot and won your freedom by serving as a hoplite; you may be sent to fight far from home, and you will never be a citizen.

text: The helots were the subject people of Laconia and Messenia, descended from Greeks conquered by Sparta, above all the Messenians. They were bound to the land and belonged to the Spartan state rather than to individual citizens. They worked the Spartiates' allotments and handed over part of the harvest, a fixed amount according to Plutarch and half in the words of the poet Tyrtaeus, keeping the rest; they lived in families, kept some property and practised their own cults, and some served as household servants, nurses or attendants on campaign. At Plataea in 479 BCE there were, says Herodotus, seven helots for every Spartiate. Spartan rule rested on fear. Each year the ephors declared war on the helots, so that killing one brought no religious pollution, and in the Krypteia, according to Plutarch, young Spartiates were sent into the countryside to kill helots. Thucydides tells how about 2,000 helots promised their freedom were made to disappear. The helots answered with revolt, above all the great rising of 464 BCE.
rise: Helots were born into their condition. Freedom came only from the state, chiefly as a reward for service as soldiers, until the Theban invasion that followed Leuctra freed the helots of Messenia in 370–369 BCE.

== mobility
Sparta's ladder was nearly closed at the top. Citizenship came only through Spartiate birth, the agoge and a place in a mess, and the law made no regular provision for admitting outsiders; the few foreigners trained in the agoge, such as the two sons of the Athenian Xenophon, came by special favour. Movement was mostly downward. Spartiates who could not pay their mess dues or who showed cowardice lost their full rights, and as land passed into fewer hands the citizen body shrank. A narrow path led up from below: mothakes raised in the agoge could become citizens, and one of them, Lysander, won the Peloponnesian War for Sparta, while helots could be freed for service as soldiers, though never made citizens. The Spartiates feared pressure from below and met it with force, as when the ephors crushed the conspiracy of Cinadon in 399 BCE. After Leuctra the shrunken citizen body could no longer hold Messenia, and Sparta never recovered its former power.

== women
Spartiate women had a freedom that startled other Greeks. Girls were fed as well as boys, exercised and ran races in public and were taught music, dance and poetry; they married at eighteen to twenty, to men near their own age, not at fourteen like many Athenian girls. With husbands living in barracks until thirty and often at war, wives ran the household and estate, while helot and slave women did the weaving and housework. Women could own and inherit land, and an heiress, unlike at Athens, did not have to divorce her husband to marry a kinsman. Aristotle complained that women owned nearly two-fifths of the land and blamed Sparta's decline partly on their wealth. Gorgo, daughter of Cleomenes I and wife of Leonidas I, was remembered for her advice, and Cynisca, sister of Agesilaus II, became the first woman to win an Olympic chariot race, in 396 BCE. Of perioikic and helot women the sources say almost nothing.
