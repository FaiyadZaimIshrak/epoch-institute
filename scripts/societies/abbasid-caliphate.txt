title: Social Hierarchy of the Abbasid Caliphate: From Caliph to Enslaved
desc: The Abbasid Caliphate's social ladder: caliph, Hashimites, viziers and secretaries, soldiers, ulama, merchants, townspeople, dhimmis, peasants and slaves.
intro: Abbasid society ranked people by religion and legal status first, then by descent, office and wealth. Islamic law divided the free into Muslims and protected non-Muslims (dhimmis), who paid a poll tax, and set both above the enslaved. Among Muslims, descent from the Prophet's clan, the Banu Hashim, gave the highest honour, while the old privilege of Arab birth faded as Persian and other converts (mawali) rose into the elite. Power lay with the caliph's servants: the viziers and secretaries who ran the treasury and, from the 830s, Turkish soldiers, most of them bought as slaves, who came to make and unmake caliphs. The scholars of religious law (ulama) won an authority of their own. Below them lived merchants, craftsmen and the peasants whose land tax paid for it all, while slaves brought from beyond the frontiers filled households, harems and armies. Most caliphs were sons of enslaved concubines.

text: The caliph was the successor of the Prophet as head of the Muslim community, its "commander of the faithful", and the Abbasids claimed the office through descent from the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas. He appointed governors, judges and the vizier, commanded the army and led prayers at the great feasts, and his name stood on the coinage and in the Friday sermon (khutba). His viziers ran the government while he lived amid elaborate ceremony in the palaces of Baghdad, founded by al-Mansur in 762, and from 836 to 892 in Samarra. Harun al-Rashid (r. 786–809) ruled at the height of the dynasty's wealth; al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833) tried to impose his own theology on the religious scholars and failed. From 861 Turkish soldiers murdered or deposed four caliphs in nine years, and from 936 a military commander, the amir al-umara, held real power, until the Buyids took Baghdad in 945 and left the caliph a figurehead.
rise: Only an Abbasid could be caliph, but no rule fixed the succession: caliphs named heirs among their sons and brothers, and the choice was often fought over, as in the civil war of 811–813 between al-Amin and al-Ma'mun. Most caliphs were sons of enslaved concubines.
you: You are the successor of the Prophet and commander of the faithful, named in every Friday sermon, but your viziers run the government and your Turkish soldiers may depose you.
kid: The caliph was the ruler of the Muslim empire and its leader in prayer.

text: Descent from the Banu Hashim, the clan of the Prophet Muhammad, became the highest mark of nobility among Muslims. The Hashimites included the Abbasids themselves, descended from the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas, and the Alids, descendants of his cousin and son-in-law Ali, whom the Shia held to be the rightful leaders of the community. Abbasid princes married within the family, as Harun al-Rashid married his cousin Zubayda, a granddaughter of al-Mansur, and they governed provinces: Harun's succession plan of 802 gave al-Ma'mun Khurasan, and al-Mu'tasim governed Egypt and Syria for his brother. They were also watched as rivals: al-Mu'tazz was imprisoned by his cousin al-Musta'in, and al-Ma'mun's son al-Abbas was left to die of thirst after a plot in 838. The Alids were honoured and feared: al-Mansur crushed the revolt of the Alid al-Nafs al-Zakiyya in 762, while al-Ma'mun named the Alid Ali al-Rida his heir in 817 before abandoning the plan. A prince such as al-Muwaffaq, regent for his brother al-Mu'tamid, could hold the real power of the state.
rise: Hashimite rank came only by birth in the male line. A prince could rise to the caliphate, or end in prison or worse at the hands of a rival.
you: You descend from the Prophet's clan, the noblest birth among Muslims, and may govern a province, but the caliph watches his kinsmen, and a suspected rival can be imprisoned or left to die.
kid: Families descended from the Prophet Muhammad's clan were the most honoured in the empire.

text: The caliph's government was run from offices (diwans) for taxes, the army, correspondence and the post. They were staffed by secretaries (kuttab), skilled in accounts and in the intricate land tax, who saw themselves as the mainstay of the state and passed their knowledge from father to son. At their head stood the vizier, and most viziers were of non-Arab origin. The Barmakids, an Iranian family descended from the Buddhist chiefs of a monastery at Balkh, ran the state for much of Harun al-Rashid's reign, until in 803 he imprisoned them and executed Ja'far. In the early 10th century two secretarial dynasties, the Banu al-Furat and the Banu al-Jarrah, the latter from converted Christian families, fought for the vizierate. Office brought great wealth but no security: a fallen vizier and his clients were fined and tortured to give up their fortunes, a practice called musadara. Ali ibn Isa, vizier from 913, was remembered as the "good vizier" for his honesty.
rise: Secretaries rose by skill, family and patronage; many were converts or their sons, and Christians served as secretaries too. A caliph could raise a secretary to the vizierate and ruin him as quickly.
you: You keep the accounts of the land tax in a government office, as your father did; if your faction wins the vizierate you grow rich, and if it falls you may be fined and tortured for your fortune.
kid: Viziers and secretaries ran the caliph's government, kept its accounts and wrote its letters.

text: The army that won the revolution of 747–750 came from Khurasan, and its men and their sons, the abna al-dawla ("sons of the dynasty"), settled in Baghdad as the regime's privileged soldiers, paid by the state. They fought for al-Amin in the civil war of 811–813 and lost. Al-Mu'tasim (r. 833–842) built a new army around Turks from Central Asia, most of them bought or captured as slaves beyond the frontier, trained from boyhood as mounted archers, paid regular salaries and probably freed; at his accession they numbered perhaps three to four thousand. Their quarrels with the people of Baghdad led him to found Samarra in 836, with separate quarters for each regiment. Turkish commanders such as Ashinas, Itakh and Bugha the Elder became the most powerful men in the state, and when pay failed their troops rioted: in 861–870 they murdered or deposed four caliphs. From the early 10th century soldiers were increasingly paid with iqtas, the right to collect the taxes of a district.
rise: A slave boy bought on the steppe could become a general: Itakh began as a cook, and Mu'nis, a Greek eunuch slave, commanded the whole army from 908 to 933. Commanders who lost a struggle at court were dismissed, imprisoned or killed.
you: You were bought as a boy beyond the frontier, trained as a mounted archer and paid a salary in your regiment's quarter at Samarra, and your commanders make and unmake caliphs.
kid: Many of the caliph's soldiers were Turks from Central Asia who fought on horseback with bows.

text: The ulama were the scholars of the Qur'an, the traditions of the Prophet (hadith) and religious law. They had no ordination or hierarchy: a student sought out renowned teachers and received their licence to teach. In these two centuries they built the schools of law named after Abu Hanifa, Malik, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad ibn Hanbal. The caliphs appointed and dismissed qadis, judges of religious law, in the cities, and Harun al-Rashid made Abu Yusuf, a pupil of Abu Hanifa, the first chief qadi; his Book of the Land Tax (Kitab al-Kharaj) set out the rules of taxation. In 833 al-Ma'mun began the Mihna, an inquisition that required judges and scholars to profess that the Qur'an was created, and punished those who refused. Ahmad ibn Hanbal refused, was imprisoned and flogged, and became a hero to the people of Baghdad. The Mihna was abandoned around 850, and its failure left the ulama, rather than the caliph, as the recognised guardians of religious law.
rise: Learning was open to any Muslim who could find teachers, converts and their sons included; a qadi, though, held office only at the caliph's pleasure.
you: You have studied the Qur'an, the Prophet's traditions and the law with renowned teachers and may sit as a judge, though the caliph can dismiss you or, as in the Mihna, try to dictate your beliefs.
kid: The ulama were Muslim scholars who studied the Qur'an and religious law, and some were judges.

text: Trade carried honour in a faith whose Prophet had been a merchant, and the caliphate joined the trade of the Indian Ocean, the Mediterranean and the Silk Road. Ships from Basra and Siraf sailed to India and China, and a whole section of the market of east Baghdad sold Chinese goods, from silk to porcelain; caravans brought spices, gold and slaves, and Jewish merchants known as the Radhanites linked the Muslim world with Christian Europe. Merchants were Muslims, Christians and Jews alike. Bankers lent to the government, and in the late 9th and early 10th centuries Jewish financiers were important at the caliph's court. Voyages were dangerous, for ships sank and pirates preyed on the sea routes, and it was said that anyone who reached China and came back unharmed was blessed by God. Wealth also drew the powerful: in the troubles of the later 9th century soldiers extorted money from anyone who seemed rich.
rise: Trade was open to any free man with capital, Muslim or not, and a great financier could win the ear of viziers; but a lost ship or an extortion could ruin a house.
you: You ship Chinese porcelain and spices through Basra or lend money to the government, and you may grow very rich, though a shipwreck or a greedy soldier could take it all.
kid: Abbasid merchants sailed as far as China and brought back silk and fine pottery.

text: The free Muslims of the towns, craftsmen, shopkeepers, porters, water-carriers and labourers, filled the markets of Baghdad, Basra, Kufa and the provincial capitals; Baghdad, founded in 762, was probably the largest city in the world until the 930s. Under the Umayyads, non-Arab converts had to attach themselves as clients (mawali) to Arab tribes and were treated as lesser Muslims. Their grievances helped bring the Abbasids to power, and under the new dynasty the distinction faded, as Persians, Turks and others entered the elite and Arabic became the everyday language of Iraq and Syria. The Shu'ubiyya, a literary movement of the early Abbasid period, argued that non-Arabs were the equals of the Arabs, or their betters. Townspeople paid the alms tax (zakat) rather than the poll tax, and they could make themselves heard: crowds in Baghdad rioted against the Turkish soldiers in 836, and took the side of Ahmad ibn Hanbal in the Mihna.
rise: Converts joined this rank freely, since conversion lifted the poll tax, and a townsman could rise by trade, by learning or by service in the army or a government office.

text: Christians, Jews and Zoroastrians were dhimmis, protected peoples. They kept their faith, their places of worship and their own leaders and courts, and Islamic law forbade enslaving them. In return they paid the jizya, a yearly poll tax on adult men graded by wealth; on one modern estimate most paid 12 dirhams. The law also barred them from building new places of worship, bearing arms or riding horses, gave their testimony no weight against a Muslim, and let a Muslim man marry a Christian or Jewish woman but not the reverse. Enforcement depended on the caliph: al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861) ordered dhimmis to wear distinguishing yellow garments and barred them from office, but after his reign such rules lapsed again. Dhimmis prospered in the professions. Eight generations of the Christian Bukhtishu family served the caliphs as physicians, the Christian Hunayn ibn Ishaq translated Galen into Arabic, and Christian secretaries and Jewish bankers served the government.
rise: Conversion to Islam lifted the poll tax and opened every career, and conversion spread steadily: Richard Bulliet estimated that Muslims in Iran grew from about 40 per cent of the people in the mid-9th century to nearly 80 per cent by the end of the 11th.
you: You are a Christian, a Jew or a Zoroastrian: you keep your faith and your community's courts and pay a yearly poll tax, a caliph may order you to wear distinguishing clothes, and you may still serve him as a physician or a secretary.
kid: Christians, Jews and Zoroastrians kept their own religions but paid a special tax.

text: Most people lived by farming, in the irrigated plain of southern Iraq, the Sawad, in the Nile valley, Iran and Syria, Muslims and non-Muslims alike. The land tax (kharaj) on their fields paid for the court and the army; by Abbasid times it was due on most land whatever the owner's faith. From the reign of al-Mahdi (775–785) the Sawad paid with a share of the crop: half on land watered by flooding, a third or a quarter where water had to be raised by wheels. Many cultivators were tenants on estates whose owners lived in the towns, and a modern model suggests that after taxes and rents they kept little more than a third of what they grew. Heavy taxes and the disorders of the later 9th century drove many from the land, and the Sawad's revenue fell from about 100 million dirhams under Harun al-Rashid to 20 million by the early 10th century.
rise: A peasant who converted escaped the poll tax but not the land tax. Many fled to the towns; others revolted, like the Christian Copts of the Nile Delta in 831–832, after which many of their women and children were sold as slaves.
you: You farm a plot in the irrigated plain of Iraq for a landlord who lives in the city, hand over up to half your harvest as land tax, whatever your faith, and may abandon your fields if the tax collectors press too hard.
kid: Most people were farmers who grew wheat, barley and dates and paid heavy taxes on their harvests.

text: Islamic law forbade enslaving Muslims or protected dhimmis, so slaves came from beyond the frontiers: war captives from Byzantium, Turks from the Central Asian steppe, Slavs and other Europeans brought down the Volga, and Africans from Nubia, Ethiopia and the East African coast. Under the Baqt treaty Nubian Makuria sent Egypt between 360 and 400 slaves a year. Many slaves served in households, and freeing a slave was counted a pious act. Enslaved women worked as servants or were bought as concubines; a concubine who bore her master a child he acknowledged became an umm walad, could no longer be sold and went free at his death, and her child was born free. Trained singing slaves (qiyan) were the most expensive of all. Eunuchs guarded the harems and served as stewards and officials. In southern Iraq enslaved Africans, the Zanj, were kept in labour camps to scrape the salty topsoil from the marshes near Basra; in 869 they rose under Ali ibn Muhammad and held out for fourteen years.
rise: Freeing a slave was counted a pious act, and a slave could also buy freedom by contract. Freed slaves became clients (mawali) of their former owners, and many rose high: the mothers of most caliphs had been slaves.
you: You were captured or bought beyond the frontier and now serve in a Baghdad household; your owner may free you as a pious act, and if you are a woman who bears him a child he acknowledges, you cannot be sold and will go free at his death.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay; some were later freed.

== mobility
Abbasid society was steep, but its upper ranks were unusually open to outsiders, because caliphs trusted men who owed everything to them. Abu Muslim, a Persian freedman of the Abbasid family, led the revolution of 747–750. The Barmakids, descended from the Buddhist chiefs of a monastery at Balkh, rose to run the state for Harun al-Rashid before their fall in 803. Slaves rose highest of all. Al-Khayzuran, sold in a slave market near Mecca, became the wife of al-Mahdi and the mother of two caliphs; Turkish slave boys became the generals of Samarra; Mu'nis, a Byzantine eunuch slave, commanded the army from 908 and in 928 briefly deposed his caliph. Ahmad ibn Tulun, a Turkish soldier sent to govern Egypt in 868, made himself its master, and Ya'qub ibn al-Layth, a coppersmith, carved out a kingdom in eastern Iran. Converts entered every career. Falls were sudden: viziers and their followers were fined and tortured, and caliphs themselves were deposed, starved or murdered.

== women
Islamic law let a free woman own property, gave her a dowry from her husband and a share of inheritance, half that of a brother, but in this period women of the upper and middle classes were secluded in the harem as never before: after al-Khayzuran's time the women of the caliph's family vanished from public life. Seclusion did not mean powerlessness. Al-Khayzuran, a former slave, received officials and petitioners and grew rich in trade while her husband al-Mahdi and her son Harun ruled; Harun's wife Zubayda paid for wells and reservoirs along the pilgrim road from Kufa to Mecca, the Darb Zubayda; Shaghab, mother of al-Muqtadir, held great influence at court during his reign (908–932). The princess Ulayya bint al-Mahdi was a celebrated poet and singer. Enslaved women had no such protection: concubines and singing slaves (qiyan) performed unveiled before men, though Arib al-Ma'muniyya, a slave singer freed by al-Mu'tasim, became a landowner with a troupe of her own.
