title: Social Hierarchy of Safavid Iran: From Shah to Enslaved
desc: Safavid Iran's social ladder under Abbas I: shah, Qizilbash lords, Persian officials, Caucasian ghulams, Shi'i clergy, merchants, peasants and the enslaved.
intro: Safavid Iran was ranked from the shah at the apex, through an aristocracy of soldiers, officials and clergy, down to the common people, merchants and peasants at the base. Its elite was divided by origin and calling. The Turkoman Qizilbash tribes, the "men of the sword", had brought the dynasty to power in 1501 and governed most of the provinces; Persians, the "men of the pen", staffed the bureaucracy and the religious establishment. From the 1540s the shahs built a third force of Georgians, Circassians and Armenians, captured or deported from the Caucasus: converted to Islam and trained as the shah's own slaves, the ghulams, they took over much of the army and many high offices under Abbas I. Offices were given, in principle, for merit rather than birth, and every rank depended on the shah, who claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad.

text: The shah stood at the apex of the state with total power over it. He bore the old title of Shahanshah, King of Kings, and his legitimacy rested on his descent from Muhammad as a sayyid, a claim many scholars doubt. To the Qizilbash he was also the morshed-e kamel, the "Perfect Guide" of the Safavid Sufi order, so a follower's disobedience was treason: Abbas I put some to death for it in 1614. Jean Chardin thought the shahs ruled with an iron fist. Abbas built an army paid by and loyal to himself, brought provinces under his direct rule and in 1598 moved the capital to Isfahan, where Naqsh-e Jahan Square set the mosque, the bazaar and his own Ali Qapu palace side by side.
rise: Only a prince of the Safavid house could reign. In the 16th century the Qizilbash chiefs had ruled for the ten-year-old Tahmasp I and assassinated Ismail II, and the shahs kept the princes of their own family confined to the harem.

text: The Qizilbash, "red heads" after their twelve-gored crimson caps, were mainly Turkoman tribes of Anatolia and Azerbaijan, sworn followers of the Safavid order, who had won the throne for Ismail I in 1501. The seven great tribes, Rumlu, Shamlu, Ustajlu, Afshar, Qajar, Takkalu and Zulkadar, supplied the army's commanders, and their amirs governed most provinces as khans, "like petty shahs": they spent the revenue in the province, sent the shah the balance and kept troops ready for his call. Abbas I cut their governorships, moved their governors away from their tribes and replaced most with ghulams. Yet the Qizilbash still gave more than half the army's strength, many senior officers and great offices of the royal household.
rise: Rank came from the tribe and its leading clans, though the sons of nobles had to prove themselves worthy of their fathers' posts. A tribesman who forsook his tribe for the shah alone could join the Shahsevans, the "Friends of the King", 12,000 strong under Abbas.
you: You are an amir of the Shamlu, wearing the red cap of the Safavid order; your father governed a province like a petty shah, but now the shah moves governors at will, and ghulams are taking their places.
kid: The Qizilbash were warrior tribes in red caps who had helped the first Safavid shah win his throne.

text: Persians, the "men of the pen", filled the bureaucracy as they had under earlier rulers of Iran, while the Turkic-speaking soldiers were the "men of the sword". Under Abbas they held the two highest offices of state: the grand vizier, the Etemad-e Dowlat, always chosen from among doctors of law, without whose counter-seal no act of the shah was valid, and the comptroller-general of the revenues, the mostoufi-ye mamalek. Every office had a deputy or superintendent who recorded its acts and reported to the shah. Persian was the language of government, letters and the coinage. The rivalry between pen and sword was old: between 1508 and 1524 the Qizilbash brought about the deaths of three of the five Persians whom Ismail I made vakil.
rise: Offices were given, in principle, for merit: sons of officials were considered for their fathers' posts but had to prove themselves, and laymen are recorded rising to high office. From Tahmasp's reign the sons of Turkic officers were educated in Persian in the royal household and began to take posts once kept for Persians.
you: You keep the revenue accounts in Persian, as your father did before you; your post depends on your skill and on the shah's favour, and a superintendent records every decision you make.
kid: Persian officials wrote the shah's letters, kept his accounts and ran his government.

text: The ghulams, "slaves of the shah", were Georgians, Circassians and Armenians brought from the Caucasus as captives and deportees, converted to Islam and trained for his service. Bound by no clan or tribe, they were loyal to him alone. Tahmasp I's Caucasian campaigns of 1540–1554 began the corps; Abbas I enlarged it from a few hundred men to 15,000 cavalry and 3,000 royal bodyguards, part of a division of some 40,000 paid by the shah. Ghulams replaced most Qizilbash governors, and by the end of his reign a fifth of the high-ranking amirs were ghulams: the Georgian Allahverdi Khan became governor-general of Fars in 1595 and commander-in-chief in 1598. The palace eunuchs, "white" from the Caucasus and "black" from India and Africa, were ghulams too.
rise: Men entered as captives or deportees and, after special training, joined the new regiments or the royal household; from there the most favoured rose to govern provinces and command armies.

kid: Ghulams were soldiers from the Caucasus who served only the shah, and some became generals and governors.

text: Ismail I made Twelver Shi'ism the state religion and conversion mandatory for a largely Sunni population; Sunni scholars were killed or exiled, and Twelver scholars were brought in and given land and money in return for loyalty. As imams and judges the ulama applied the sharia, and qazis judged cases in the provinces, ranking under the governors. Mindful of their old role as a buffer between ruler and subjects, the religious officials usually did their best to shield ordinary people from oppressive government. Landowners fearing confiscation gave their lands to the clergy as vaqf, keeping title while the ulama took a share of the revenue, and in time mujtahids and seyyeds came to own many such lands outright.
rise: Rank among the ulama came by learning and the shah's grants; a seyyed's standing came by descent from the Prophet. Gifts of vaqf land turned many clerical families into landowners, a new and significant group in the words of the chronicler Iskandar Munshi.
you: You were trained in Shi'i law, judge your neighbours' disputes as a qazi under the governor, and draw part of the income of lands that their owners gave to the clergy to keep them from confiscation.
kid: Shi'i Muslim scholars served as judges and were seen as protectors of ordinary people.

text: Merchants and craftsmen stood at the base of the hierarchy with the peasants, yet the bazaar was one of the three powers Abbas I gathered around Naqsh-e Jahan Square, beside the mosque and the palace. The trade and artisan guilds, the asnāf, appeared from the 1500s, as did the futuvva brotherhoods run by dervishes, and communities chose a kadkhoda to administer their customary law, under a kalantar whom the shah appointed to protect them from the governors. Abbas revived the Silk Road with roads, bridges, bazaars and caravanserais, which wealthy merchants built too, and sold carpets, silk and textiles to the English and Dutch. Carpet weaving became an industry, centred on Tabriz.
rise: Trade could make a fortune, but property was insecure, so many owners put their land in the clergy's hands as vaqf. The alliance between the bazaar and the ulama became a lasting feature of Iranian society.

text: Pastoralism and agriculture were the twin bases of the economy, and the lower levels of society were divided like the upper: Turkoman tribes bred livestock and lived apart from their neighbours, while Persians farmed settled land. Farm produce paid most of the state's taxes. Before Abbas most land was assigned to officials; he brought more under the crown. The harvest was shared between landlord and farmer in five parts of a fifth each, for land, water, plough animals, seed and labour, so a farmer who brought his labour and animals kept 40 per cent. Chardin judged Iran's farmers better off than those of Europe's most fertile lands; others found them well paid and well dressed, but subject to forced labour and heavy demands.
rise: The shahs moved whole peoples onto the land: under Abbas some 130,000–200,000 Georgians, about 300,000 Armenians and tens of thousands of Circassians were brought into Iran, many of them settled as farmers and cattle breeders. Under Shah Safi, the people put under royal commissioners in place of governors suffered from their rapacity and corruption.
you: You farm with your own oxen and labour and keep two-fifths of the harvest; the rest goes to the landlord who provides the land, water and seed, and you may still be called out for forced labour.
kid: Most people in Safavid Iran were farmers who grew fruit, melons and tobacco, or herders who raised animals.

== mobility
The Safavid state was, in principle, a meritocracy: offices were not inherited as of right, and laymen are recorded rising to high posts. The great movements of the age were made by the shahs. Tahmasp I and Abbas I built a new layer of society out of Caucasian captives and deportees, who rose from captivity to command: by 1598 the Georgian Allahverdi Khan was commander-in-chief of the armed forces. Many hundreds of thousands of other deportees were settled across Iran and took every kind of role, from woodcutters and farmers to traders, generals and governors. The Qizilbash fell as the ghulams rose: their governorships were cut and their governors moved, and after the persecutions of Abbas and Shah Safi many of them turned against the dynasty. Tahmasp's education of Turkoman officers' sons in Persian opened the bureaucracy to them, and gifts of vaqf land made a new class of landowners of the clergy.

== women
Women's place followed their families', and at the top the harem was a political arena. Tajlu Khanum, Ismail I's queen, financed the Golden Iwan of the Fatima Masumeh Shrine at Qom in 1519, built her husband's tomb at Ardabil and supported Tahmasp's accession in 1524. From Tahmasp's reign Circassian and Georgian women, brought as captives, became wives and concubines of the shahs, and the harem became, in the words of one modern account, "a competitive, and sometimes lethal, arena of ethnic politics"; the shahs drew queen mothers from these women. The dynasty itself had married into Turkoman, Georgian, Circassian and Pontic Greek families: Ismail I's grandmother was Despina Khatun, a princess of Trebizond. Chardin described women who at home wore a veil over the hair and back only, but in public a cloak that hid all but the face, their hands and feet dyed with henna.
