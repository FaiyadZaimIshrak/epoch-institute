title: Social Hierarchy of the Mali Empire: From Mansa to Enslaved
desc: The Mali Empire's social ladder: the mansa, noble clans and governors, Muslim scholars, merchants, free farmers, griots and craft groups, and the enslaved.
intro: The Mali Empire, founded by Sundiata Keita around 1235, ranked people above all by birth into a lineage. Most were horon, the freeborn: the royal Keita line, the warrior clans who carried the quiver and led the army, Muslim clerical families, merchants and the farmers who made up the great majority. Beside them stood the nyamakala, hereditary groups of griots, smiths and leatherworkers who married among themselves and were believed to handle a dangerous force, and at the bottom the jon, the enslaved. The mansa ruled a loose federation of provinces and tributary kingdoms, and Islam, strong at court and in the trading towns, lived beside older beliefs in the countryside. What we know comes from Arabic writers, above all al-Umari, Ibn Battuta, who visited in 1352–53, and Ibn Khaldun, and from the oral traditions of the griots, written down centuries later and partly legendary.

text: The mansa was the supreme ruler, heir to Sundiata Keita, who defeated the Sosso king Sumanguru Kanté at the Battle of Kirina around 1235 and was proclaimed mansa over twelve allied Manding kingdoms. He received tribute from provincial governors and vassal kings, appointed governors to conquered lands, heard cases himself in solemn audiences, and kept every gold nugget found in the empire for himself, leaving only gold dust to trade. Ibn Battuta, who visited Mansa Sulayman's court in 1352–53, saw subjects answered by the mansa throw dust over their heads and backs. The mansa had to be both a pious Muslim sultan and the heir of hunter-kings protected by spirits. Mansa Musa made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–25, spending so much gold in Cairo that its value fell, and built mosques and palaces at Timbuktu and Gao. No fixed rule settled the succession: sons, brothers and uncles disputed the throne, and in 1285 a general named Sakura, who may once have been a royal slave, seized it.
rise: The throne passed within Sundiata's lineage, to a son, brother or uncle of the last mansa, as struggles among the royal kin and the great men of the court decided; Sakura alone broke the line, and after his death the Keita returned.
you: You own every gold nugget in your empire, hear your subjects' cases in solemn audience and receive the tribute of kings, but brothers, uncles and generals watch for a chance to take your throne.
kid: Mansa Musa of Mali gave away so much gold on his way to Mecca that gold lost value in Cairo.

text: Below the mansa stood the great lineages of the Manding heartland. Oral tradition holds that the kings of the twelve allied lands who submitted to Sundiata kept their kingdoms as his farba, "great commanders", and that sixteen clans of "quiver-bearers" formed the warrior elite; in the 14th century two of their chiefs commanded the northern and southern armies. Conquered or vital provinces were ruled by a farin, a military governor chosen by the mansa, who kept the garrison, collected taxes and customs and oversaw justice; the post was prestigious and could pass to a son with the mansa's approval. The nobles fought as cavalry, with swords, lances, iron helmets and mail, at the head of an army that contemporary writers put at 100,000 men, 10,000 of them horsemen, raised by quotas from the free clans.
rise: Rank came by birth into a noble lineage, but the mansa could raise a commander to a governorship or replace a farin quickly, and a successful general, like Sakura, could reach the throne itself.
you: You belong to one of the quiver-bearing clans, ride to war in mail with sword and lance, and may govern a province for the mansa, collecting its taxes and keeping its garrison.
kid: Mali's nobles fought on horseback, led the army and governed provinces for the mansa.

text: Islam had reached the Manding by the 10th century, and the mansas, their courts and the trading towns professed it, while most villagers kept the older religion. The Muslim learned, many from clerical families, served as imams, scribes and teachers of the Quran and as qadis, judges of Islamic law, in the towns and at court. Mansa Musa brought scholars back from his pilgrimage, among them Maliki jurists and the Andalusi poet Abu Ishaq al-Sahili; the great mosque of Timbuktu, the Djinguereber, was built under him in 1327, and he sent West African scholars to study at Fez. Timbuktu and Djenné grew into centres of learning, and before Mali lost Timbuktu in 1433 a jurist from the Hejaz found the city full of West African scholars who knew more law than he did. Some clerical towns, such as Dia, were left to govern themselves by Islamic law alone under the Jakhanke, followers of the quietist teaching of al-Hajj Salim Suwari.
rise: Learning ran in clerical families, but the mansas sponsored scholarship, sending students to Fez and welcoming foreign scholars at court and in Timbuktu.
you: You teach the Quran and Islamic law in a trading town and judge disputes among Muslims as a qadi, and the mansa may send you to study in Fez.
kid: In Mali's trading cities, such as Timbuktu, Muslim scholars taught students the Quran and Islamic law.

text: Trade made Mali rich. Mande-speaking Muslim merchants, the Wangara or Dyula, carried gold from the fields of Bambuk and Bure, kola nuts from the forest and cotton cloth, and brought back salt cut by slaves at Taghaza in the desert and copper from Takedda; they met North African traders at Walata and Timbuktu, where the caravans crossing the Sahara arrived. By the early 14th century Mali supplied almost half the gold of the Old World. The mansa taxed every load of gold, copper and salt that crossed his borders, and payments were made in gold dust, salt, copper and cowrie shells. Ibn Battuta praised the security of the roads, on which neither traveller nor resident had anything to fear. Merchants travelled with slaves who carried their goods, and slaves were themselves traded: the caravan Ibn Battuta joined for home in 1353 took 600 enslaved women north. Merchant families also spread Islam along the trade routes, far into the forest zone.
rise: Trade ran in Muslim merchant families who settled along the routes; wealth bought slaves and horses, but rank still came from birth into a lineage.
you: You send your slaves with gold dust and kola nuts to Walata, trade them for desert salt and pay the mansa's tax on every load.
kid: Mali's merchants traded gold for salt, which camels carried in great slabs across the Sahara Desert.

text: Most of Mali's people were free farmers, members of horon lineages living in villages of related households. They grew grain, above all millet, and cotton, working the land with iron tools made by the smiths, and raised sheep, goats and cattle; in the inland delta of the Niger the Bozo, Somono and Sorko lived by fishing, and Fulani herders kept cattle. The heads of the families in each village chose a village head from the line of its founder, and the mansa's authority reached them mainly through tribute and the provincial governor. Free men owed military service: each clan sent a quota of fighting men, who came with their own weapons, bows and poisoned arrows in the south and spears and hide shields in the north. Visitors described a fertile, crowded land, and the Tarikh al-Sudan, written in the 17th century, claimed that the villages around Djenné stood so close that a message could be shouted from one to the next.
rise: Free status came by birth into a horon lineage; capture in war or in a raid could turn free people into slaves.
you: You farm millet and cotton with your kin in a village run by its family heads, send your sons with their own bows when your clan's quota is called, and pay tribute to the mansa's governor.
kid: Most people in Mali were farmers who grew millet and cotton and lived in villages with their relatives.

text: Beside the freeborn stood the nyamakala, hereditary groups who practised special crafts and married only among themselves: the griots (jeli), musicians, praise-singers and keepers of genealogies; the smiths (numu), who made the tools and weapons of an empire rich in iron; and the leatherworkers (garanke). Their name links them to nyama, a vital force, dangerous as well as powerful, believed to flow through their work, so they were both needed and feared. At court the griot was tutor to the princes, master of ceremonies and adviser to the mansa, and in Mande memory Sundiata's own griot, Balla Fasséké, founded the Kouyaté line of griots that still exists. Every noble family had its griots, who could praise or shame the mighty in public. Historians debate how far these groups already formed castes in the 14th century: Tal Tamari dates the rise of castes in West Africa to about the 13th century, Susan McIntosh to about the 10th. Later Mande society ranked the nyamakala below the freeborn.
rise: Membership came by birth and marriage stayed within the group; a griot rose by the favour of a great patron, and the mansa's griot was among the most influential men at court.
you: You were born into a family of griots and sing the genealogies of the noble family you serve, whose name your words can raise or shame.
kid: Griots were musicians and storytellers who remembered the history of Mali's kings and sang it aloud.

text: The enslaved, jon in Mande, were captives taken in war and in raids on neighbouring peoples, the children of enslaved mothers, and people bought in the markets. They worked the fields of the great households, carried merchants' goods, and served at court and in the army, and in the desert at Taghaza the salt was dug by slaves. Some were sold north across the Sahara: the caravan Ibn Battuta joined in 1353 carried 600 enslaved women to Morocco, and later Arabic accounts say Mansa Musa took some 12,000 slaves on his pilgrimage, a figure historians treat with caution; Michael Gomez estimates that Mali may have captured over 6,000 people a year to prepare for it. Ibn Battuta noted that Malians vied with one another in the number of slaves they owned. Slave soldiers called sofa, armed by the state, served in the garrisons under their own commanders, and slaves of the royal household could become powerful. Oral tradition credits Sundiata's charter with a rule against ill-treating slaves.
rise: Freedom could come by an owner's grant, and slaves of the court and the army could win wealth and power, as Sakura, perhaps a freed slave of the royal family, did; later Mande custom set people born into a household apart from those bought or captured.
you: You were captured in a raid and now work your owner's fields or carry his goods to market; sent to the army instead, you would serve the mansa as a soldier in a garrison.
kid: Enslaved people in Mali were owned by others and made to work without pay, and some were sold across the Sahara.

== mobility
Rank in Mali came chiefly by birth: a person belonged to a lineage, free, nyamakala or enslaved, and married within it. Service to the mansa could still carry men far. Sakura, a general who may once have been a slave of the royal family, seized the throne in 1285 and led Mali to new conquests, and the mansa could make or unmake provincial governors at will. War moved people down the ladder: raids and conquests turned free people of neighbouring lands into captives, and the trade across the Sahara carried some of them away. Islam offered another path. Learning and the pilgrimage brought prestige, Mansa Musa sent West Africans to study at Fez, and scholars from Granada and the Hejaz found a place at court and in Timbuktu. After Mansa Sulayman's death around 1360, a string of short reigns and palace coups showed how much power great officials could seize, and an officer called the kankoro-sigui became a power behind the throne.

== women
A woman took her place from her lineage, free, nyamakala or enslaved, and married within it. Royal women could hold real power. Mansa Sulayman ruled with his principal wife and cousin Qasa as co-ruler until, while Ibn Battuta was in Mali in 1352–53, he imprisoned her on a charge of plotting with an exiled prince and raised another wife, Banju, who was not of royal blood; Qasa took refuge with the preacher of the mosque, and her fate is not recorded. Mande tradition honours mothers: Sundiata's name means "Sogolon's lion", after the mother whom the epic makes central to his story. Ibn Battuta, a strict Muslim jurist, was shocked that Malian women, from slave girls to the mansa's daughters, did not cover themselves as he expected. Enslaved women had the least protection: the 600 women in the caravan he joined for home were bound for North Africa as servants and concubines.
