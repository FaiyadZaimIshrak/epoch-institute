intro: From 1250 to 1517 Egypt and Syria were ruled by a military caste of men who had begun life as slaves. Mamluks were bought as boys, mostly Kipchak Turks and later Circassians from the steppes and the Caucasus, converted to Islam, trained in the barracks of the Citadel of Cairo and then freed. As free men they formed the ruling caste: they chose the sultan, commanded the army as amirs and drew the revenues of the land through iqta grants. Their own sons, born free and Muslim, were mostly kept out of it. Below the military elite stood the civilian notables, the ulama and the scribes, then the merchants and craftsmen of the towns and the peasants of the Nile valley and Syria, who were the great majority. Christians and Jews lived as protected but subordinate dhimmis, and slaves served in households across the realm.

text: The sultan ruled Egypt, Syria and the Hejaz from the Citadel of Cairo. Many sultans had begun as mamluks themselves, bought as boys and freed; many others were sons of sultans. A new sultan was chosen by a council of the leading amirs and mamluks, who swore loyalty to him; he then took the title al-malik, rode in procession through Cairo and had his name read in the Friday sermon, and from 1261 an Abbasid caliph kept in Cairo, with no real power, confirmed his authority. The sultan made war, levied taxes, issued decrees, assigned iqtas and appointed the governors of Damascus, Aleppo and the other provinces, and he owned more mamluks than anyone else: his own royal mamluks were his surest support. Some sultans founded dynasties, and Qalawun's descendants held the throne for most of a century, but the senior amirs often set up a sultan's young son as a figurehead, and usurpations and murders were common.
rise: No fixed rule decided the succession: an amir with enough mamluks behind him could seize the throne, as Baybars did in 1260 and Barquq in 1382, and a sultan who lost the amirs' support could be deposed or killed.

text: The amirs were the senior mamluks, the commanders and governors of the realm. Baybars graded them as amirs of ten, of forty and of a hundred, by the number of mamluks each kept, and an amir of a hundred could lead a thousand horsemen in battle. Each held an iqta, the right to collect the revenues of assigned lands, sized to his rank, and with it he paid and equipped his own corps, a private army; when rival amirs used their corps against each other, civilian life was badly disrupted. Unlike a European fief, an iqta was not hereditary, and the state spread an amir's holdings across several provinces and short terms. The great offices of the court, from the master of the household (ustadar) to the secretary (dawadar), and the governorships of Damascus, Aleppo and the other provinces went to amirs. They built mosques, madrasas and tombs in Cairo and endowed them as waqf, partly to provide for their descendants, and they rose and fell with their factions.
rise: A mamluk became an amir by the sultan's favour after years of service and might climb from ten to forty to a hundred; at the top an amir could make himself sultan. A fall from favour could bring imprisonment, the confiscation of his wealth and death.
you: You command your own mamluks as an amir of forty and live on the revenues of lands assigned to you, but your iqta is not yours to pass on, and a turn of faction could cost you everything.
kid: Amirs were the top army commanders, paid with the taxes from lands the sultan assigned to them.

text: The mamluks were the free soldiers of the ruling caste, men who had begun life as slaves. Dealers bought boys from beyond the frontiers of Islam, Kipchak Turks from the steppes at first and, from about 1400, mostly Circassians from the Caucasus, shipped through the Black Sea. In the barracks of the Citadel of Cairo they were converted to Islam, taught Arabic and the religious sciences, and drilled in horsemanship, archery and swordplay; at the end of their training they were freed. They then served either among the royal mamluks, the sultan's own regiments and the most privileged troops, or in the corps of an amir, for pay and in time a share of an iqta. They kept Turkish names and spoke Turkish among themselves, rode horses forbidden to other Muslims, and owed lifelong loyalty to the master who had freed them and to their comrades of the same household. Baybars bought some 4,000, Qalawun 6,000–7,000, and by 1293 there were about 10,000.
rise: A mamluk rose by his master's favour, from trooper to amir and sometimes to sultan; when a new sultan came to power, the mamluks of his predecessors could find themselves pushed aside. His own sons were born free and Muslim, and so were mostly barred from following him into the caste.
you: You were bought as a boy from beyond the Black Sea, raised in the barracks of the Citadel and then freed; now you ride in the sultan's regiments, a free Muslim soldier of the ruling caste.
kid: Mamluks were boys bought as slaves and trained as soldiers, then set free to serve their masters in the army.

text: The sons of mamluks, the awlad al-nas, were the paradox at the heart of the system. Born free and Muslim in Egypt or Syria, they never passed through the slave market, the barracks and the manumission that made a mamluk, and so they were mostly kept out of the ruling caste their fathers had joined. Some served in the halqa, the corps of non-mamluk cavalry under the sultan, which ranked below the mamluk regiments and declined in the 14th century, and a few rose high: Sultan al-Nasir Hasan (r. 1347–1351, 1354–1361) promoted awlad al-nas in the army and government, which so angered his own mamluks that they killed him in 1361. Most turned to civilian careers, in trade, the administration and above all scholarship. The historian Ibn Taghribirdi (1411–1470), son of the amir Taghribirdi, studied under al-Maqrizi and wrote a great chronicle of Egypt and its sultans, al-Nujum al-zahira.
rise: A father's wealth and connections gave them a start, and a sultan's son might be set on the throne as a figurehead, but the rank their fathers held could not be inherited.
you: You are the son of an amir, born free and Muslim, and for that very reason barred from the mamluk regiments; you study with the scholars of Cairo and may make your name in the administration or as a historian.
kid: Sons of mamluks were born free, so most could not join the soldier class; many became scholars instead.

text: The ulama, the scholars of Islamic law and religion, led the civilian population and stood between it and its rulers. Baybars ended the custom of a single Shafi'i chief judge and appointed a chief qadi for each of the four Sunni schools of law, Shafi'i, Maliki, Hanafi and Hanbali, though the Shafi'is kept some privileges. Below the chief qadis served deputy judges, preachers, prayer leaders and the teachers of the many madrasas that sultans and amirs built and endowed in Cairo, Damascus and Jerusalem, and until the 15th century a scholar or qadi usually held the post of muhtasib, the inspector of Cairo's markets. The sultans funded the lodges of Sufi orders such as the Shadhiliyya, whose shaykhs and saints the common people revered. The ulama owed their posts to the sultan, who could bend them: al-Nasir Muhammad compelled the qadi to rule in his interest, and Ibn Taymiyya, the most prominent scholar of the age, was arrested several times for his teachings.
rise: Learning was open to any free Muslim man, and scholars rose by study, patronage and appointment: Ibn Khaldun, a newcomer from Tunis, served in Cairo as Maliki chief judge.
you: You teach law in a madrasa endowed by an amir and may one day sit as a judge, but your post depends on the sultan's favour.
kid: Muslim scholars called ulama worked as judges and teachers, and people looked to them for guidance.

text: The sultanate ran on paper. Its scribes (kuttab), the "men of the pen", staffed the chancery, which drafted the sultan's letters and decrees, the bureau of the army, which kept the records of the amirs and their iqtas, and the treasuries. Their craft demanded fine Arabic, accounting and a mastery of forms, and their manuals show the reach of the bureaucracy: al-Qalqashandi, a clerk of the chancery from the Nile Delta, completed his fourteen-volume manual, the Subh al-A'sha, in 1412, building on the work of the administrator al-Umari. Many officials were Coptic Christians or converts from Christianity, and their wealth and influence made them targets: popular pressure drove Copts from government service at least nine times between the late 13th and mid-15th centuries, though they were usually restored once tensions passed, and many converted to Islam, or at least professed it, to keep their posts. From the late 14th century the master of the sultan's household, an amir, became the chief financial official.
rise: Skill and patronage carried a scribe up the bureaus; for a Coptic scribe, conversion to Islam was often the price of keeping his career.
you: You draft the sultan's letters in the chancery or keep the accounts of a treasury; if you are a Copt, the next riot could cost you your post unless you convert.
kid: Scribes wrote the sultan's letters and kept the government's accounts, and many of them were Christians.

text: Merchants, shopkeepers, craftsmen and labourers filled the towns. Cairo was the greatest: by 1340 it had close to half a million people, the largest city west of China, and many lived in rented apartments in multi-storey blocks (rab') built above shops and storerooms. At the top of town society stood the great merchants, among them the Karimi, who brought spices and other goods from India through the Red Sea, for Egypt and Syria were the hinge of the trade between the Indian Ocean and Europe, and Venetian, Genoese and Catalan merchants bought in their ports. Below them came the shopkeepers and the craftsmen who made textiles, sugar, glass, soap, paper and inlaid brass, watched by the market inspector (muhtasib), and below them porters, water-carriers and beggars. Townspeople bore the taxes and exactions of needy sultans and sometimes rioted against them, and from the 1420s Sultan Barsbay's monopoly of the spice trade enriched the state at the expense of local merchants.
rise: Trade could make a fortune, and the plagues, which left fewer hands, improved the lot of the craftsmen who survived; confiscation, debt or a sultan's monopoly could ruin a merchant.

kid: Cairo was one of the biggest cities in the world, full of merchants, shopkeepers and craftspeople.

text: Most of the sultanate's people were peasants: the fellahin of the Nile valley and Delta and the villagers of Syria and Palestine. In Egypt all farming depended on the Nile flood, and the state controlled the land more closely than in Syria, where most farming was rain-fed. Peasants grew grain and the sugar cane and cotton on which Egypt's chief industries depended, and every crop was taxed. The revenues of each village went to the sultan's treasury or as iqtas to amirs and soldiers, and holders of short, scattered grants collected the taxes but often neglected the canals and dykes. Land surveys in 1298 and 1315 recorded each village's fields, their quality and their expected revenue. Bedouin raids destroyed crops and sugar works, and plague returned again and again: the Black Death of 1347–1349 killed about 40 per cent of Egypt's people, and around Asyut the taxpayers fell from 6,000 to 116. Many survivors fled to the towns and refused to return to the estates.
rise: Flight to the towns, easier once the plagues had emptied them, was the usual way off the land.
you: You farm the land along the Nile with your family and hand over a share of every crop to the amir who holds your village's revenue as his iqta, fearing the plague and the Bedouin raids.
kid: Most people were farmers, and in Egypt their fields were watered by the Nile's yearly flood.

text: Christians and Jews lived as dhimmis, protected but subordinate. Their own religious leaders governed much of their community life, but the state, often invoking the Pact of Umar, set the jizya poll tax paid by adult men, licensed their houses of worship and regulated their dress and public conduct: in Egyptian towns they might not even ride donkeys. Most Christians in Egypt were Copts, peasants and townspeople as well as scribes, and Syria had Greek Orthodox, Maronite, Syriac and Armenian Christians; Jews lived chiefly in the cities. Jews generally fared better than Christians, whom many Muslims linked with the Crusaders and the Mongols and resented for their places in the bureaucracy. Hostility came mostly from the streets rather than from the sultans, but in 1301 the government closed the churches, and in 1321 church burnings were followed by fires in Cairo. Because conversion lifted the jizya and the restrictions, a great wave of Copts turned to Islam in the 14th century.
rise: Conversion to Islam ended the jizya and the restrictions at once and saved a scribe's career; so many Copts took that road that by 1517 Muslims may have outnumbered Christians in Egypt by ten to one.
you: You are a Copt who pays the jizya, wears the dress the law prescribes and may not ride even a donkey in town, and your church could be shut after the next riot; converting to Islam would free you of all of it.
kid: Christians and Jews could keep their religions but paid a special tax and had to follow extra rules.

text: Apart from the boys bought to become mamluks, slaves in the sultanate served mostly in households, and women always outnumbered men among them. Custom kept free Muslim women from working as maids, so slave women cooked, cleaned and served in the homes of amirs, scholars and merchants, and some, trained as singers and dancers, became concubines: al-Nasir Muhammad left 1,200 slave women at his death. Slaves reached the markets from many directions: Black Africans across the Sahara and up the Nile, Greeks from Anatolia, Indians across the Red Sea, Circassians and others through the Black Sea. Dealers' handbooks ranked them by ethnic stereotype, and prices followed: in the late 14th century an Ethiopian girl cost about 300 dirhams and a Greek girl up to 550. African men were set to menial work, tending the mamluks' horses, and eunuchs guarded the harems. A slave woman who bore her master a child he acknowledged became an umm walad: the child was free, and she could no longer be sold.
rise: A slave could be freed by an owner or agree a contract to buy his freedom with his own earnings, and an umm walad was freed at her master's death. Khawand Tughay, bought as a slave by al-Nasir Muhammad, was freed and married by him.
you: You were sold as a girl in a slave market and now cook and clean in an amir's household; if your master takes you as a concubine and acknowledges your child, you can no longer be sold.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and made to work without pay, most of them as household servants.

== mobility
The Mamluk system turned the usual ladder upside down: the surest road to power began in a slave market. Baybars, a Kipchak boy captured in a Mongol invasion, was sold at the slave market of Sivas and again at Hama, came into the service of the sultan as-Salih Ayyub and in 1260 seized the throne, which he held until 1277; Barquq, a Circassian mamluk of the amir Yalbugha, became sultan in 1382. Yet the military caste was closed by birth to everyone else, the mamluks' own sons included, who rose instead through learning and office. Among civilians, scholarship and the bureaucracy were the ways up: Ibn Khaldun, an exile from Tunis, became a chief judge in Cairo, and Coptic scribes kept their careers by converting to Islam. Women could rise through the harem: Khawand Tughay, bought as a slave, was freed and married by al-Nasir Muhammad. Falls were sudden, as amirs lost their iqtas and fortunes to the next faction, and the plagues drove peasants into the towns.

== women
A woman's place followed her household's rank and whether she was free or enslaved. Under Islamic law a free woman could own and inherit property, though a daughter inherited half a son's share, and she received a dower at marriage. Many women of the ruling elite had been slaves themselves: the consorts of the Bahri sultans began as slave girls, and Shajar al-Durr, the widow of the Ayyubid sultan as-Salih Ayyub, ruled Egypt as sultan in 1250 before her marriage to the amir Aybak. Khawand Tughay, al-Nasir Muhammad's favourite wife, had been bought as a slave; at her death in 1348 she owned 1,000 slave women and 80 eunuchs and was buried in her own mausoleum. Mothers and widows of sultans could help make or secure a reign, and the Burji sultans often married free women of mamluk families. Enslaved women had no protection from their owners.
