title: Social Hierarchy of Feudal Europe: From King to Serf
desc: Feudal Europe's social ladder, c. 1000–1300: kings, great lords, bishops and abbots, knights, clergy, townspeople, free peasants, serfs and slaves.
intro: In the 1020s the bishops Adalbero of Laon and Gerard of Cambrai described Christian society as three orders: those who pray, those who fight and those who work. That picture, the period's own, ranked people by birth and function, with kings above all three. Great lords and churches held land in return for loyalty and service, and knights held smaller fiefs from them. A small landholding elite lived from the labour of the peasant majority, many of whom were serfs, legally unfree and bound to a lord's manor. Towns, which grew fast from the 11th century, fitted the scheme badly, and Jewish communities stood outside it. The tidy "feudal pyramid" of textbooks is a later simplification, and since Elizabeth Brown (1974) and Susan Reynolds (1994) historians have debated whether "feudalism" is a useful word. Customs differed between France, England and the German lands and changed greatly over three centuries.

text: Kings were anointed with holy oil at their coronation, which set them apart from every lord, and they were expected to defend the Church, keep the peace and do justice. The great lords owed them homage, but royal power varied greatly. William the Conqueror and his successors held England firmly: every landholder held land from the crown, and in 1086 the king could survey most of the kingdom in Domesday Book. The early Capetian kings of France ruled little beyond the Île-de-France around Paris until Philip II of France (1180–1223) conquered Normandy and Anjou. In the German lands the princes elected the king, who sought coronation as emperor in Rome, and the long quarrel with the popes over the appointment of bishops, settled by the Concordat of Worms in 1122, weakened his office. Kings lived from their own estates, feudal dues, justice and, increasingly, taxes, and travelled constantly with their courts. Even a strong king could be defied: in 1215 the English barons forced King John to accept Magna Carta.
rise: The crown passed by inheritance in France and England, though William the Conqueror won England by conquest in 1066. In the German lands the princes elected the king, often choosing his predecessor's son.
you: You are anointed king and the great lords owe you homage, but you must keep them loyal, do justice and defend your realm, and you spend your life on the road with your court.
kid: Kings were crowned in church and ruled with the help of great lords, who promised to be loyal to them.

text: The great lords were the dukes, counts and barons who held whole provinces or clusters of manors, often directly from the king. They owed him homage, counsel at his court and a quota of knights for his wars, and in return governed their lands much like small kingdoms: they built castles, held courts that could hang thieves, took tolls and fines, and kept households of knights and officials. Their wealth came from rents, labour services and the profits of justice. In France the dukes of Normandy and the counts of Flanders, Anjou and Toulouse ruled with little interference from the king for much of the period. In Germany a duke could defy the emperor, as Henry the Lion, duke of Saxony and Bavaria, did until Frederick Barbarossa stripped him of his duchies in 1180. In England after 1066 a small circle of Norman lords received the lands of the old English nobility, and the richest held hundreds of manors.
rise: Most great lords inherited their lands, in England and Normandy usually whole to the eldest son. Kings raised favoured servants and soldiers into their ranks, often by marriage to an heiress, and could confiscate the lands of rebels.
you: You hold castles and many manors, judge the people of your lands and lead your own knights, but you owe the king homage, counsel and soldiers, and a rebel can lose everything.
kid: Great lords such as dukes and counts lived in castles, ruled large lands and led knights for the king.

text: Bishops governed the dioceses of the Church, and abbots and abbesses the great monasteries. Most came from noble families and lived as great lords: their churches held vast estates, more than a quarter of the manors of England by value in 1086, and they kept households and courts of their own. In England and the German lands they owed knights to the king for their lands, and many German bishops and abbots ruled whole territories as princes of the Empire. Kings relied on their learning and loyalty, and many served as royal advisers, as Suger, abbot of Saint-Denis, did for Louis VI and Louis VII. Who should choose them lay at the heart of the struggle between popes and emperors that ended with the Concordat of Worms in 1122, when the emperor gave up investing bishops with ring and staff. As clergy, prelates answered to church courts rather than the king's.
rise: Bishops were elected by the clergy of their cathedrals and abbots by their monks, though kings and great lords often decided the choice. Learning and royal service could carry a man of modest birth to these offices: Thomas Becket, a London merchant's son, became Archbishop of Canterbury in 1162.
you: You rule a diocese or a great abbey with wide estates, advise the king and answer only to church courts, and like most prelates you were probably born into a noble family.
kid: Bishops and abbots led the Church and its monasteries, owned great lands and often advised kings.

text: Knights were mounted warriors in mail armour, the elite of every army. A knight usually lived from a fief, often a single manor, held from a lord in return for homage and military service, commonly forty days a year; in England the service could be paid off in money (scutage). Horse, armour and weapons were costly, so a knight lived from the labour of the peasants on his land. Over the 12th century knighthood became a rank of the lesser nobility: boys of knightly families were trained in a lord's household and dubbed knights in a ceremony, and the Church tried to restrain their violence through the Peace and Truce of God and to turn it towards the crusades. In the German lands most knights were ministeriales, legally unfree servants of the emperor, princes and bishops, who nevertheless ranked above free commoners and by the 13th century had merged with the lesser nobility.
rise: Most knights were sons of knights, though in the 11th century the word could still cover any armed horseman in a lord's service, and commoners were sometimes knighted for bravery in war. A landless younger son could make his fortune in tournaments and war, as William Marshal did.
you: You hold a manor from your lord, ride to war in mail armour when he calls, usually for forty days a year, and live from the labour of the peasants on your land.
kid: Knights were warriors who fought on horseback and were given land in return for serving their lord.

text: Below the prelates stood the great body of the clergy: parish priests, cathedral canons, clerks in minor orders, and the monks and nuns of hundreds of religious houses. A parish priest served a village church and lived from its tithe, a tenth of the parish's produce, and many priests came from peasant families. The Gregorian reforms of the late 11th century insisted that priests be celibate. Monks and nuns lived by a rule, usually that of Saint Benedict, keeping the daily round of prayer and living from their house's estates. New orders spread fast: the Cistercians from 1098, and in the 13th century the friars, Franciscans and Dominicans, who preached in the towns and lived by begging. All clergy were tried in church courts, not the king's, and since learning was almost wholly theirs, clerks also ran the writing offices of kings and lords.
rise: The Church was the career most open to talent: a boy of humble birth could become a priest, a monk or a scholar. Since clergy could not marry, the rank was the man's alone and passed to no heirs.
you: You serve a village church or live in a monastery under its rule, keeping the daily round of prayer; you may not marry, and if accused of a crime you answer to the Church's courts.
kid: Priests looked after village churches, while monks and nuns lived together in monasteries, praying and working.

text: Walled towns grew fast from the 11th century, and their people did not fit the three orders. They were merchants, craftsmen, shopkeepers, carters, servants and labourers; the full citizens, or burgesses, were the householders who shared the town's privileges. Towns bought or won charters from kings and lords that gave them markets, their own courts and the right to govern themselves, and by 1300 one French town in four was run by mayors and councils or by consuls. In English towns property was held for cash rents rather than labour services (burgage tenure). Merchants' and craftsmen's guilds regulated trades, prices and workmanship. Moralists often condemned merchants at the start of the period, but by the late 13th century trade was more readily accepted. Lords did not always accept self-governing towns: when the commune of Laon was suppressed in 1112, the townspeople rose in revolt.
rise: Many peasants moved to the towns, and in England a serf who lived for a year and a day in a chartered borough became free. The sons of rich townsmen could rise through the Church or royal service.
you: You live in a walled, chartered town, work at a trade your guild regulates, and share the townspeople's freedoms: your own market, courts and council.
kid: Townspeople were merchants and craftspeople, and many towns won the right to run their own markets and councils.

text: Jewish communities lived in the towns of France, England and the Rhineland, outside the Christian orders and under the protection of the ruler. In law they belonged to him: a 12th-century English law book declared that "the Jews themselves and all their chattels are the king's", and in 1236 Emperor Frederick II made all the Jews of Germany servants of his treasury (servi camerae). Rulers could therefore tax them at will. Kept to few occupations, many lent money at interest, which the Church forbade Christians to do; others traded, and the commentary of Rashi of Troyes (1040–1105) is printed in every edition of the Talmud. They suffered massacres during the First Crusade in 1096 and repeated violence later, and the Fourth Council of the Lateran (1215) ordered Jews to wear distinctive dress. Philip II of France expelled them from his lands in 1182 and recalled them in 1198; Edward I of England expelled all Jews from his kingdom in 1290, and Philip IV of France did the same in 1306.
rise: Standing in the community came from learning and wealth, but both rested on the ruler's protection, which a new tax, a confiscation or an expulsion could end.
you: You live in a town under the ruler's protection, perhaps lending money or trading, but he can tax you at will, and after 1215 you must wear dress that marks you out.
kid: Jewish families lived in many towns, but rulers taxed them heavily and in 1290 forced them to leave England.

text: Free peasants held land without the marks of serfdom. In England they were freeholders and sokemen, most numerous in the east, who could buy and sell their land and paid money rents instead of working on the lord's fields. Some were prosperous, others had only a few acres, and all still paid tithe and attended the lord's manor court. Lords who needed settlers to clear forests and marshes, or to farm the lands east of the Elbe opened by German settlement (the Ostsiedlung), offered them easier terms. What set free peasants apart was the law: they could leave, marry without paying the lord a fine, and in England, once the reforms of Henry II had built the common law, take a dispute with their lord to the king's courts. Free status could be lost, however, and after 1066 many English free peasants sank into dependence.
rise: Prosperous free families added to their land and sent sons to the towns or the Church; in hard times, debt or a lord's pressure could push free men into serfdom.
you: You hold your land for a money rent, pay your tithe and attend the manor court, but you are free: you may leave, sell your land or marry without paying your lord.

firm: yes
terms: villein, serfdom

text: Most peasants were dependent tenants of a lord, and many were serfs, legally unfree, whose children inherited their status. A serf held a house and strips in the open fields of a manor, and in return worked part of each week on the lord's own land, the demesne, besides paying dues in produce or coin. He could not leave the manor or give up his holding without the lord's leave, paid a fine (merchet) when his daughter married, and his heir paid a due to take over the land. In England the common law drew a sharp line between free men and villeins, whose disputes with their lords belonged to the lord's own manor court. Serfs were not slaves: they held land, had customary rights that the manor court upheld, and could own goods, and a few grew richer than their free neighbours. Villagers ran the open fields, grazing and woods together. Around 1300 perhaps half of the English peasantry had too little land to live securely.
rise: A serf could buy his freedom, be freed by his lord or run away: in England one who lived for a year and a day in a chartered town became free. From the late 13th century many lords took money in place of labour services, and serfdom faded in western Europe after the Black Death of 1348.
you: You hold a house and strips in the village fields, but you must work part of each week on your lord's land, pay a fine when your daughter marries, and may not leave without his leave.
kid: Serfs were farmers who had to work on their lord's land and could not move away without his permission.

text: At the start of the period slavery still existed. In England, Domesday Book (1086) counted about 28,000 slaves (servi), fewer than in 1066. They worked full-time for their lord, held no land, lived on what he gave them and could be bought and sold. The Church opposed the trade: William the Conqueror forbade selling slaves overseas, and in 1102 the Council of London, called by Anselm of Canterbury, condemned "selling men like animals". Slavery faded because the Church disapproved and because slaves, unlike serfs, had to be fed entirely by their owners, and by the middle of the 12th century the slaves of England had merged into the unfree peasantry. On the Continent the slaves of the great estates had become serfs between the 10th and the 12th centuries, by a process historians still debate, though Muslim captives were still sold at Marseille in the 13th century.
rise: Freedom came by manumission, which the Church called a pious act; more often, slaves were turned into unfree tenants with land of their own, and their descendants joined the serfs.
you: You belong to your lord, who can sell you; you work for him full-time, hold no land and live on what he gives you.
kid: Enslaved people were owned by others and worked without pay; in England slavery had died out by about 1150.

== mobility
Medieval writers presented the three orders as fixed, and most people died in the rank they were born into, but there was movement at every level. The Church was the widest ladder: Thomas Becket, a London merchant's son, became the king's chancellor in 1155 and Archbishop of Canterbury in 1162. War and service raised others. William Marshal, a younger son with no land, won fame in tournaments, married the heiress Isabel de Clare in 1189, became Earl of Pembroke and from 1216 governed England for the boy-king Henry III. In the German lands the ministeriales, unfree by law, rose into the lesser nobility. Towns offered serfs a way out, and lords seeking settlers for new land offered easier terms. Falls were real too: after 1066 the old English nobility lost almost all its land to the Normans, and many free English peasants sank into serfdom.

== women
A woman's standing followed her family's rank and, after marriage, her husband's. Church law required both partners to consent to a marriage, but a wife was expected to obey her husband, and in England daughters inherited land only when there were no sons. Widows had more freedom: an English widow received a third of the couple's property, and many managed farms and estates on their own. Some women ruled in their own right: Eleanor of Aquitaine became duchess of Aquitaine at about fifteen and was queen of France and then of England. Women could not be priests, but abbesses such as Hildegard of Bingen ran great houses and were writers and scholars. Peasant women worked in the fields as well as the house; when a serf's daughter married, her father paid the lord a fine (merchet), and a serf woman's children were born unfree. In towns, guilds often let a widow carry on her husband's trade.
