desc: The social ladder of French Saint-Domingue before 1789: royal officials, great planters, poorer whites, free people of colour, the enslaved and maroons.
intro: Saint-Domingue, the western third of Hispaniola, was the richest colony in the Americas: by the 1780s it produced some 40 per cent of the sugar and 60 per cent of the coffee consumed in Europe. Its wealth rested on slavery. Nearly nine people in ten were enslaved, most of them born in Africa, and colonial law and ideology ranked everyone else by colour as well as by wealth. The Code Noir of 1685 made the enslaved movable property but gave freed people the rights of the freeborn; from the 1760s colonial laws took many of those rights from free people of colour, however rich, and kept office and honour for whites. Among the whites, the great planters and merchants (grands blancs) looked down on the poorer petits blancs, and all were governed by officials sent from France. In 1791 the enslaved rose, and the order collapsed.

text: Saint-Domingue, the richest colony of the French crown, was governed for the king by two officials sent from France. The governor-general, a nobleman and soldier, commanded the regular troops, the forts and the colonial militia; the intendant ran justice, police and the royal finances. Both answered to the Secretary of State for the Navy at Versailles, who was responsible for the colonies, and the division of power often set them against each other and against the colonists. Royal laws, and the officials' own ordinances, were registered by the colony's superior councils, the high courts on which leading planters and lawyers sat. The officials enforced the exclusif, the rule that the colony should trade only with France, which the planters bitterly resented. Terms were short and the climate deadly: between 1751 and 1789 the governorship changed hands more than twenty times, counting acting governors. The colony was divided into three provinces, North, West and South.
rise: The king appointed both officials, the governor usually from the nobility and the officers of the army or navy, the intendant from the royal administration; few stayed more than a few years before recall, promotion or death.
you: You govern the king's richest colony for a few years at most, command its troops and militia, and enforce trade rules that the planters around you resent.

text: At the top of colonial society stood the grands blancs, the "big whites": the owners of the great sugar estates, the leading merchants of Cap-Français and Port-au-Prince, and the senior officials and officers. A sugar estate, with its mill and boiling house, needed great capital and hundreds of enslaved workers, and the colony had some 800 of them, while thousands of smaller coffee, indigo and cotton plantations belonged to lesser planters. Many great planters were minor nobles, and many lived in France, leaving their estates to managers and drawing the income at home. Those who stayed lived in style, served by enslaved domestics, and were often deep in debt. They sat on the superior councils and led the militia, resented the exclusif and the officials from France, and lived in fear of revolt: the writer Mirabeau said the colony's whites "slept at the foot of Vesuvius". In 1789 many planters saw the French Revolution as a chance to govern the colony themselves.
rise: Wealth made a grand blanc: a fortune in sugar, trade or office, often built by an earlier generation or gained by marrying into a planter family. Debt, epidemics or a fall in prices could ruin an estate: the collapse of coffee profits around 1770 drove many planters into debt.
you: You own a sugar estate worked by hundreds of enslaved people, which you may run from France through a manager, and you resent the trade rules made in Versailles.
kid: The richest white planters owned huge sugar farms, and many of them lived far away in France.

text: Most whites were petits blancs, "little whites": overseers and managers on the plantations, artisans, shopkeepers, clerks, sailors, soldiers, slave dealers and day labourers, along with small planters of coffee, indigo and cotton and indentured servants (engagés) bound to work for a set term. Many were young men from France who came to make a fortune and return, and many died of fevers instead; men far outnumbered women among the whites. Many owned a few enslaved people, and their claim to standing rested on colour: however poor, they stood above every person of African descent in the colony's law and custom. They resented the wealthy free people of colour, who competed with them for land, trade and marriage partners, and worked to keep them shut out of government. They also resented the grands blancs and the royal officials, and when the Revolution came they were the fiercest opponents of equal rights for free people of colour.
rise: A petit blanc could rise by managing an absentee's estate, by trade or by marriage, and buy land and enslaved workers of his own; a lucky few joined the grands blancs, while many died young of disease.
you: You came from France to make your fortune as an overseer, artisan or shopkeeper; you may own a few enslaved people, and the colony's law ranks you above every person of colour, however rich.

text: Saint-Domingue had the largest and richest free population of colour in the Caribbean. Of the roughly 28,000 people free before 1793, about 16,000 were of mixed descent and some 12,000 were Black, many of them freed slaves (affranchis), though most free people of colour were born free. Many were children of planters and enslaved or free women of colour, and some were acknowledged by their fathers, educated in France and left property. They worked as artisans, traders, livestock dealers and overseers, and many owned land: coffee planters in the hills of the West and South held enslaved workers of their own. Free men of colour served in the militia and in the rural police that hunted runaways. Article 59 of the Code Noir gave freed people the same rights as the freeborn, but from the 1760s colonial laws barred them from public office and the respected professions, forbade them to carry swords or firearms in public or to dress like whites, and kept them away from social gatherings of whites.
rise: Freedom came through manumission, which needed official permission and a tax, through purchase, or by birth to a free mother; a few families, after generations of marriage with whites, came to count as white. From the 1760s the laws closed most other doors.
you: You were born free, perhaps the child of a planter, and may own land, a trade and even enslaved workers of your own, yet the law bars you from office, the respected professions and the dress and weapons of whites.

text: The plantations kept a hierarchy of their own among the enslaved. The commandeurs, drivers chosen by the owner or manager, led the field gangs, set the pace of work and were expected to whip those who fell behind. Skilled men worked as carpenters, coopers, masons, smiths and sugar boilers; others were coachmen, grooms and herdsmen; women and men served in the great house as cooks, maids, nurses and valets. These places went mostly to people born in the colony (creoles), and they brought better food, more freedom of movement, sometimes leave to hire themselves out in town, and a better chance of being freed. They remained property, and could be sold, punished or sent to the fields at the owner's will. Their freedom of movement made some of them leaders of resistance: Jean-Jacques Dessalines rose from the cane fields to be a driver, and Dutty Boukman, a driver and later a coachman, helped lead the rising of 1791.
rise: Owners promoted trusted or skilled workers, most often creoles, and could demote them at will; such people were the enslaved most likely to be freed, as the coachman Toussaint Louverture was in the 1770s.
you: You were born in the colony and drive a field gang, boil the sugar or serve in the great house; you eat better and move more freely than the field hands, but you are still property and can be sent back to the fields.

text: The great majority of Saint-Domingue's people were enslaved field workers, and about two-thirds of them had been born in Africa. The colony depended on the slave trade: deaths outran births, and in the late 1780s ships landed some 20,000 to 30,000 captives a year. The Code Noir declared the enslaved movable property (article 44); it required masters to feed and clothe them, forbade the separate sale of a married couple and their young children (article 47) and let the enslaved complain to the royal courts, but masters broke it openly and were rarely punished. On the sugar estates gangs dug cane holes, cut cane and fed the mills under the drivers' whips, at harvest from about five in the morning until sunset; on the coffee estates they worked steep mountain slopes. Many grew food on garden plots and sold what they could spare. They built lives of their own as far as they could: families, the Creole language, and Vodou, which joined African religions with Catholic rites.
rise: Freedom came rarely, by an owner's grant, which needed official permission and a tax, or by purchase, which many owners refused; others fled. A child took its mother's status (article 13 of the Code Noir), so the children of enslaved women were born enslaved.

text: Some enslaved people escaped. Short absences (petit marronage) were common, and some owners tolerated them; others fled for good, to the towns, where they could pass among the free, or to the mountains and forests, where maroon bands farmed, hunted and raided plantations for food and weapons, and sometimes came back to free family and friends. The Code Noir punished a runaway absent for a month by cutting off the ears and branding, a second flight by cutting a hamstring and a third by death (article 38), and the rural police hunted them. Yet maroons endured. In the Bahoruco mountains on the Spanish border, communities held out for generations against French expeditions, and in 1785 the colony made peace with one of their leaders, Santiago, granting freedom to more than 100 maroons in return for sending back future runaways. In 1758 the authorities burned alive Mackandal, a one-armed maroon whom they accused of plotting to poison the planters.
rise: Flight was the only way in; capture, surrender or a treaty such as Santiago's were the ways out.
you: You have fled your owner for the mountains, where you farm, hunt and raid plantations with a maroon band; if the rural police catch you, the law prescribes mutilation, and death for a third flight.

== mobility
Before the 1760s the line between whites and free people of colour was still blurred: a planter father's favour, wealth and marriage could carry a family far, and some "white" creoles had African ancestors. From the 1760s colonial law made colour decisive. Free people of colour could still grow rich, as the saddle-maker Julien Delaunay did in becoming a planter, but no fortune opened office to them. Whites could be ruined by debt and disease. Revolution overthrew the order. In 1790 Vincent Ogé, a wealthy merchant of colour, rose to claim the rights he believed the National Assembly had granted; he was broken on the wheel in 1791. France gave rights to some free men of colour in May 1791 and to all in April 1792, and in August 1791 the enslaved of the northern plain rose. The commissioner Sonthonax abolished slavery in the north in August 1793, the National Convention in all French colonies on 4 February 1794, and after Napoleon's attempt to reconquer the colony Haiti declared its independence on 1 January 1804.

== women
Colour and status shaped women's lives as sharply as men's. White women were few, and the crown's efforts to send brides from France were given up in the 1740s. Many white men lived with enslaved or free women of colour, and some provided for them and their children with freedom, property or schooling. The Code Noir fined a free man who fathered a child with an enslaved woman, and if he was her owner the woman and child were confiscated unless he married her (article 9). Women were freed more often than men, and free women of colour such as Marie Bety and Zabeau Bellanton ran property and trade; Bellanton was among the richest free people of colour in the colony. Enslaved women worked in the fields beside men, their children were born enslaved, and rape by owners and overseers went unpunished. In August 1791 the priestess Cécile Fatiman presided with Boukman over the ceremony at Bois Caïman.
