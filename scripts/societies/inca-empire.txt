intro: Inca society ranked people by descent and by the service they owed the state. At the top stood the Sapa Inca, believed to be the son of the Sun, with his principal wife, the Coya. Around them in Cusco lived the Inca nobility, perhaps 15,000 to 40,000 people among some ten million subjects, and below them the "Incas by privilege". The curacas, hereditary lords of the conquered peoples, governed their own people in the Inca's name. Most people were commoners in kin groups called ayllus, who paid their dues in labour, since the empire had no money. The state could also resettle whole communities and bind people to lifelong service. The Incas had no writing, only knotted-cord accounts (khipu), so the evidence comes from archaeology and from chroniclers writing after the conquest: Pedro Cieza de León, Juan de Betanzos, Garcilaso de la Vega and Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala.

text: The Sapa Inca, the "unique Inca", was believed to descend from the sun god Inti and was hailed as the son of the Sun. He ruled from Cusco as an absolute and sacred monarch: he commanded the armies, appointed governors and inspectors, decided what each province owed in labour, led the festivals of the Sun and controlled the storehouses from which he rewarded service and fed the hungry. A scarlet fringe worn across the forehead, the maskaypacha, marked his office. His principal wife, the Coya, led the worship of the Moon, and by the last reigns she was often the ruler's own sister. No fixed rule settled the succession: the ruler named the son he judged most able, and the Coya and the royal lineages had a voice, so reigns often opened in conflict. A dead ruler's palaces, estates and servants stayed with the rest of his descendants, who kept his mummy and consulted it, so each new Sapa Inca had to win lands of his own.
rise: The ruler came from the royal family, normally a son of the previous Sapa Inca. When Huayna Capac died around 1527, his sons Huáscar and Atahualpa fought a civil war (1529–1532), and in November 1532 the Spaniards captured the victor, Atahualpa, at Cajamarca.
you: You are hailed as the son of the Sun, command the armies and the storehouses of the empire, and must win lands of your own, since your father's estates stay with the rest of his descendants.
kid: The Sapa Inca ruled the Inca Empire from the city of Cusco, and his people believed he was the son of the Sun.

text: The Inca nobles were the descendants of the rulers of Cusco, grouped in royal lineages (panaqa). The chroniclers name ten or eleven of them, one for each past ruler, divided between Upper and Lower Cusco (Hanan and Hurin). Each lineage kept its founder's mummy, served his shrines and held his palaces in the city and his country estates, such as Machu Picchu, probably Pachacuti's, worked by retainers who served it for life. Nobles wore fine tapestry cloth (qompi) and large ear spools, for which the Spaniards called them orejones, "big ears". Their sons were initiated at the December festival of Qhapaq Raymi and schooled at the yachaywasi, the "house of knowledge" in Cusco, in language, accounting, astronomy and war. Nobles led the armies, governed the four quarters of the empire and served as the ruler's inspectors, the highest of whom was usually his close kinsman. Rank within a lineage depended on the mother's standing as well as the father's, and the lineages competed over each succession.
rise: Rank came by descent from a ruler, through the mother as well as the father. A lineage that backed the losing side in a succession struggle could be destroyed: in 1532 Atahualpa's generals took Cusco, burned the mummy of Túpac Inca Yupanqui and killed many of his lineage, who had supported Huáscar.
you: You belong to a royal lineage of Cusco: you wear the great ear spools and fine cloth of the nobility, keep the mummy of the ruler you descend from, and may lead an army or govern a quarter of the empire.
kid: Inca nobles wore large ear ornaments, so the Spanish called them "big ears".

text: The chroniclers describe a second order of Incas: the peoples of the valleys around Cusco who were not descended from the rulers but had been granted the rank of Inca. Garcilaso de la Vega, who calls them "Incas by privilege", traces the grant back to Manco Capac, the dynasty's legendary founder, and says they were allowed to wear the headband and to pierce their ears like the Incas of the blood, though with marks of a lower degree. To the conquered peoples of the empire they were Incas all the same. With too few Incas of royal descent to govern some ten million people, the rulers used them as officials and overseers in the provinces. Their standing rested on the ruler's grant and on their loyalty, and they ranked below every Inca of royal descent. Modern historians use the chroniclers' brief accounts of this order with care, since they were written after the conquest and the line between it and the lesser Inca nobility is unclear.
rise: The rulers created the order by granting Inca status to whole peoples of the Cusco region; a person belonged to it by birth into one of those peoples.
you: You were born into one of the peoples around Cusco who were granted the rank of Inca: you may wear the Inca headband and serve as an official in the provinces, but you rank below every Inca of royal descent.
kid: Some peoples who lived near Cusco were given the rank of Inca as an honour, below the royal family.

firm: no
terms: kuraka
people:
text: The curacas were the hereditary lords of the peoples the Incas had conquered, kept in office to rule their own subjects in the Inca's name. Their authority now came from the Inca, and under the decimal scheme of administration they were graded by the number of taxpaying households they answered for, from a hundred to a thousand and more, while the greatest lords ruled whole provinces. A curaca allotted land, settled disputes and judged minor crimes, chose wives for men still unmarried at twenty-five, and saw that his people sent their quota of workers and soldiers. In return he was spared the labour tax, could take several wives, travelled in a litter and had his own fields worked by his people. Sons of the leading curacas were schooled in Cusco in the Incas' ways, and the rulers gave curacas wives from among the chosen women. A lord who resisted conquest could be executed and replaced by a son raised in Cusco.
rise: Office passed within a lord's family, usually but not always from father to son, and needed the Inca's approval; a superior could appoint a lesser curaca to his post. Rebellion meant death for the lord and, often, deportation for his people.
you: You rule your own people as your forefathers did, but in the Inca's name: you send him workers and soldiers, pay no labour tax yourself, and may take several wives and travel in a litter.
kid: Curacas were local lords who ruled their own people for the Inca and sent him workers and soldiers.

text: A large priesthood served the state religion. At its head stood the Willaq Umu, the high priest of the Sun, usually a brother of the ruler and second only to him. Under him, priests served the Sun, the Moon, the Thunder and the creator god Viracocha in Cusco, above all at the Coricancha, the gold-clad temple of the Sun, and at the state temples the Incas built in conquered provinces, while every people kept its own priests for its local shrines (wak'a), some of which, like the oracle of Pachacamac, the Incas honoured too. Priests consulted the shrines, made offerings of llamas, cloth, coca and maize beer, and conducted the festivals of the calendar, above all Inti Raymi at the June solstice. Before great ceremonies they fasted, giving up salt, chilli and meat. The harvests of the fields set aside for the Sun across the empire paid for the cult, and in Cusco the royal lineages shared the care of some 328 shrines on lines running out from the Coricancha.
rise: The high priesthood of the Sun belonged to the royal family; the keepers of local shrines came from their own communities.
you: You serve the Sun at one of the state's temples, make offerings of llamas, cloth and maize beer, and fast without salt or chilli before the great festivals.
kid: Inca priests looked after the temples of the Sun and the Moon and led the festivals of the year.

text: Each year officials toured the provinces to choose girls of about eight to ten, most of them daughters of curacas and other families of rank, for their looks, skill and intelligence. They were taken into houses of chosen women (acllawasi) in the provincial centres, each holding up to 200 women, and the most favoured were sent on to Cusco. For about four years they learned religion, spinning and weaving, cooking and the brewing of maize beer (chicha). They then wove the fine cloth the rulers gave as gifts and prepared the food and drink of the state's feasts and festivals. Some stayed for life as mamakuna, priestesses of the Sun and other gods; others were given by the ruler as wives to nobles, curacas and men who had served him well, or became secondary wives of the Sapa Inca himself. A few were chosen for the capacocha sacrifice. They had to remain chaste, on pain of death, and the families of chosen women rose in standing.
rise: Officials chose girls from the provinces, most of them from families of rank; most chosen women left the house only as wives given by the ruler, and a girl's family gained standing from her selection.
you: You were chosen as a girl and taken to a house of chosen women, where you weave fine cloth and brew maize beer for the state; you must remain chaste, and the ruler may give you as a wife to a man who has served him.
kid: The "chosen women" were girls picked to live in special houses, where they wove fine cloth and made food for festivals.

text: Most people were farmers and herders living in ayllus, kin groups that held their land in common and traced their descent from a shared ancestor. The ayllu's lord shared out its fields among the households each year according to their size, and the community also worked the plots of widows, the sick and soldiers away at war. As the empire had no money, commoners paid their dues in labour, the mit'a: married men took turns at service on the fields of the Inca and the Sun, on roads, bridges, terraces and buildings, or in the army, while households wove cloth for the state. In return the state fed and gave maize beer to those who worked for it, held feasts, and kept food in its storehouses for war and famine. Commoners wore coarse cloth and married within their rank, and a man took a single wife, while men of rank took several. Each people had to keep its own dress and headgear, so that anyone's origin could be seen.
rise: A commoner who served well in war could be rewarded with fine cloth or a wife from among the chosen women, and some became overseers of ten or fifty households, a post that was not hereditary. Rebellion could see a whole community deported as colonists.
you: You farm your share of your ayllu's land, take your turn at labour service on the state's fields, its roads or in its army, and are fed by the state while you work.
kid: Most people in the Inca Empire were farmers and herders who paid their taxes by working for the state.

text: The Incas moved whole communities from their homelands to other parts of the empire, a policy called mitma, and the settlers were known as mitmaqkuna. Loyal peoples were planted in newly conquered or restless provinces to hold them for the empire, garrison the frontiers and watch the natives; rebellious peoples were sent the other way, into regions the Incas held firmly. Other communities of specialists, such as metalworkers from the conquered kingdom of Chimor, were moved to Cusco or elsewhere to work for the state. For their first two years the settlers could draw on the state's storehouses, and the local people were made to help provide for them. They had to keep their own dress and customs, so that officials could tell them from their neighbours, but they were forbidden to return home, on pain of severe punishment. On modern estimates a quarter to a third of the empire's people may have been moved, and their movements helped spread Quechua to regions where it is still spoken.
rise: Communities were moved by order of the state: loyal ones to hold new provinces, rebellious ones as punishment, skilled ones to work for the state; their descendants stayed where they had been placed.
you: Your community was moved by order of the Inca to a province far from home, where you must keep your own dress and may never return; for the first two years you lived from the state's storehouses.
kid: The Incas moved whole villages to faraway parts of the empire to guard its borders and keep it loyal.

text: The yanakuna were people who had left their ayllus to serve a master full-time and for life: the Sapa Inca, the Coya, the royal lineages and nobles, or the temples. Some were born to the status, which passed from parents to children; some were chosen by nobles; others left their ayllus to take it up. They herded the nobles' llamas, farmed their estates, fished, made pottery, built and kept house, and they lived and served at royal estates such as Machu Picchu. Because they no longer belonged to an ayllu, they owed it no share in the community's labour turns; their work belonged to their master. Their standing varied widely. A few retainers of the ruler enjoyed high status and were appointed to office, and some owned property and farmed plots of their own, while most led humble lives in service. Whether they should be called slaves is debated: the Inca state had no money and no slave market, and yanakuna were not bought or sold.
rise: The ruler, the nobles and the temples took people from their ayllus as yanakuna, and their children inherited the status; a trusted retainer of the ruler could be raised to office.
you: You left your ayllu to serve a noble household for life, herding its llamas and farming its estates; you owe no labour turns to the community, and all your work belongs to your master.
kid: Some people served a noble family or a temple all their lives, herding llamas, farming or keeping house.

== mobility
The Inca order was steep and largely hereditary, but the state itself moved people up and down it. Descent fixed most ranks: the nobility by descent from a ruler, the curacas by their forefathers' lordship, the commoners by birth into an ayllu. Yet the rulers could raise whole groups, as when they granted Inca status to the peoples around Cusco, and individuals too: a commoner who served well in war might receive fine cloth or a wife from among the chosen women, a trusted retainer of the ruler could be given office, and when the girl Tanta Carhua of Urcon was chosen for the capacocha sacrifice, her father was made curaca of his ayllu. Falls were as real. Lords who resisted conquest were executed and replaced, rebellious peoples were deported, and in the civil war of 1529–1532 Atahualpa's generals destroyed much of the lineage of Túpac Inca Yupanqui for backing Huáscar. After 1532 many curacas went on to serve the Spanish conquerors as they had served the Incas.

== women
Andean custom saw men and women as complementary halves, and descent ran in parallel lines: women traced their kin through their mothers and could inherit land and herds from them, so women held property in their own right. The Coya led the worship of the Moon and had a voice in choosing the heir, and colonial records show women of the royal lineages owning land in the Cusco valley. Women farmed, herded, spun, brewed maize beer and wove, and cloth was valued even above gold. Girls married at about sixteen, often after a trial marriage that either partner could end; a commoner had one wife, while curacas, nobles and the ruler had several. The state took some girls as chosen women, to weave for it, to marry men it rewarded or, rarely, to die in the capacocha sacrifice. Juan de Betanzos's history of the Incas drew on the memories of his wife, Cuxirimay Ocllo, a noblewoman of Atahualpa's family.
