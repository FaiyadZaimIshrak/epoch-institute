title: Social Hierarchy of Qing China: From Emperor to Bondservants
desc: Qing China's social ladder: emperor, Manchu nobles, officials, gentry, bannermen, clergy, farmers, artisans, merchants, bondservants, mean people and slaves.
intro: By statute, Qing society was divided into relatively closed estates, five in all: the officials, a comparatively minuscule aristocracy, the degree-holding gentry and, among ordinary Chinese, the "good people" (liangmin) and the "mean people" (jianmin). Qing law counted the four traditional occupations, scholars, farmers, artisans and merchants, as good. Slaves and bondservants, entertainers, tattooed criminals and the low-level employees of officials were mean: barred from the examinations and usually from marriage with commoners. Across these lines ran a division the dynasty drew itself, "not between Manchu and Han, but between bannerman and civilian": about 1.5 million people of the hereditary Eight Banners lived on state stipends under their own courts. Over all stood the emperor, the Son of Heaven. Most people farmed, and their numbers doubled in the 18th century, from about 150 million to 300 million.

To his Mongol subjects he was the Bogda Khan, and to Tibetan Buddhists a patron of their faith. All important matters were decided in the Inner Court, dominated by the imperial family and the Manchu nobility, where the Yongzheng Emperor created the Grand Council in the 1720s. The Kangxi Emperor reigned 61 years; the Qianlong Emperor led the Ten Great Campaigns, sponsored the Siku Quanshu and used the literary inquisition to silence opposition.
rise: The throne passed among the sons of the imperial house: on the Kangxi Emperor's death in 1722 his fourth son became the Yongzheng Emperor, and in 1735 Yongzheng's son became the Qianlong Emperor. A child emperor reigned through regents, as Kangxi did from the age of seven.

In the provinces, eight viceroys, the governors and prefects stood over the county magistrate, who by rule could not serve in his home district. text: The gentry were defined by law: a man who had passed at least the first level of the examinations held a degree that qualified him for office, and his wife and some relatives shared his status. He could wear gentry robes and speak to officials as an equal; the shengyuan, or xiucai, were exempt from statute labour and partly from corporal punishment. Informally the gentry presided over local society: they influenced the magistrate, acquired land, lent money, ran schools and charities, settled disputes, helped gather the taxes and led Confucian ceremonies. Those who failed the higher examinations became tutors, secretaries or managers of guilds and temples. They travelled by sedan chair and collected scholars' stones and porcelain.

text: Every Manchu household belonged to one of the Eight Banners, which had been joined by Mongol and Han Chinese banners: in effect twenty-four armies, in companies of nominally 300 men. Membership was hereditary, and after the conquest the banners became a salaried military caste. Bannermen received stipends, land, grain rations and housing in garrison quarters, about half of them in Beijing and the rest in some eighteen provincial garrisons, and they answered to banner courts rather than the civil magistrates. All bannermen called themselves the emperor's slaves (aha, nucai) when they addressed him, a Mongol model of service. By the 1730s many neglected their duties, and the Green Standard Army, formed from surrendered Ming troops, outnumbered the banners three to one.

== mobility
The examinations were the great road up. Since the system did not formally discriminate by social status, it offered an avenue of upward mobility, and its quotas spread the prizes across the provinces in rough proportion to their people. Yet only about 5 per cent of those who sat the examinations passed, most candidates were men of some means, and preparing one son was a burden for the whole family. Failure did not cost a family its wealth or local standing: failed candidates taught, served as secretaries or managed local projects. Wealth also bought rank, as merchant families purchased degrees and, in the 19th century, offices, and gentry and merchant families mixed. Within the banners, Han Chinese bondservants' sons were adopted into Manchu households and whole groups were moved from Han to Manchu banners by order. Below, the line between good and mean people closed the examinations to the mean, and the court's efforts to narrow it never fully succeeded. Failure could also turn to revolt: Hong Xiuquan, after his fourth failure in the shengyuan examination, went on in the 19th century to lead the Taiping Rebellion.

== women
Women were excluded from the examinations, so a woman's standing came from her father and husband: the gentry included degree-holders' wives. The Kangxi Emperor's attempts to discourage Chinese women from foot binding failed, while the regent Dorgon had directed Manchu women not to bind their feet in the Han Chinese style. A decree of 1648 let Han civilian men marry Manchu women of the banners with official permission, a policy later abandoned; the family of an emperor's mother was raised into the Upper Three Manchu Banners, as the Han bannerwoman Empress Xiaoyichun's was. Empress Dowager Xiaozhuang, mother of the Shunzhi Emperor, made most decisions for her twelve-year-old son and proved a skilled political operator. Under the Qing women wrote poetry in larger numbers than before, and among the Manchus shamans were often women. At the bottom, many entertainers and prostitutes were enslaved, and poor families' daughters were sold under the guise of adoption.
