desc: Ayutthaya's social ladder under the sakdina system: the king, princes, officials, monks and Brahmins, Chinese and foreign traders, phrai commoners and slaves.
intro: Ayutthaya ranked every person by law. Under the sakdina system, which King Borommatrailokkanat fixed in 1454 and the Three Seals Law records, each rank carried a number, in name the rai of land its holder was entitled to: 5 for a slave, 20 for a commoner, 600 for a learned monk, 10,000 for a chief minister and 100,000 for the uparaja, the king's chosen successor. What the numbers measured in practice was manpower. Every free man was registered as a phrai under a lord, who called him up for labour and war, and an official's standing rested on how many phrai he commanded. A service nobility of a few thousand ran the kingdom for a king revered as semi-divine. Outside the scale, to some extent, stood the Buddhist monks and the Chinese, who owed no labour service. In the 17th century, an age of peace and trade, foreigners rose high at court, and almost every succession to the throne was fought out in the capital.

text: From the 17th century the king of Ayutthaya was an absolute monarch with a semi-religious standing. Hindu tradition cast him as an incarnation of Vishnu, Indra or Shiva; Buddhist thought made him a righteous ruler and a bodhisattva, whose duty was to build temples and Buddha images. His subjects called him Phra Chao Phaendin, "Lord of the Land", and court etiquette required a royal language, Rachasap, for speaking to him or of him. In theory he commanded all manpower and so owned all land; since Naresuan every phrai was bound to him directly. He appointed ministers and governors, sat at times as the highest judge over traitors and rebels, and held a virtual monopoly of the kingdom's income. François-Timoléon de Choisy wrote in 1685 that "the king has absolute power".
rise: No settled law fixed the succession. The king often named a preferred successor as uparaja, but any man of the royal clan could claim the throne by defeating his rivals: in the 17th century, as one study puts it, "the method of royal succession at Ayutthaya … was battle". Officials took it too: the Kalahom Prasat Thong in 1629, and Phetracha, Narai's elephant commander, in 1688.

kid: The king of Ayutthaya was called Lord of the Land, and people spoke to him in a special royal language.

text: The king's kin stood above every official. The highest of them was the uparaja, an office created by Borommatrailokkanat and usually held by the king's senior son or full brother; the Three Seals Law gave the Maha Uparat a sakdina of 100,000, ten times a chief minister's. Meant to settle the succession in a polygamous dynasty, the office bred rivalry between king and heir. The chronicles distinguish legitimate sons from the sons of concubines: Songtham, a son by a first-class concubine, became king only after both legitimate sons of Ekathotsarot had died. After Naresuan no princes governed provinces. They were kept in the capital, and their struggles for power went on there, under the king's eye.
rise: Princes were born to the rank. Thai tradition favoured brothers over sons, but nearly every succession was contested, and the losers often died: Prince Sisin, the late king's brother, was captured and executed in 1628, and King Chai was deposed and executed by his uncle in 1656.
you: You are a son of the king by one of his many wives and must live in the capital under his eye; if you are named uparaja you may reign one day, should you outlast your brothers and uncles.
kid: The king's sons and brothers lived at court, and one of them was chosen to be the next king.

text: A service nobility of a few thousand men governed the kingdom. Each held a title and a sakdina, up to 10,000 for a chief minister, and his allotment answered to the number of phrai he commanded. Two prime ministers headed the court: the Samuha Nayok for civil affairs, over the four ministries, and the Samuha Kalahom over the armed forces. The king appointed the governors of the cities, each watched by an inspector, the yokkrabat. Officials were rewarded with rice fields, not salaries. As mun nai, lords, they raised their own phrai for war and royal works, which made them powerful in politics. Ministries and governorships, with their sakdina, usually passed within a few families, often tied to the king by marriage.
rise: Offices were mostly inherited, but the king could raise new men, and foreigners often rose high: the Greek adventurer Constantine Phaulkon became Narai's prime minister. A fall could be fatal. Purges of officials followed conflicts with the king, and Phaulkon was executed in 1688.

text: Theravada Buddhism was the kingdom's main religion, and its monastic community, the sangha, stood partly outside the sakdina order, for men of every class could join it. The Three Seals Law gave a learned monk a sakdina of 600, thirty times a commoner's. Building temples was one of a king's chief duties: Songtham raised a great temple at Wat Phra Phutthabat, and Borommakot, celebrated as a pious king, sent two Siamese monks to reform Buddhism in Sri Lanka in 1753. The court Brahmins conducted the coronation and the rites of divine kingship, for many elements of the political order came from Hindu scripture. The Buddhist clergy resented the favour Narai showed to French interests and the Catholic missionaries.
rise: Any man could enter the sangha, and many phrai did so to escape their lords' control. A monk could also leave it: in 1760 the people of the capital urged Uthumphon, a former king living as a monk, to lead its defence against the Burmese.
you: You have joined the sangha, free of the labour service your lord once called you to; the rice the peasants have left over after their own needs and their taxes goes to support temples like yours.

text: Ayutthaya was a great port, and its foreign communities had a place of their own. The Chinese were not obliged to register for labour service and could move about the kingdom and trade at will; by the 16th century they controlled its internal trade and held important posts in civil and military service. The Japanese quarter, Ban Yipun, under a chief the Thai authorities appointed, held traders, Christians fleeing persecution and former samurai. Persian, Malay, Cham, Makassar, Dutch and Portuguese communities lived and traded there too. The kings hired foreign mercenaries, and foreigners, lacking ties within the kingdom, often rose high at court: Yamada Nagamasa's Japanese served as King Songtham's royal guard.
rise: Foreigners rose with a king's favour and fell with it: Prasat Thong had Yamada Nagamasa assassinated in 1630 and banished the Japanese, and a Makassar revolt failed in 1686. The Chinese grew with the rice trade to China in the late 17th and 18th centuries.

text: Every free man was registered as a phrai under a lord, a system Ramathibodi II (r. 1491–1529) made general. A phrai owed labour for half the months of the year, on public works such as canals and on the lands of his official, and in war the men were conscripted; the phrai suay paid in taxes instead. Phrai luang served the king, worked crown lands and fought in his army, phrai som the officials. Most lived in villages of extended families, often along the canals near the cities, where the headman held the land for the community and families kept its use as long as they farmed it. They grew rice to eat and to pay taxes, and made textiles for sale abroad. Their sakdina was 20.
rise: A phrai could become a monk, or sell himself as a slave to another lord, who compensated his old one. Many simply escaped: they fled into the forests or bribed conscription officers, and as trade grew more of them slipped away to farm on their own.

text: Slaves, that (from the Pali dasa), formed the lowest rank, with a sakdina of 5. By law a master "had absolute power over their slaves other than the right to take their lives". People became slaves as war captives, through debt or by birth to slave parents, and a phrai could sell himself to a lord. Ayutthaya's victorious campaigns brought conquered people into the kingdom, where they were put into its labour force. Masters set their slaves to work on their lands and gardens and in their households, or let them earn their own living in return for a yearly payment: Simon de la Loubère, who visited in 1687, put it at four to eight ticals.
rise: Slavery came by capture, debt, sale or birth. It was abolished in stages from 1874 to 1905, under King Chulalongkorn, and legally ended only in 1915.

== mobility
Rank in Ayutthaya was fixed by law but not sealed by birth. Commoners moved by choice as well as misfortune: a phrai could join the sangha, sell himself into slavery or flee to the forest, and the laws tightening control of labour were issued again and again, a sign of how hard the elite found it to hold its people. Foreigners climbed furthest: Constantine Phaulkon became Narai's prime minister, and Yamada Nagamasa became ruler of Nakhon Si Thammarat. Officials could reach the throne itself, as Prasat Thong did in 1629 and Phetracha in 1688. In the kingdom's last 150 years, trade made some commoners rich: late records name a new category, the phrai mangmi, rich "serfs", and nobles took titles and privileges once kept for royalty. The same weakening of labour control left the kingdom short of soldiers when the Burmese invaded in 1759–1760 and 1765–1767.

== women
The sources say little of women outside the court. Kings married to bind powerful families to the throne, and their wives usually numbered in the dozens; the queen wore a chada headdress and the sabai, a breast cloth over one shoulder, while concubines wore their hair long. The chronicles set legitimate sons apart from the sons of concubines, and royal consorts could be drawn into deadly politics: in the 18th century the uparaja Thammathibet was executed for an affair with two of his father's consorts. Only men were conscripted, and the sangha was a community of men. Village women wore the sabai and pha nung, as noblewomen did. In Lavo, before the kingdom was founded, a Chinese account noted that women held considerable authority over economic and travel decisions, and the rabam that La Loubère saw in 1687 was danced by men and women together.
