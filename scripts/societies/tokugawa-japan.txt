title: Social Hierarchy of Tokugawa Japan: From Emperor to Outcasts
desc: Japan's social ladder under the Tokugawa shoguns: emperor and court, shogun, daimyo, samurai, clergy, peasants, artisans, merchants and outcasts.
intro: Tokugawa Japan ranked people by hereditary status, fixed in law and recorded each year in registers built on the certificates of Buddhist temples. Official ideology, drawn from Confucianism, named four classes in order of moral worth: samurai, peasants, artisans and merchants (shi-nō-kō-shō). Historians since the 1990s stress that the real legal line ran between the samurai and everyone else, and that peasants, artisans and merchants were separate status groups rather than ranks one above another. Above the samurai stood the daimyo, the shogun who ruled from Edo, and, highest in honour but without power, the emperor and his court in Kyoto. Outside the four classes stood the clergy and, at the bottom, the outcast kawata and hinin. Status decided where people lived, what they wore, whether they carried swords and whom they married. Samurai families made up about 6 per cent of the people; four in five were peasants.

text: Highest in honour stood the emperor in Kyoto. He was the official ruler of Japan and the source of court ranks and titles, the shogun's included, but he had virtually no power. The shogunate's Laws for the Imperial and Court Officials of 1615 told him to devote himself to scholarship and poetry, a shogunal deputy, the Kyoto shoshidai, oversaw the court, and the emperor lived secluded in his palace on revenues granted by the shogunate. Around him lived the court nobles (kuge), about 130 families of the higher dōjō rank, among the most honoured families in Japan. Each house could rise only to the court offices its rank allowed, and the regency was reserved for five houses of the Fujiwara line. With little income of their own, the kuge earned fees by licensing pupils in the arts of the court, such as waka poetry and the biwa lute; their pupils included daimyo and rich commoners. In the 1860s, as the shogunate weakened, the court regained political weight.
rise: Birth decided everything: the throne passed within the imperial family, and court offices within the kuge houses by the rank of each house. The court went on granting ranks to the daimyo, but only on the shogunate's recommendation.

text: The shogun was the real ruler of Japan. In law he was the emperor's general, appointed by the court to govern in the emperor's name, and the emperor outranked him in honour, though the shogun held the highest or near-highest court ranks, above most court nobles. In fact he was a military ruler who governed from Edo through his own government, the bakufu. The office was hereditary in the Tokugawa house: fifteen shoguns, all descended from Tokugawa Ieyasu, held it from 1603 to 1867, and when the main line failed, the related houses of Owari, Kii and Mito could supply an heir. The shogun controlled foreign relations, coinage and the great roads, confirmed every daimyo in his domain, could move or abolish domains, and made the daimyo spend alternate years in Edo and leave their families there. By the end of the period his own lands yielded about 7 million of the country's 30 million koku, and about 22,000 retainers served him directly.
rise: The office passed within the Tokugawa house, to an heir from a branch house when the main line failed, as in 1716. Ieyasu meant to close the road by which Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the son of peasants, had risen to rule Japan a generation earlier.
you: You rule Japan from Edo in the emperor's name: you confirm or remove the daimyo, keep their families in your capital and decide on foreign affairs and coinage, though the emperor in Kyoto still outranks you in honour.
kid: The shogun was Japan's real ruler, a general from the Tokugawa family who governed from the city of Edo.

text: Below the shogun stood the daimyo, the lords of about 260 to 270 domains (han), each assessed at 10,000 koku of rice or more; the greatest, the Maeda of Kaga, held about a million. The shogunate graded them by loyalty: the shinpan were Tokugawa kin, the fudai were old vassals of the house who staffed the shogun's government, and the tozama, the "outside" lords, had submitted only after the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and were kept out of office. Within his domain a daimyo made his own laws, collected his own taxes and kept his own samurai, but he was allowed only one castle, and the Laws for the Military Houses (buke shohatto) of 1615 forbade new fortifications or marriages without the shogun's permission. Under alternate attendance (sankin-kōtai) he spent every other year in Edo, where his wife and children lived permanently, in effect as hostages. The cost of the journeys and the Edo mansions drove many daimyo into debt to merchants.
rise: A daimyo's son or adopted heir succeeded him with the shogun's approval. The shogun could make a loyal retainer a daimyo by granting him more land, or reduce, move or abolish a domain, as he often did in the early decades of the period.
you: You rule a domain of at least 10,000 koku with your own samurai and laws, but every other year you must go to Edo, where your wife and children live as the shogun's hostages.
kid: Daimyo were great lords who ruled parts of Japan for the shogun and had to live in his city every other year.

text: The samurai were the hereditary warrior class, perhaps 400,000 men. They alone could carry the long sword, and from 1629 those on duty wore two swords, the long and the short; they bore surnames and, in law, could cut down a commoner who failed to show them respect, though how often they did so is unknown. From the late 16th century most were moved off the land into their lord's castle town and paid hereditary stipends in rice, and in the long peace they became the officials, clerks, police, guards and teachers of the shogun and the domains. The class was finely graded: the shogun's bannermen (hatamoto) could approach him in audience, the highest retainers of a great domain drew more than some daimyo, and the lowest ranks earned little more than subsistence. Forbidden to farm or trade, many fell into debt as prices rose and their stipends did not. On one estimate almost all samurai could read by 1800.
rise: Status passed from father to son, or to an adopted son, and a talented man could rise only a few steps above his birth. From the 18th century rich commoners bought their way in by paying a poor retainer of the shogun to adopt a son; a samurai who lost his lord became a masterless rōnin.
you: You wear two swords and draw a rice stipend from your lord, and you serve him as an official or guard in his castle town; you may not farm or trade, and your stipend may not cover your debts.
kid: Samurai were warriors who wore two swords, and in the long Edo peace most of them worked as officials and guards.

text: Buddhist monks and priests and the priests of Shinto shrines stood outside the four classes. The shogunate made the Buddhist temples an arm of the state: after the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638, every household had to belong to a temple, which certified each year that its members were not Christians, and the registers built on these certificates became, in effect, a register of the whole population. No one, not even a samurai or a Shinto priest, could marry, travel or take some kinds of work without a temple's certificate, and people refused one could be recorded as hinin, outcasts, or treated as Christians. Temples drew a steady income from fees, funerals and memorial services, and from 1729 a family could no longer leave its temple for another. The magistrates of temples and shrines (jisha-bugyō), the highest-ranking of the shogun's three commissioners, supervised them, and many temples and shrines held landed estates.
rise: Men of any class could take the robe. The samurai Yamamoto Tsunetomo became a monk in 1700, when his lord died and retainers were forbidden to follow their lord in death.
you: You serve a Buddhist temple to which every household of the neighbourhood must belong: you register its families each year, certify that none of them is a Christian, and live on the fees for funerals and memorial services.
kid: Buddhist priests looked after temples and kept a record of every family that belonged to them.

people: ninomiya-sontoku
text: Four people in five were peasants (hyakushō), the villagers who grew the rice on which the whole order rested. Confucian theory honoured them as producers, second only to the samurai, but they bore most of the taxes: a domain's land tax took around 40 to 50 per cent of the harvest, and every household of the village had to cooperate to meet it. Most land was farmed by families on their own holdings, under village officials who kept the peace and collected the tax, and wealthier families had more say in village matters. Peasants could not carry the long sword, and journeys or pilgrimages beyond the village needed a permit. Rice paddies almost doubled between 1600 and 1720, and some families grew rich through commercial crops, money-lending and rural manufacturing, while others lost their land to debt. When taxes or famine grew unbearable, villages petitioned and rose, as in the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638; historians count about 3,000 outbreaks of peasant protest and violence between 1590 and 1871.
rise: Peasant status passed with the household, though young people sometimes went to work outside the village for a season. A rich peasant family could buy a samurai adoption for a son, and some peasants' sons won samurai status by serving in the office of a shogunal intendant (daikan); debt and famine pushed others into the towns' poor or even among the hinin.
you: You farm rice with your family, hand over close to half the harvest in tax, and need a permit to travel beyond your village.
kid: Most people in Edo Japan were farmers who grew rice and paid a large share of it to their lord.

text: In the four-class scheme the artisans (shokunin) came third, after the peasants, as makers of useful things. Most lived in the castle towns and cities, in quarters set aside for them around the castle, and together with the merchants they formed the townspeople (chōnin). The daimyo and their samurai produced nothing themselves, and artisans supplied them and the growing towns with cookware, clothing, children's toys, books and writing materials, tools for hunting and fishing, and ornaments for the home. Crafts were organised in guilds, and by 1800 as much as a tenth of the people may have lived in large towns, one of the highest shares in the world at the time. Townspeople created the woodblock prints, comic storytelling (rakugo) and handicrafts of a new urban culture. Recent historians stress that the law did not rank artisans below peasants: the real legal line ran between samurai and commoners, and in the towns the line between artisan and merchant often blurred.
rise: Artisans belonged to the townspeople, whose wealthier members could buy a samurai adoption for a son from the 18th century. Movement also ran the other way: a low-ranking samurai could step down into a town trade.
you: You live in the craftsmen's quarter of a castle town and make goods for samurai households and townspeople; in law you rank with the peasants and merchants, below every samurai.
kid: Artisans in Edo Japan made things like cooking pots, clothes, toys and books, mostly in the towns.

terms: chonin, kabuki
people:
text: Merchants stood last in the Confucian scheme, regarded as parasites who lived off others' work, yet they became the richest commoners in Japan, and many were richer than the samurai who borrowed from them. Osaka, "the kitchen of Japan", held the national rice market, where daimyo sold much of their tax rice, and Edo and Kyoto were great centres of trade. Brokers turned domain rice into cash, merchants devised credit instruments, rice was sold forward before the harvest, and great houses lent to daimyo and samurai, who often never repaid them and could not be made to by law. Commerce went largely untaxed, but the shogunate and the domains forced merchants to make "loans", and the shogun's laws forbade them to display their wealth as samurai did. Merchant patrons sustained the theatre, prints and pleasure quarters of the "floating world" (ukiyo). Like artisans, merchants were not legally ranked below peasants: they were townspeople (chōnin), and the legal line ran between samurai and commoners.
rise: Wealth could not buy rank directly, but it opened other doors. A merchant's daughter who married into a samurai family brought it money and her own family standing, a rich merchant could buy a samurai adoption for a son, and merchants who served as a domain's financial agents received rice stipends and some samurai privileges.
you: You trade rice or lend money in Osaka or Edo and may be richer than the samurai who borrow from you, but the law forbids you to show your wealth and gives you no way to make a lord repay.
kid: Merchants were ranked low in theory, but many became very rich by trading rice and lending money.

people:
text: The kawata, called eta in official documents (a term now a slur), were hereditary outcasts. Their status came from work that Shinto and Buddhist ideas of pollution (kegare) tied to death and blood: they skinned dead animals, tanned hides and made leather goods and drums, and the authorities also used them as jailers, executioners and police. The Tokugawa order made the status hereditary: kawata were entered in separate registers and lived in segregated villages or quarters, and the Kyōhō reforms of the early 18th century codified rules on their clothing, hairstyle and movement; some authorities forbade them to enter towns at night. Yet many also farmed like other villagers, they held a monopoly of their trades, and some leaders grew wealthy; in Edo their hereditary chief, Danzaemon, had authority over the outcasts under him. Their communities grew in the 18th and 19th centuries, partly as poor commoners fell into them.
rise: There was almost no way out: kawata status was inherited and permanent, and the rules of the 18th century made it harder still to leave. Records show that some nevertheless left their communities by flight.
you: You belong to a segregated kawata community: you tan hides or work the land, may be called on to guard prisoners, and must keep to rules on dress and movement that mark you as an outcast.
kid: Kawata families did jobs such as leatherwork that others thought unclean, and they were made to live apart.

text: The hinin, literally "non-people", were outcasts of another kind. Many lived by begging and street performance, and they also cleaned the streets, guarded and worked in the prisons, buried the bodies of executed criminals, collected waste paper and cared for the sick. Unlike that of the kawata, hinin status was not always for life. Some were born into it, but others were commoners who had fallen into it through destitution or as a punishment under the shogunate's law code of 1742: survivors of a failed lovers' suicide, for example, were made hinin. People whom a temple refused a certificate could also be recorded as hinin. They lived in quarters of their own under their own chiefs, could not wear robes below the knee or hats, and in Edo came under the authority of the kawata chief Danzaemon. A hinin who had fallen from commoner status could be restored to it if relatives paid for the release.
rise: A hinin born to the status rarely left it, but one who had fallen into it could be restored to commoner status if the conditions were met, and the Kyōhō reforms made even this much harder.
you: You live in the hinin quarter, beg or perform in the streets and are called on to clean the town or guard prisoners; if you fell to this status as a commoner, your relatives may still buy you back.
kid: Hinin were very poor outcasts who begged, performed in the streets and did jobs such as cleaning the towns.

== mobility
The Tokugawa order was built to stop people moving. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, a peasant's son, had risen to rule Japan, and his Sword Hunt of 1588 and Separation Edict of 1591 began to fix warriors and farmers apart; the Tokugawa made status hereditary, and within the samurai class a talented man could rise only a few steps above his birth. Yet the boundaries leaked. From the 18th century rich merchants and peasants bought samurai status for a son by paying a poor retainer of the shogun to adopt him, about 1,000 ryō for a yoriki officer's post and 500 for a kachi foot soldier's; some peasants' sons won samurai status by serving in a shogunal intendant's office; and Ninomiya Sontoku rose from a ruined farming family to restore villages for the shogunate. Movement ran downwards too: masterless samurai became rōnin, low-ranking samurai turned to town trades, as the writer Takizawa Bakin did, and destitute or convicted commoners fell among the hinin. The Meiji government abolished the four classes after 1868, and the outcast statuses in 1871.

== women
A woman's life depended on her family's status. Samurai wives were expected to obey the head of the house, took no part in politics and rarely headed a household, though a widow could become its senior member; the Greater Learning for Women (Onna daigaku), attributed to Kaibara Ekken, told a wife to obey her husband in everything. Yet by the end of the period nearly all samurai women could read. Peasant women did the housework early in the morning and then worked in the fields, and townswomen often worked in the family business. In law a husband could end a marriage by letter, while a wife who wanted to leave could take refuge in a convent, as at Kamakura; among commoners divorce was common. In Edo Castle's women's quarters (the Ōoku), Kasuga no Tsubone, wet nurse of the shogun Iemitsu, wielded influence likened to a senior councillor's, and Otama, a grocer's daughter, became mother of the fifth shogun, Tokugawa Tsunayoshi.
