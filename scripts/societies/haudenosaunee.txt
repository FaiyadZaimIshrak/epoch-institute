intro: The Haudenosaunee, "the people of the longhouse", ranked people less by wealth or birth into a class than by clan and lineage, age, sex and deeds. Descent ran through the mother: children belonged to her clan and took their standing from hers. The clans' elder women, the clan mothers, chose and could depose the fifty chiefs of the League's Grand Council, and decisions needed consensus. A British colonial administrator wrote in 1749 that they allowed "no Kind of Superiority of one over another". Yet the same society took captives in its "mourning wars" to replace its dead: some were adopted, often into lesser places, others were tortured and killed, and a few were kept as slaves. The Haudenosaunee live today in the United States and Canada, on eleven reservations and seven reserves, and continue to consider themselves sovereign; what is known of the past comes from their oral tradition, archaeology and the accounts of Jesuits and other Europeans, who came from a patriarchal society and often overlooked the power of women.

text: The clan mothers, the elder women of each clan, chose its chiefs. When a chief's title fell vacant, the most senior woman of the hereditary lineage selected his successor in consultation with other women, and the chief's sister was historically responsible for nominating the successor. A chief who proved unsound, became corrupt or did not listen to the people could be removed by the council of the clan's women elders, a process called "dehorning": the deer antlers of his office were taken from his headgear. The women held councils of their own, sent runners or an orator to the men's councils, often proposed legislation and could veto treaties and declarations of war.
rise: The clan mothers were the elder women of their clans, held in high regard as those who had raised children; leadership passed within the clan's female line.

text: The League's Grand Council was an assembly of fifty chiefs (royaner), each representing a clan of a nation. Their offices were hereditary within the clan: when one fell vacant, a candidate was selected from the clan's members and "raised up" by a council of all chiefs in the Condolence Ceremony, and the new chief gave up his own name for the title. The Onondaga chief who held the title Tadodaho chaired the council. Unanimity in public acts was essential, so the council worked by consensus; it ruled on disputes, but the nations still acted on their own and formed their own war bands.
rise: Clan mothers chose a chief from his clan and could remove him, when he gave up the title and took his old name again. In 1687 the French seized fifty chiefs under a flag of truce and shipped them to Marseille as galley slaves; 13 came back in 1689.
you: Your clan's women chose you, and you gave up your own name for a chief's title; you sit in council seeking agreement with the other chiefs, and if you fail your people the clan mother can take your antlers away.
kid: Fifty chiefs met in the Grand Council of the League, and they had to agree before acting together.

text: Below the League chiefs stood two kinds of leader who could not vote in council: the pine tree chiefs, who served as diplomats, and the war chiefs, who led the war parties. The Great Law of Peace, as later written down, names one war chief for each nation, to carry messages for the chiefs, take up arms in an emergency, watch the council's proceedings and bring the complaints of the people and the warnings of the women to a chief who erred. In the 18th century such men led their nations in the colonial wars: the old Mohawk war chief Peter Hendricks was killed at Lake George in 1755, and Joseph Brant raised forces for the Crown in the Revolution.
rise: No birthright made a war chief: valour in war was a prerequisite for any man to be made a chief, and patronage counted, as Molly Brant's did when her brother Joseph became a war chief.

text: Faithkeepers are part-time specialists who conduct the religious ceremonies. Both men and women are appointed by the elders, and some personal names are reserved for faithkeepers, as for chiefs. The ceremonies follow the farming year, from the Maple and Planting festivals to the Strawberry, Green Corn and Harvest festivals and Midwinter, the New Year; in the 17th century Europeans counted seventeen festivals. Male and female healers knew the use of herbs, dressed wounds, set broken bones and performed surgery, and fortune-tellers sought the spiritual causes of illness. The medicine societies, brotherhoods entered by formal initiation, held curing rites: in the best known, the False Face Society, masked dancers sought to frighten away the spirits believed to cause illness.
rise: Faithkeepers were appointed by the elders and took names reserved for their office; the medicine societies admitted members by initiation, at first, it seems, by moiety.

text: War was a young man's road to standing. It showed valour and courage, a prerequisite for becoming a chief and essential for a man who wanted to marry. A war party succeeded when it took many prisoners without losses of its own: killing was accepted only when necessary, and captives counted for far more than scalps. Warriors preferred ambushes and surprise attacks, almost never attacked a fortified place and retreated when outnumbered. When a family lost a member, the clan mothers could demand a mourning war, and men who refused were marked as cowards forever and could not marry. Guns bought from the Dutch and the English made war far deadlier in the Beaver Wars of 1609–1701.
rise: Every man could become a warrior; renown in war opened the way to a chief's title, while cowardice, branded by the clan mothers, left a man unmarriageable.
you: You go out with a small war party to bring back captives for a grieving family; a captive is worth more than a scalp, and losing one of your own would undo the raid.
kid: Young men went to war to prove their courage, often to bring back captives rather than to kill.

text: Most people lived in palisaded villages that Europeans called castles, one to four for each nation at a time, moved every five to twenty years as soil and firewood ran out. Between two and twenty families shared a longhouse of elm bark, with fires down the middle. Clans of about 50 to 200 people were grouped into two halves (moieties), and marrying within one's own clan was incest. Farming was women's work: they planted, harvested and cooked the corn, beans and squash called the Three Sisters, and gathered with the children, while men hunted deer and went to war. No one owned land; women were held to be its stewards. Some became Christians, among them the Mohawk-Algonquin Kateri Tekakwitha.
rise: One was born into one's mother's clan. Outsiders entered by adoption, and at marriage a young couple lived in the longhouse of the wife's family.
you: You belong to your mother's clan and live in its longhouse with other families; the women plant and harvest the Three Sisters, the men hunt and go to war, and no one owns the land.
kid: Families lived in long bark houses; women grew corn, beans and squash, and children belonged to their mother's clan.

text: The mourning wars brought captives into the villages to replace the dead. All captives were stripped, tied to poles and burned, and some had fingernails pulled out; then the families decided for each whether to adopt or kill. Women and children were adopted more often than older men. Adoptees were renamed and expected to reject their former lives; those who made a sincere effort were embraced and could take the place of the person they replaced, though many were adopted to fill lesser places and did chores for their families. Those who did not were swiftly executed, and runaways were punished: the recaptured French teenager Pierre-Esprit Radisson had his fingernails pulled out.
rise: Capture and adoption brought people here; an adoptee who lived up to the dead person's place could rise with it, and in time many became full members, while one who resisted or fled could be killed.

text: Not every captive was adopted. A few never were and only performed tasks in the village, perhaps to be exchanged. Jacques Bruyas's Mohawk dictionary defines gatsennen as "domestic animal, servant, slave", and Oneida and Onondaga words likewise compared slaves to dogs. Captives slated for execution wore red and black face paint, were addressed as "uncle" or "nephew" by a family that fed them, and were killed after a day of torture; the body was then eaten by the community, a practice modern anthropologists seem to accept as probable, which ended, with the ritual torture, in the early 18th century. Most European captives were ransomed or killed, and later Indigenous captives were traded to European settlers, some ending in Quebec households.
rise: War, ransom or a family's choice put a captive here; adoption was the way out, and a captive who resisted could be killed. Once European settlers brought African slavery to North America, some Haudenosaunee, such as Joseph Brant, owned enslaved Africans.
you: You were taken in a raid and not adopted: you work at the village's tasks, may be traded away, and the word for you is the word for a domestic animal.

== mobility
Standing among the Haudenosaunee came from clan and lineage, age, sex and deeds rather than from wealth. Chiefs' titles were hereditary within clans, but the clan mothers chose who held them and could take them away, and only valour in war made a man a chief. Adoption moved whole peoples into the League: during the Beaver Wars the Haudenosaunee defeated and assimilated most of the Wendat (1649), Petun (1650), the Neutral Nation (1651), the Erie (1657) and the Susquehannock (1680), and in 1722 the Tuscarora, sponsored by the Oneida, were accepted as a sixth nation. New wealth brought new distinctions in the 18th century: after about 150 Palatine families leased land from the Haudenosaunee by 1713, the Haudenosaunee elite gave up longhouses for European-style houses, on an income equal to a middle-class English family's. Many Catholic converts moved to the mission villages of Kahnawake and Kanesatake near Montreal. The Revolution broke the League apart, and after the Sullivan Expedition of 1779 destroyed 40 to 60 towns, more than 5,000 Haudenosaunee fled to British Canada.

== women
Women held real power. Kinship was matrilineal: children belonged to their mother's clan, and her brothers were important teachers of her children, introducing boys to men's roles. Women held the dwellings and farmed land; a woman's property before marriage stayed hers, and the work of her hands was hers to use as she saw fit. At marriage a couple lived in the longhouse of the wife's family, and a woman could divorce an unsatisfactory husband by asking him to leave with his possessions; if a couple separated, the woman kept the children. Agoianders, women named for their good qualities, served social, political and diplomatic functions. Canadian Haudenosaunee clan mothers met in council with men on war and peace and joined delegations to the French in Montreal, and in 1711 Palatine refugees asked the clan mothers for leave to settle on their land. Tradition names Jigonhsasee, the Mother of Nations, among the founders of the League, but early researchers consulted only male informants, so the women's oral tradition was largely lost to them.
