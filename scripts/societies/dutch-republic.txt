intro: In the 17th-century Dutch Republic social status was largely determined by income. The landed nobility, living mostly in the inland provinces, counted for relatively little; the urban merchant class dominated, and from its richest families came the regents, the closed circle of men who sat, usually for life, on the town councils and through them in the States of the provinces, which held sovereignty. The stadtholder, usually the Prince of Orange, commanded the army but was by law an official of those States. The Reformed Church was the public church, and only those who conformed to it could hold office, so Catholics, Anabaptists and Jews worshipped apart. Below the affluent middle class came farmers, craftsmen and shopkeepers, then labourers, servants and sailors, and at the bottom the paupers. The Republic's wealth also came from the trade and conquests of the Dutch East India Company and the Dutch West India Company: in 1621 Jan Pieterszoon Coen had almost all the inhabitants of the Banda Islands massacred, and in the Atlantic slave trade Dutch ships carried more than 550,000 people to the Americas in slavery.

text: The stadtholder, the "steward", had once stood in for an absent lord. After the provinces renounced Philip II in 1581, sovereignty and the highest executive and legislative power passed to the States of each province, and the stadtholder became their highest executive official, appointed by each province's States. The office was open in name to anyone, but most provinces chose the Prince of Orange, so that one man held most of the stadtholderships and acted as head of state. By law he was a mere official. He was Captain-General of the States Army and Admiral-General of the fleet, appointed army officers and sometimes chose burgomasters from the town councils' lists. Maurice of Orange ruled after 1618 as a military dictator.

The regents could also leave it empty: after William II died in 1650 Holland had no stadtholder for 22 years, until the invasion of 1672, the "Disaster Year", swept William III to power.

text: The regents (regenten) were the rulers of the Republic, the men who governed its cities and through them the provinces: not formally a hereditary class, but in practice patricians, from rich merchant families long formed into a closed group. Each city's council, the vroedschap, filled its own vacancies, its members usually sitting for life; it nominated the burgomasters and schepenen and sent the city's deputies to the States. Amsterdam was governed by a large but closed oligarchy, which spent heavily on waterways, almshouses, hospitals and churches. The elite wore black as a mark of status. Merchant capital, much of it brought by refugees from Antwerp, went into ventures such as the Dutch East India Company, founded in 1602.
rise: Only men with enough wealth and long residence in the city could join, and an ambitious merchant family's first step was a marriage into an old regent family. Everyone who held public office had to conform to the Reformed Church, and upheavals could purge a whole council: the Remonstrant regents in 1618, the Orangists in 1650, the followers of Johan de Witt in 1672.

Most nobles lived in the less developed inland provinces, and in the provincial States they sat as the ridderschappen, the bodies of nobles that represented the countryside; after the revolt their Catholic members were replaced by supporters of the new religion. Nobles mixed with the other classes for financial reasons: they married their daughters to wealthy merchants, became traders themselves or took public or military office, and their sons, like rich merchants' sons, made a Grand Tour of Europe's universities with a tutor. text: Calvinism was the state religion, and the Dutch Reformed Church the "public" or "privileged" church: anyone who wished to hold public office had to conform to it and take an oath. The Union of Utrecht let Holland and Zeeland accept one religion only, and Catholic services were soon forbidden in every province. The clergy had little worldly influence, yet in many places the church exercised more social control than the Catholic Church had. Early in the century the church split over predestination, and the Synod of Dort (1618–19) condemned the Remonstrants, who denied it, and banned their faith. Reformed churches, built from 1595, had no statues inside. Ministers ranked with the lawyers and physicians of the middle class.

== mobility
In the Dutch Republic status was largely determined by income. Wealthy merchants bought themselves into the nobility by becoming landowners and acquiring a coat of arms and a seal, while nobles married their daughters to merchants, became traders or took office; merchants in turn came to value public office, and universities became career pathways to it. The top of the ladder was harder to reach. Only men with enough wealth and long residence could join a city's regents, a marriage into an old regent family was the usual first step, and the councils filled their own seats for life. Upheavals could replace a whole regent elite: Maurice's coup of 1618, the death of William II in 1650 and the Disaster Year of 1672 each brought new men in. Later in the century William III encouraged the regents to promise one another's families the offices, in "contracts of correspondence", which closed the oligarchy further in the 18th century, when the regents became rentiers living on the public debt. Lower down, the Republic lived on newcomers: nearly half of Amsterdam's people were first-generation immigrants, and real wages for unskilled labourers rose by 62 per cent in forty years. Religion set a limit no income removed: Catholics, Anabaptists and Jews could not hold public office.

== women
The home was a woman's sphere and an integral part of Dutch public life, a microcosm of the Republic whose good order reflected the state's. The husband held authority in the public realm and controlled a small front room, the voorhuis, where he did his work or business; the wife ran the kitchens and family rooms, supervised the maids, cooked, cleaned, sewed and spun, went to market and kept some financial control over the household, and in some cases a wife held considerable control in the family business. Calvinist writers such as Jacob Cats stressed equality and companionship between husband and wife, though women were still held to be the weaker sex. Married women could publicly shame husbands who used brothels and could refuse their husbands if there was reason to fear venereal disease; women took communion alongside men, and widows could inherit property and keep control of their finances. Burgher girls were taught household duties and reading by their mothers, and travellers wrote of the freedom young women enjoyed in courtship. Mothers were urged to breastfeed rather than use a wet nurse. Noble families married their daughters to wealthy merchants, joining the two elites.
