title: Social Hierarchy of the Classic Maya: From Holy Lord to Captive
desc: The Classic Maya social ladder: holy lords and overlords, nobles, scribes and priests, craftsmen and traders, farmers, and war captives.
intro: The Classic Maya lived in dozens of rival kingdoms, each ruled from its own city by a holy lord (k'uhul ajaw), while the strongest kings claimed overlordship of the rest. Birth divided society sharply between a hereditary nobility, which held the titles, offices, scribal learning and priesthoods, and the commoners, on modern estimates over nine-tenths of the people, who farmed, built and fought. The evidence is thin and uneven. The hieroglyphic inscriptions speak almost only of kings, queens and lords, and the titles they record are not fully understood. Archaeology of houses and burials, as at Copán and the buried village of Joya de Cerén, shows how ordinary people lived. Spanish accounts of the 16th-century Maya fill some gaps, but they describe a later society and must be used with caution, so the lower ranks are known only in outline.

text: Each kingdom was ruled by a holy lord, the k'uhul ajaw, who stood between his people and the gods. He was identified with the young maize god, served as the highest priest, offered his own blood in ritual and dedicated the temples, and as war leader he was expected to take captives himself. The throne normally passed from father to eldest son. The heir, the b'aah ch'ok or "head youth", had to prove himself in war, and at his accession sat on a jaguar-skin cushion and received a jade headband and a sceptre of the god K'awiil. Kings recorded their births, accessions, wars and rituals on stone stelae dated in the Long Count calendar, and were buried in pyramid temples such as the Temple of the Inscriptions at Palenque. The most powerful took the title kaloomte' and ruled other kings as vassals: in the 7th century Yuknoom Ch'een II of Calakmul controlled the kings of Dos Pilas, Cancuén and Naranjo. Defeat could mean capture and a sacrificial death.
rise: Kings were born to their thrones, but war could unmake them: in 648 Yuknoom Ch'een II of Calakmul captured B'ajlaj Chan K'awiil of Dos Pilas and restored him as his vassal, and in 738 Copán's king Waxaklajuun Ub'aah K'awiil was captured and beheaded by his own vassal, the ruler of Quiriguá.

text: Below the kings stood a hereditary nobility of royal kin and great families. In the Early Classic ajaw, "lord", was the title of a ruler; by the Late Classic many nobles bore it, and a large city could have several ajaw, each over a district. A sajal, "feared one", was a lesser lord who governed a second- or third-rank town for a higher lord and often led its warriors, and the inscriptions name sajals as the holders of war captives. Young nobles were called ch'ok, "youths". Court offices were in the gift of higher lords, who were said to "own" the officials they sponsored. Nobles lived in stone houses near the city centre: one at Copán took an estimated 10,686 days of labour to build, against 67 for a commoner's hut. They wore jade and fine cotton, some had jade set into their teeth, and they did most of the fighting. By the Late Classic the nobility had grown large and shared power the kings had once held alone.
rise: Noble rank came by birth into a noble lineage. Within it a man rose from office to office under the sponsorship of a higher lord and by taking captives in war, while a lord captured in battle could be sacrificed.
you: You were born into a noble lineage: you live in a stone house near the city centre, wear jade, fight in your lord's wars and hope that a powerful patron will give you an office at court.
kid: Maya nobles lived in stone houses near the centre of their city, wore jade and served at the king's court.

text: Writing and ritual knowledge belonged to the nobility, and commoners could not read. Scribes, called aj tz'ib, "he of the writing", came from noble families, often the royal family itself, and were probably trained in scribal schools. They painted books of bark paper, all lost but for four later ones, and composed the texts of the stelae and of the painted pottery of the court, and some signed their work: eight sculptors signed one stela at Piedras Negras. A palace at Copán belonged to a noble lineage of scribes, and King Jasaw Chan K'awiil I of Tikal was buried with his paint pot. Courtiers also bore the title aj k'uhuun, often translated "keeper of the holy books". The priesthood was likewise a closed group drawn from the elite: priests kept the calendar and the astronomical tables, recorded ritual knowledge, history and myth, and led public ceremonies of feasting, bloodletting, incense, music and dance, and at times human sacrifice. In the Classic period the king himself was the highest priest.
rise: Scribal and priestly learning passed within noble families; a scribe's skill could win him honour at court, and some signed their names to their work.
you: You were born to a noble family and trained to write: you paint books on bark paper, compose the texts carved on your king's stelae and help keep the calendar of the rituals.
kid: Maya scribes came from noble families and painted books and wrote the words carved on stone monuments.

text: Specialists made the goods on which the courts, and the trade between kingdoms, depended. The finest work was done for the court: carving stelae and jade, painting murals and the polychrome vessels used at royal feasts, and knapping eccentric flints in the shapes of people, animals and sacred signs, a feat of great skill. Some of these artists were nobles, and a few signed their work. Others made goods for wider use: potters, stoneworkers, flint and obsidian knappers, and weavers of the cotton cloth the Maya traded across Mesoamerica. Traders carried cacao, cloth, salt, obsidian and jade overland on their backs, since the Maya had no pack animals, or by canoe along the rivers and coasts, and cacao beans served as money. Marketplaces are hard to find, but soil studies suggest that some cities had them by the Early Classic. Archaeologists think that by the Late Classic artisans, merchants, lower officials and soldiers may have formed a middle stratum between the nobles and the farmers.
rise: Skill could bring a craftsman to the court's notice, and enterprising commoners could become influential; on 16th-century evidence, long-distance trade belonged to the nobles, while most traders worked locally.
you: You work at a craft such as pottery or stone tools, or carry cacao and cotton cloth on your back along the trade routes, where cacao beans serve as money.
kid: Maya craftspeople carved stone and jade and painted fine pots, and traders used cacao beans as money.

text: The great majority were commoners: farmers, labourers and servants, and also craftsmen and a few minor officials. They grew maize, beans and squash in fields cleared from the forest, on terraces and raised fields and in house gardens with fruit trees and chillies, and some grew cotton and cacao. Their houses of poles, wattle and thatch, sometimes on low platforms, have mostly vanished. The village of Joya de Cerén, buried by volcanic ash around 600, preserves such houses: about 200 people lived there, in households with kitchens, storehouses and workshops, a shared sweat bath, and buildings for festivals and divination, and each household kept some seventy pots. Commoners buried their dead beneath their house floors with offerings, paid taxes to the nobles in maize, flour and game, built their lords' temples and palaces, and were called up in war, where they fought with the blowguns they used for hunting. A lakam, or standard-bearer, perhaps the only non-noble office at court, collected the taxes of districts in the largest cities.
rise: Birth fixed most lives, but commoners could advance by proving themselves as outstanding warriors, and skilled and enterprising commoners could become influential. Drought, war and the collapse of the 9th century drove many from their land.
you: You farm maize, beans and squash near your house of poles and thatch, bury your dead beneath its floor, pay taxes to the nobles in maize and game, and go to war when you are called.
kid: Most Maya were farmers who grew maize, beans and squash and lived in houses with thatched roofs.

text: Warfare filled the lowest rank with captives. Raids aimed above all at seizing prisoners and plunder, and a warrior's captives were his trophies: the inscriptions name them as the captives of their takers, and monuments show kings standing over bound prisoners. High-ranking captives, above all captured kings and lords, were sacrificed, often by beheading, to dedicate a building or mark a king's accession, sometimes after being kept, humiliated and tortured; captives of lower rank were generally put to work. Whether the Classic Maya also kept slaves in the full sense is debated. Spanish accounts of the 16th-century Maya describe slaves and a busy slave trade with the rest of Mesoamerica, and some scholars see the captives put to labour in Classic times as slaves, but the inscriptions say almost nothing about captives who were not sacrificed. A captive's fate could also turn: in 648 the king of Calakmul captured the ruler of Dos Pilas and then restored him to his throne as a vassal.
rise: Most of the captives the inscriptions name were sacrificed; a few lords were released as vassals, and the fate of lesser captives is little known.
you: You were taken in a raid and now belong to the warrior who captured you; if you are of high rank you may be kept for sacrifice, and if not, you will be put to work.
kid: Prisoners taken in war were made to work for their captors, and some were sacrificed to the gods.

== mobility
Classic Maya society was ruled by birth: kingship passed within royal lines, offices within noble families, and commoners rarely crossed into the nobility. Within these limits there was movement. Commoners could rise by proving themselves as outstanding warriors, and nobles climbed from office to office under the sponsorship of higher lords. Royal marriages moved women between dynasties and could refound a kingdom, as when Lady Six Sky came from Dos Pilas to Naranjo in 682. War moved whole courts up and down: in 738 Waxaklajuun Ub'aah K'awiil of Copán was captured and beheaded by his own vassal, the ruler of Quiriguá, while Calakmul installed and controlled the kings of its allies. In the Late Classic the nobility grew and took a larger share of power, and from the 9th century the system fell apart. Kings stopped raising dated monuments, the last at Toniná in 909, cities emptied within a few generations, and squatters built thatched huts in the plazas and royal palaces of Tikal.

== women
Women took their rank from their families, and royal women were central to dynastic politics. Princesses married into other kingdoms to seal alliances: Lady K'abel of Calakmul, wife of the king of Waka', bore the title kaloomte', above her husband, and Lady K'ab'al Xook of Yaxchilan is shown on carved lintels drawing blood from her tongue. Royal power passed to a woman only when a dynasty would otherwise have ended, but some did rule: Yohl Ik'nal held the full title of holy lord at Palenque from 583 to 604, Sak K'uk' was regent there for her son K'inich Janaab Pakal, who took the throne at twelve in 615, and Lady Six Sky ruled Naranjo for her young son and was shown standing over captives like a warrior king. Female scribes appear in Maya art. Commoner women are known mainly from archaeology: they ground maize and wove cloth, and at Joya de Cerén a woman's ritual kit suggests a female diviner.
